Show Your Working

Chapter one. Untold.

Learn the lines as you learned your prayers, by heart and in order. A Measurer does not ask why a line is true. A Measurer finds the line that is needed and says it as it is written. These lines have held this city up for four hundred years. Your opinion has held up nothing.

R. Cordwin, Master of Tables. Preface to the apprentice's edition of the Tables

The ninth pier

The capstone of the ninth pier had sunk on its rotten bed over the winter, and now there was a gap between the stone and the beam above it, wide enough to slide a hand into. Every cart that crossed came down on that gap with a knock Quenby could hear from her bed. The brass plate on the bridgehead post said MAINTAINED BY THE HALL OF MEASURE, with a chisel line struck through it by the last man the Hall had sent.

She pushed the wedge into the gap thin end first. It went in as far as her knuckles and rattled.

"Three coppers a sack," said Rell Saddow from the bank, "and the Weir gangs for anyone stood next to you, and I've come down in the dark for a doorstop."

"Bring the sack."

"Bring it yourself. I don't touch a telling. I sell dust, and what people do with dust is their business and the Hall's, and the Hall can choke on it."

Her two sacks held shed, the grey dust that fell off stone when the Hall's Measurers told it small, swept up at the Wick quarry where the bridge stone had been cut. Quenby dragged one out along the footing and propped it against the pier with its neck open, so that the dust spilled against the wedge.

Her cord had a small knot for every finger and a fat double knot at every span. She held its end to the tip of the wedge and ran her thumb down the knots. Double knot, one, two.

One span and two. Up to three times.

She put her palm flat on the stone. A Measurer would have touched a lead weight to it and given every word its own room, and she had neither. "One span and two fingers," she said, "along the long line. One to three."

The heap of dust shivered and a grey thread lifted off it and ran into the side of the wedge, and the thick end of the wedge began to swell, and the thin end did not. Its top face rose into a blister, and the blister split with the crack of a knuckle in a cold joint, and a chip of grey spat past her ear into the river. When it stopped, the thing in the gap was fat at one end and starved at the other and torn open down the middle, pale and raw inside, a stone told two sizes at once.

The sack had slumped by a third.

"And that," said Rell, "is a copper of my dust gone into a turd nobody will buy."

Quenby dug the torn thing out with her knife and dropped it in the river. Then she unhooked Rell's lantern without asking and held it to the cord on her knee: double knot, one, two, three. She had lost a finger in the dark.

A finger in thirteen. It wants better than a finger in a hundred.

Up the street, four lamps still burned.

She always brought two wedges, because she was usually wrong. The second was cut from the same block as the first, with a thread of white quartz across one face, and she measured it knot by knot under the lantern. One span and three, the last knot square on the tip.

"That's the second sack you're opening," Rell said. "Three more coppers. I'm not a charity, whatever your face thinks."

Quenby took the coins out of the toe of her boot. They were the fee for the Hall's examination, which she had told her father she would never sit again. She put the lot in Rell's palm, and Rell counted it with her thumb.

She set the second sack against the first and the wedge in the gap, and put her hand on it. "One span and three fingers, along the long line. One to three."

This time the stone drank. The dust rose off both sacks in two smooth grey ropes and poured into the side of the wedge, and the wedge grew all of itself at once, thin end and thick end together, every edge pushing out by the same share and the slope keeping its slope. It was the same stone, the same chipped tip and white thread, only larger, as if she were leaning closer to it. It shouldered into the gap. Above her the beam groaned and lifted by a hair, and frost dropped off the planks into her collar.

When both sacks lay flat she ran the cord along the wedge: three spans and nine fingers.

Three times thirteen. True.

She was folded under the beam with the empty sacks against her chest when her father's boots came onto the planks overhead. At the bridgehead he stopped, and she heard him rap the post with the brass cap.

"Still here," Rudd Marl told the bridge, and put out the last lamp, and went home to his breakfast.

The cutting

The slab came down on the east cutting at first bell, and by the time the measure crew reached it, the man underneath had stopped screaming and started to bargain.

"Somebody see to my mule," Piran the carter was telling the quarrymen. "She isn't hurt, she's sulking, and she bites when she sulks. Am I dying? If I'm dying somebody should say."

The slab had come off the face in a single sheet a hand thick, and it lay tilted across the track with its high end propped on the wreck of his cart and its low end across his left hip and leg. The mule stood in the traces with half a cart hanging off her, tearing at a thistle.

Measurer Pascoe Dunn came down the track at a careful half-run with one palm over the lead weight on its cord at his chest. His apprentice Roan came behind under the leather case that held the four volumes of the Tables. Quenby came last with two baskets of feed stone, river ice still melting out of her hems.

"Slant block," Pascoe said. "Roan. Volume two." He held his thumb up against the slab and squinted along it, as the Hall taught, and named its size off the thumb.

Quenby had her cord out already. The short edge agreed with him. The long edge came up a whole double knot short of his thumb, whichever end she started from.

Roan found the page and read out the line in his examination voice.

"It's short," Quenby said. "The long side. I've had the cord on it."

"Cord stretches in the frost, Miss Marl."

"Not that much."

"It is a common slant block, and the Tables have its line. The Tables were written before your grandmother was born, Miss Marl, and they have not been wrong in my lifetime. Baskets, if you please."

She had sat the Hall's examination until her father stopped asking how it went, and every time worked out the lines she could not remember, slowly, in the margin, while the board waited. The last examiner had written across the top of her paper in letters she could read from the door: no measure in her. She picked up the baskets and held them where his left hand could reach.

Pascoe pressed his right palm to the slab. The lead weight swung down and touched the stone, and he said Roan's line back to it word for word and told the slab small.

It was the wedge again, the size of a cart. The stone hissed and threw off shed in a pour, and the short edge rushed in toward Pascoe while the long edge hardly moved, and with nowhere else to go the middle rucked up into a ridge, and the ridge split with a crack she felt in her back teeth. The near corner lifted clear of the ground. The middle of the slab dropped onto the carter, and he screamed.

Round Pascoe's right hand the stone had run soft. It rose over his knuckles in a slow grey lip and closed, and was rock again, with his hand inside it to the wrist and the cord of his weight trapped in with it, so that his head was dragged down and his cheek lay flat on the slab.

Up first

Pascoe braced a boot on the stone and hauled. The cord cut a white line under his jaw, and he breathed out through his nose in short high whistles.

Roan knelt by the case with his lips moving. "When a telling shears, the Measurer shall withdraw from the work, send for a senior Measurer, and touch nothing. When a telling shears..."

"Where's the nearest senior?"

"It doesn't say nearest. It says senior. Top of the gorge. It says touch nothing. It's underlined."

The top of the gorge was an hour up and an hour down. Quenby lay on her belly in the frost to look under the slab. The carter's lips were the colour of the shed, and he was taking little sips of air that stopped halfway down.

"Get it off," the carter whispered, "and you can have the mule."

She was frightened, and her hands were already running the cord. The slab was no shape in any of the four volumes now, but it had broken along one side as straight as a cut, and she laid the cord along that edge and pinched the knot at the corner.

She put her palm on the stone to take it down, and her knuckle came up against Pascoe's wrist where it went into the rock. The stone had shut round him too close to slide a fingernail into the join.

"Roan. When you tell a stone small, what happens to a hole in it?"

Roan answered without lifting his head, in the reciting voice. "All that is in the stone is told with the stone, the hollow as the solid. Volume one, the first page. Everyone knows that."

Pascoe heard it. He stopped hauling and looked at his own wrist.

"Then it goes up," Quenby said.

"Up costs," said Roan. "Up eats."

She tipped both baskets out against the slab, and it was nowhere near enough. She kicked the cart's spilled load across, rubble off the same face, until the heap was up to her shins, and knelt in it, and put her palm flat on the slab beside Pascoe's wrist.

Pascoe turned one eye to her along the stone. "Miss Marl. You hold no licence and no line. I shall be bound to say what I saw, to the Bench, to their faces. Take your hand from the stone."

She told the broken edge to the stone, low and quick, the way she said things under the Wickbridge, and told it a little larger.

The heap went first. It slumped under her knees and ran, rubble sliding into the side of the slab grain over grain with a long dry hiss, until she knelt on bare frost. The slab grew, every edge at once by the same small share, and settled heavier by every stone she had kicked into it, and its low end bore down.

Something in the carter's hip gave with a deep wet pop. His free heel dug a trench through the frost into the black mud. He had bitten through his lip, and the blood ran sideways across his cheek into his ear.

"Please," he said, with no air behind it. "Lady. Please, please."

"Not yet," said Quenby, and kept her hand on the stone.

Pascoe's wrist turned in the stone. Then his whole hand turned, loose in the socket that had held it, and he tore it out and fell back with the weight swinging on its cord.

She laid the cord on the broken edge again, one-handed, and the edge ran on past the knot she had pinched before. She told it to the stone, and this time she told it small.

The stone breathed out, and went on breathing out. Shed came off it in a grey flood that rolled over the carter's chest and her arms, and the slab came down to meet her hand, every edge drawing in together, every corner keeping its corner, until there was nothing under her palm but a lump of quarry stone she could have closed her fist on.

Two quarrymen dragged the carter clear. He cursed them in a flat steady voice the whole way, and his left foot came after him pointing the wrong way.

Rell Saddow's barrow came bumping down the track, and she looked at the drifts of shed with open hunger.

"A copper a sack for this," she told the quarrymen. "The Hall gives me less and calls it charity, the thieving bastards." When the shovel came near Quenby's knees she said, without lifting her head, "Didn't see a thing. Either time."

Pascoe sat against the cutting wall with his right hand in his lap like something that belonged to another man.

Roan was sitting on the case. "There's no line for a torn slab," he said. "I looked while you were doing it. What did you say it from?"

"The cord."

"The cord isn't a line." He frowned at the fist of stone in the drifts. "Have you done it before?"

"No," said Quenby.

Upstairs

Two Proctors in grey coats walked her up the gorge with a hand each in the back of her coat. From a landing on the steps she could see down to the Wickbridge on its row of piers, and she found the ninth without trying.

They left her on a bench in a corridor until dark. The windows faced north, onto the Sill: a black wall across the head of the gorge, four hundred years of stone with the Mere behind it. At dusk a Weir gang came down the road below, roped together at the waist, grey with silt to the roots of their hair.

A clerk pasted a notice by the stairs.

BY ORDER OF THE HALL OF MEASURE. At the thaw bell on the fourth day from this, the Sill will be told up by half again, two to three, to hold the waters of the thaw. The Raising has been proved forty times. Keep off the Sill road.

Half again. Four days.

Another clerk took her up to a room with a fire, where a man stood when she came in.

Magister Rafe Cordwin had written the preface about prayers. She had recited it at every one of her examinations. He poured her a cup of hot barley water himself and moved the honey where she could reach it.

"I have had Measurer Dunn's account," he said. "Drink that, Miss Marl. You're frozen."

"How is he?"

"Measurer Dunn will keep the hand. He will not use it again, I'm afraid. The carter will walk with a stick, in time. He asked us to see to his mule. Measurer Dunn's account is clear. Your baskets were short. The telling drew on them, found too little stone, and tore. Then you panicked and did a thing no woman who carries stone should know how to do, and you were lucky."

All afternoon on the bench she had been sure Pascoe would tell it straight. He was proud and slow and frightened of the Bench, but he had never once lied to her, and he had heard her say short in front of the whole cutting.

She hated him. It came up her throat, hot and plain, and she sat with it.

"He was telling it down," she said. "Down doesn't take stone. Down throws dust."

"I know," Cordwin said, and smiled at her kindly.

He slid a sheet across the desk with a space at the foot for her name. "This says your baskets were short and you acted in fear, by chance. Sign it, and Measurer Dunn keeps his licence and his pension, and what happened becomes a frightened woman's luck, which is no crime. If you do not sign it, then you told a torn slab without a line, and the Bench will wish to hear how, and then who taught you. You saw the men on the road this evening."

"Nobody taught me."

"That," said Cordwin, "would be worse."

He held out his hand, and she took the slate out of her coat and gave it to him.

He read the front first, aloud. "Ninth. One span and two, struck out. One span and three. One to three. The ninth what, Miss Marl?"

"Sum. I do them at night."

He turned the slate over. The back was covered from edge to edge, and it was the Sill. The wall's height and weight, the rock it stood on and what that rock would carry before it cracked, worked once as the wall stood now and once at two to three. She had rubbed it out and worked it again every night for a month, because it came out the same, and every time she decided the mistake was hers. The last line said cracks at the foot.

Cordwin read it with one finger under the lines. The finger went back up a line and came down again, slower.

"And what does this say?"

She had been telling her father it said nothing, across the supper table, for a month, while he trimmed his wicks.

"It cracks," she said.

"The wall."

"The rock under it. Then the wall goes, and the Mere comes down the gorge."

"The Raising has been proved forty times."

"On little models, in a yard." She wanted him to stop looking at her like that, as if for the first time that evening he could see her.

"Has anyone else seen this?"

"No."

"Good," said Cordwin. He licked his thumb and rubbed the Sill off the back of her slate, line by line, until it was black and wet, and handed it back to her with the ninth still on the front.

"Sign it by first bell, Miss Marl," he said, "or at second bell my Proctors go down to the Wickbridge with a lamp, and ask your father to hold it for them while they look under the ninth pier."
