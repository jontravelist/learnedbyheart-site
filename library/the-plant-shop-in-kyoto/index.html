The Plant Shop in Kyoto

Chapter one. To Be Collected.

Rain

The rain came sideways off the hills that evening. Sayo Mizuhara stood under the eaves of her great-aunt's shop with her suitcase against her shins, and she was crying a little. She had not cried in November, and she was angry with herself for starting now, in front of a closed door.

The shop was a narrow wooden house of two storeys, between a tea shop and a house with a blue tarpaulin tied over half its roof. The indigo curtain was out over the door. The name on it, Tsuyukusa, was in her great-aunt's brushwork, and the rain had got into the dye so that the last character ran down into the hem. A card taped to the glass said the shop was closed and that enquiries should go next door.

Ferns and a kumquat stood on the steps, with a bowl of narcissus that the rain had pushed flat. Somebody had put a brick on a sack of compost to keep the top from blowing open.

Next door, a man in a dark apron was carrying a signboard in out of the wet. He stopped and looked at the suitcase.

"Tsuji-san's family?" he said.

"Good evening. I'm Sayo Mizuhara, her great-niece. I'm sorry to trouble you at this hour."

"You rang your bicycle bell outside my door at six in the morning," said Mr. Hirose.

"I'm sorry."

He set the board down inside his own door and took a key from his apron pocket. He held it out on his flat palm, so that she had to pick it up from his hand.

"I watered. I sold nothing. The garbage goes out on Tuesday and Friday."

"Thank you for looking after everything. I'm very grateful. May I ask why the curtain is still out?"

"She telephoned from the hospital and told me to leave it. So people see it and knock on my door and ask me if she has died. The lamp is for the cat."

He went in and slid his door shut. Then it opened again by the width of a hand.

"You have grown taller," he said, and shut it.

Sayo unlocked the shop and slid the door open. The brass bell on its spring gave one note. Inside it smelled of wet earth and kerosene, and of something sour, which was a bucket of rainwater left standing by the ferns. A lamp with a paper shade was lit on the counter. In its light a large grey cat lay across the open ledger.

"You're Shachō," she said. "Hello."

The cat got up, walked off the ledger and went upstairs.

She knew him from Aunt Kiyo's New Year cards, which were mostly about the cat. The president has put on weight. Sayo had not answered the last two.

At the back of the shop, three long shelves ran the width of the wall, and every inch of them was plants. Each pot had half a paper ticket tied to it with string. They belonged to people who had gone into hospital, or abroad, or to live with a son in another city, and Aunt Kiyo kept them free until the owner came back with the other half of the ticket. That was the keeping shelf, and Sayo had been allowed to water the bottom of it when she was seven, with Aunt Kiyo standing over her.

Most tickets had a name and a telephone number, and one had a dog drawn on the back in marker.

The pot at the far end was glazed blue and chipped at the rim. It held a camellia with a thick grey trunk, dark leaves and no buds at all. Somebody had wiped every leaf by hand, and on one of them there was still a dry smear where the cloth had missed.

Its ticket had gone brown, and the string was much newer than the paper. The writing was Aunt Kiyo's, rounder than it was now. There was no name and no telephone number, only two initials, S.T., over the words To be collected, and a year, 1986.

Sayo was thirty-four. The ticket was older than she was.

Upstairs, among stacks of seed catalogues, she lay under bedding that smelled of mothballs and opened her bank account on her phone, which she did most nights, and looked at the number, which was the same. Later the cat came up the stairs, considered her for some time, and lay down against the backs of her knees, heavy and warm. She slept until it was light.

Visiting hours

By morning the rain had gone. There was snow on the hills to the north, thin enough for the trees to show through it. Sayo rode Aunt Kiyo's bicycle to the hospital with a box of strawberries in the basket.

Aunt Kiyo was in a room of four beds on the third floor, by the window. She had pinned her own hair. A walking frame stood by the bed, and a plastic basket on the bedside cabinet held her own cup and her own chopsticks. She had become smaller, and the first thing she said was, "Visiting begins at one."

"I thought it was two."

"It is one. Not that chair. That is hers." She meant the woman in the next bed, who was asleep with her mouth open. "Sit on the end, then. Be careful of my foot."

Sayo sat down and held out the strawberries.

"I can't eat those. They give me rice porridge three times a day. I have all my own teeth. I showed the doctor." Aunt Kiyo took the box anyway and put it in the cabinet without looking at it. "Somebody has been using my television card. There was eight hundred yen on it on Sunday."

"I'm sure nobody..."

"You weren't here."

Sayo asked about the hip. Aunt Kiyo told her about the pin, and the ice on the step outside the tofu shop, which the tofu maker should have salted. Then she told her about the pin again, in nearly the same words, and in the middle of it she stopped talking and put her hand out for the call button and pressed it, and pressed it again.

Nobody came, though a buzzer was going in another room down the corridor. Aunt Kiyo said, "Pull the curtain," and Sayo pulled it around the bed on its rail, and then her aunt was trying to get her legs over the side on her own, holding the frame, with her mouth shut tight.

"Let me. Aunt Kiyo, let me help."

The commode was a chair with a lid, beside the bed. Sayo got an arm under her. Aunt Kiyo weighed almost nothing, and she was shaking, and the back of her nightgown had come open over a long dressing on her hip, yellow at the edges.

"Turn around," Aunt Kiyo said. "Turn around. Why are you standing there? Turn around."

Sayo turned around and looked at the curtain, which had a pattern of small grey leaves, and she heard everything.

The nurse came after it was over. She said, "Very good, Tsuji-san, very good, we did it," and got Aunt Kiyo back into bed and took the commode away.

For a while neither of them spoke. Sayo sat on the end of the bed with her coat on.

"Hirose-san has his own shop," Aunt Kiyo said at last. "He cannot keep watering mine."

"No."

"I was going to have to decide about it this year. Even before I fell." She moved her hands on the blanket. "You are not working at the moment."

"I'm between things."

"Then you can stay there. For now. Your mother will want you back in Osaka, and she will call me and cry. Tell her I said so."

"Said what?"

Aunt Kiyo did not explain. "The camellia at the back. Wipe its leaves in the morning, before you open. With a damp cloth. You will forget."

"It has no buds on it, Aunt Kiyo."

"It is a camellia. Camellias flower in March."

"Has it ever flowered?"

"When it flowers, we will see about the shop. Whether it can stand by itself, or whether it is time." She did not say time for what. "And do not move it. Hirose-san moved it once to sweep, and I did not speak to him until summer."

"Whose is it? There are only initials."

"A customer's. The man from the nursery used to bring the stock down in a little white van with no door on the passenger side. He tied the door on with rope. He drove it until it fell apart. I don't know what happened to that van."

Then she reached into the cabinet, took out the strawberries and put the box on the blanket. "Eat those. I don't want to look at them."

Sayo ate one, and then another.

"You still eat the green part," Aunt Kiyo said. "You did that when you were six. Your mother used to pick them out of your mouth." She watched her eat a third, and for a moment she looked the way Sayo remembered her at the shop counter, when a customer had chosen well.

"I'm no good with money," Sayo said. "You know I'm not."

"Your grandfather was no good with money, and he had a car." Aunt Kiyo turned her head on the pillow. "And the café?"

"Sold."

"What?"

"Sold," Sayo said, louder. "In the autumn. A couple from Kobe made an offer for the site, and it was the right time to move on. Everything has worked out for the best. Your two million yen is safe. There is some paperwork with the bank, and then I will bring it to you."

It had not been sold. It had closed in November, full every day and owing money, and nobody had wanted the site. She said the bank was slow.

Aunt Kiyo said, "Hm."

She did not ask about the paperwork. She asked whether Hirose-san was giving the cat canned food or dry food, because he gave him the canned food, and she had told him not to. Then she said she was tired, and Sayo should go before the traffic.

Sayo had been afraid of the question for three months. On the stairs going down she found that she minded very much that it hadn't been asked.

She rode home slowly, trying to put together what she had agreed to. She was to stay. There was the camellia, and March, and something about whether it was time. At the lights by the river she thought, clearly, in words, that Aunt Kiyo was eighty-two and that the two million yen would not be owed forever. She got off the bicycle and stood with it at the side of the road while the lights changed, and changed back.

Iris Lane

She opened at ten the next morning. The tofu maker opposite had salted his step. By a quarter past ten the whole of Iris Lane knew that Tsuyukusa was open.

The first customer was a woman in her seventies in a good wool coat, pulling a shopping bag on wheels. She pointed at the nearest shelf before she was through the curtain.

"How much is the fern?"

"Eighteen hundred yen."

"And the small one next to it? Does Kiyo-san still get them from the man up in the hills? Is he still alive? No, it was the bonsai man who died. How much is the small one?"

"Nine hundred."

"And the two together?"

Sayo said two thousand seven hundred.

"Two thousand four hundred," said Mrs. Mori.

"All right."

Mrs. Mori looked at her, and then counted the coins out onto the counter one at a time, slowly, as a kind of punishment. "Kiyo-san would have argued with me. That is why I come. You should have said two thousand five hundred. Why didn't you say two thousand five hundred?"

Sayo did not have an answer. She put the coins away and thought, in that coat, three hundred yen, and was ashamed of thinking it, and thought it again.

Mrs. Mori was still turning her bag toward the door when another woman came in past her. She was about fifty, with hair clips fastened in a row along the pocket of her apron, and she was talking before the bell had stopped.

"You're Sayo-san. I'm Tomoe, I have the hair salon at the end of the lane. Your aunt called me at eight this morning from the ward. You came from Osaka, you had a café, you sold it for a very good price, more than you put in, she said. And now you are taking over the shop. My nephew sold his apartment in Suita last year and lost money on it, and his wife blames the stairs. He should have bought on the ground floor. I told him so at the time."

"I'm only looking after the shop," Sayo said. "And it was a fair price. I wouldn't say very good."

"She said very good. She sounded proud. She said you would stay until the camellia. I didn't understand that part."

Because Tomoe was waiting, and Mrs. Mori had stopped by the door to listen, Sayo said, "It was mostly the oven. I had a second oven, almost new. That is what they really paid for."

"An oven," said Tomoe. "Come to the salon on Thursday. I will do something about your hair."

When they had both gone, Sayo stood with one hand flat on the counter. She had told the lie yesterday, to one old woman in a hospital bed. It had already been to the end of the lane without her and come back larger.

At noon Mr. Hirose came and stood in the doorway with the curtain resting on his shoulder.

"Is she eating?"

"She says the porridge is cold."

"It was cold when I was in there." He looked at the plants on the steps, which she had turned so their best sides faced the lane. He bent down and turned the kumquat back the way it had been, and went next door.

Just before three an old man in a cloth cap came in with his wallet already open. He took out half a ticket, soft as cloth from being carried, and put it on the counter. "The rubber plant," he said. "I left it in October. I had an operation on my eyes."

Sayo found the other half on the middle shelf, tied to a rubber plant that had put out new leaves while he was away, still rolled tight in their pink sheaths. The old man put on his glasses and bent right down to it.

"It has grown," he said. "Look at that. And I can see it now." He tried to pay, and Sayo told him there was nothing to pay, because that was the shelf. He stood with his wallet for a while. Then he put a hundred-yen coin on the counter in front of the cat. "For the president," he said, and he stroked the cat once, from the ears to the tail, before he went.

A little after four a boy came in. He was in a high-school uniform and went and stood in front of the ferns.

He stood there a long time. He read the labels. He crouched to look under a leaf without touching it, and twice he looked toward the keeping shelf at the back. Sayo decided he was going to take something, and she stopped pretending to write in the ledger and watched his hands.

"Are you looking for something?" she said in the end.

"I'm waiting for my friend."

No friend came. After a while the boy said thank you, though she had done nothing, and left. When he was out of sight she went outside and counted the small cacti on the step. They were all there, and she counted them again.

Notes

She closed at six, when the tofu maker pulled his shutter down. She made tea on the gas burner behind the counter and sat down with the ledger, which the cat had at last left for the windowsill.

It was a long cloth-bound book, the newest of a row going back to 1973. On the right was a column wider than the rest put together, and over it, on every page, Aunt Kiyo had written one word, Notes.

TsuyukusaThe ledger for February, in Kiyo's hand

3 February, pothos, small, ¥900.
New flat. Her mother died in spring.

5 February, fern in a glazed pot, ¥1,800.
For the dentist's waiting room. He hopes people will look at it and not at the door.

5 February, soil, one bag, ¥350.
Kanae Mori. Paid ¥300 and was pleased with herself all morning.

8 February, narcissus bulbs, six, ¥500.
His first winter on his own. He wants to see something come up.

Sayo read the page, and then the one before it, while the tea went cold.

There was a note beside nearly every sale. A cactus had gone to a boy who had failed an examination and did not want flowers. A woman had bought a fern and come back the next day to return it, and Aunt Kiyo had written only, She was right, it was the wrong fern. Sayo could not think who it was written for.

She wrote her own sales underneath. She left the wide column empty, because she could not think of anything to put in it that was true.

Then she turned back through the winter, and kept turning, into November.

On a Tuesday in November she had mailed the keys of the café to her landlord in Osaka. She found that Tuesday. Aunt Kiyo had sold a fern and two bags of soil.

On the Saturday after, the last sale of the day was a small cactus for ¥600. The note beside it was longer than most.

Sayo read the first word of it and shut the ledger, hard.

The word was her own name.

End of chapter one
