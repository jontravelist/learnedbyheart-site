Noise diary sheet

Wroxbury District Council, Environmental Health. Kept by Ursula Fenn, Flat 2, Lantern House. Week beginning Monday 28 September.

Mon. 3.02 woke up. Piano from Flat 3 straight over my bed. Slow tune. Same as always. Lots of little wobbles on the notes. Ends with one low note over and over, soft, then stops 3.09. Lay there till the alarm.

Tue. 3.01. Same tune. Same low note at the end. Rest day so I let it go.

Wed. 3.04 to 3.11. Played it twice. Drove the 5.40 with my eyes propped open. Clipped a wing mirror on the bypass, first time ever, theirs not mine, still counts.

Thu. 3.00. Banged on the ceiling with the broom. It kept going. Low note at the end again like it's saying goodnight to someone.

Fri. Nothing. Slept. Cried in the shower from relief which is not normal.

Sat. 3.03. Back.

Officer's note at foot of sheet, in pencil: Noted. V.P.

Letter

From Vaughan Prentice, Environmental Protection Officer, Wroxbury District Council. To the Occupier, Flat 3, Lantern House, Wroxbury. Copy by email to the owner, Ms Z. Lindqvist. Wednesday 7 October.

Dear Occupier,

Re: Your piano

The council has received a complaint about noise from your flat. A neighbour reports that a piano is played in Flat 3 every night shortly after three in the morning, for between five and fifteen minutes, and that the sound carries clearly into her bedroom. She has kept a diary over several weeks, which I have read.

You may not be aware that the sound of a piano carries through the floors of a converted house far more than through the walls of a modern building. Most people who receive this letter had no idea, and most problems of this kind are put right by a change of habit. I would be grateful if you would avoid playing between eleven at night and seven in the morning.

If the noise continues, the council has a duty to investigate further. That would mean a visit at night with recording equipment, and if a problem is confirmed, a formal notice served on the occupier. Nobody wants it to come to that, and in my experience it rarely does.

If you would like to talk about this, or think the noise is coming from somewhere else, please write to me at the address above or by email. I'm in the office most mornings.

Yours faithfully,

Vaughan Prentice

Email

From Zora Lindqvist to Vaughan Prentice. Subject: Re: Your piano. Saturday 10 October, 23:52.

Dear Mr Prentice,

Your letter has been forwarded to me, and I have read it twice, once with a glass of wine and once aloud to an empty room in a voice I imagine to be yours, which is the version I enjoyed more.

Yes, it is my piano, and it is my playing, and I'm afraid I'm going to go on doing it.

What your neighbour hears at three in the morning is the Aria from the Goldberg Variations by Johann Sebastian Bach, published in 1741, and according to a story nobody believes, written for a count who couldn't sleep. You'll appreciate the irony, or you won't. What do council officers appreciate? I've no idea; nobody has ever told me. I have played that Aria in some of the best halls in Europe and been paid more for those few minutes than your department spends on biscuits in a year, and I have never once had it described to me as repetitive piano sounds, which is the phrase on the form your neighbour filled in. I would like to frame that form. Could you send me the original?

I'm not going to apologise to Mrs Fenn, though I'm sorry about her wing mirror, and you can tell her so. I'm going to make you a proposal instead. Before you call what she hears a nuisance, before you come round with your clipboard and your nice manners, I want you to listen to it. Once. All the way through, sitting down, with nothing else going on. That's all. Find a recording if you must, any recording; most of them are worse than mine, but you won't know that. Then write and tell me what you heard, in your own words, which I suspect are better than you think. If, after that, you still want to call it noise, I will put a blanket over the strings and play to the blanket.

I know what you'll say. You'll say this isn't a matter of taste; you'll say it's three in the morning and the woman has a bus to drive, and you'll be right, and I don't care. Have you ever been awake at three in the morning on purpose? There are things a person can do at three in the morning that she can't do at three in the afternoon, and you are going to have to take my word for that, because I'm not going to explain it to a stranger whose job is to measure things.

Do you listen to music, Mr Prentice? I ask because nobody who listened to music would have used the word "habit".

Yours in a state of high artistic offence,

Zora Lindqvist

Email

From Vaughan Prentice to Zora Lindqvist. Subject: Re: Re: Your piano. Monday 12 October, 08:14.

Dear Ms Lindqvist,

Thank you for your email, which I have added to the file.

To deal with your points in order:

1. I can't send you the original form. Complaint forms belong to the complainant and are kept on the council's system. I can tell you that Mrs Fenn's handwriting is clear and her spelling is better than mine.

2. You are right that this isn't a matter of taste. The council doesn't decide whether music is good; it decides whether a sound, at the time and level it happens, interferes with someone's use of their home to an unreasonable degree. A brass band and a car alarm are judged the same way, and I know that sounds harsh to someone who plays for a living.

3. Since my letter, Mrs Fenn reports that the playing is quieter. She also reports that it hasn't stopped.

4. In answer to your question: no. Not in the way you mean.

I will be visiting Lantern House at night later this month to take readings from Mrs Fenn's flat. I'm required to tell you that this may lead to a formal notice. I'm not required to listen to anything, but I will be listening, because that is what the visit is for, and you may consider that your proposal accepted on a technicality.

Kind regards,

Vaughan Prentice

PS. Nobody has ever asked me that question in writing, and I've thought about it more than I should have over the weekend. The honest answer is that music happens near me. It's on in shops and at weddings, and I know some songs from hearing them a lot. I've never sat down and listened to a piece from the start to the end on purpose. When I was eleven, a teacher who ran the school choir asked me to move my mouth during the carol concert and not let any sound come out, because I was pulling the others off the note. I've kept to that since, more or less, and it hasn't done me any harm that I know of. I suppose I thought listening was for people who could do the other thing. I don't know why I'm telling you this. Please don't reply to the PS.

Do you know what a thing like that does to a child, said by a grown-up in a voice like that? I've been sitting here for an hour working out what I'd do to her, and I've got as far as her car.

I'm a bit drunk, you'll have gathered, I'm not usually, is that obvious? I wanted to tell you something about the Aria, and why three o'clock, because you asked, well, you didn't ask, Mrs Fenn asked, the form asked. I used to play it at three. Every night, at three, because that was when he woke, and it was the only thing that stopped him. Not the bottle, not his father singing, and his father had a beautiful voice, nothing worked but that. The Aria. And then I'd get to the end and I couldn't stop playing or he'd start again, so I'd just keep playing the bottom note, the last one, soft, again and again till he went. You'd think you'd forget a thing like that. You'd think a person would. And he can't possibly remember, he was a baby, it's not, you can't remember that, can you, so how does he, how would anybody

[Silence. Then she hums the start of a tune, a long way off the note, and stops. A nose blown, loudly, twice.]

Noise monitoring visit record

Wroxbury District Council, Environmental Health. Officer: V. Prentice. Address of complaint: Flat 2, Lantern House. Suspected source: Flat 3, above. Date: night of Tuesday 20 to Wednesday 21 October. Equipment: sound-level meter, calibrated before and after. Weather: dry, still. Readings taken in the main bedroom, window shut, meter on a tripod at the height of the bed.

Arrived 02.30 to find Mrs Fenn awake and dressed in her uniform trousers and a dressing gown. She sat in the kitchen and asked me to tell her when it was over, because she didn't want to watch somebody listen to it.

Background level 02.30 to 02.55: quiet house, low and steady, nothing above it but a single car on the main road at 02.41.

02.52: front door below opened and closed. Footsteps on the stairs, past this flat, up. A door above.

03.03: piano begins, directly overhead. Readings well above the background for the whole of the playing. Full figures attached.

Observations. I'm required to describe the character of the noise, so I'll try. It is slow, and it is one tune, which goes up and comes down, with a great deal going on at the edges of the notes, which Mrs Fenn calls wobbles and which I don't have a better word for. Under it there is a lower line, moving at a walk. It stops in the middle, and goes back, and plays the first part again, and I found I was glad it went back, though I can't say why, since it was the same. Then a second part and that went back too. Partway through I stopped writing. I'm putting that on the record because I have never stopped writing on a visit before.

At the end there is one low note, played softly, again and again. It went on for a little over two minutes by the meter clock. It got slower, and then it stopped, and the house was quieter than it had been before it started.

03.14: playing ends.

Mrs Fenn came to the bedroom door and asked if I'd got it. I said yes; she asked if I'd heard the low bit; I said yes. She said that's the bit, and went back to the kitchen.

To confirm the source, as the case may proceed to a notice, I went up the stairs to Flat 3 at 03.21. The stair light is on a timer and went out halfway. I knocked twice, and after a while the door opened as far as its chain. A young man stood there, mid-twenties and tall, with a jumper on inside out and white dust on his forearms which I took to be flour. I said who I was and that I was sorry to call at this hour. He looked at my lanyard for some time. He said, "She's not here," and shut the door. I heard the chain go back on.

Source confirmed: Flat 3.

Recommendation: the noise amounts to a nuisance at the time it occurs. Proceed to an abatement notice on the occupier.

Left 03.40. Mrs Fenn asked me to tell her when the notice went in, and whether, when it did, the low bit would stop too.

Email

From Vaughan Prentice to Zora Lindqvist. Subject: Visit to Lantern House. Wednesday 21 October, 10:02.

Dear Ms Lindqvist,

I visited Lantern House last night, and I attach my record of the visit, as you're the owner of Flat 3 and entitled to see it.

The noise was confirmed, and I'm afraid the levels in Mrs Fenn's bedroom were high enough at that hour that the council will now serve an abatement notice. That's a legal document, and it will give a date by which the playing at night must stop. I expect to serve it next week, by hand, on the occupier. I'll send you a copy at the same time.

I want to tell you, outside the record, that I did what you asked. I sat down and listened to it once, all the way through, with nothing else going on but a meter. You'll see from the record that I'm not good at saying what I heard. I'd like to try again in a letter, when this is over, if you'd let me. I've never wanted to describe a sound to anyone before. Mostly I want to know why I was glad when it went back to the beginning. Is that something you could explain, or is it the sort of thing nobody can?

Please thank the young man who answered the door, whether he's your lodger or your son, I didn't ask. I'm sorry I woke him, though he didn't look as if he'd been asleep. Does he work nights, do you know?

Kind regards,

Vaughan Prentice

Email

From Zora Lindqvist to Vaughan Prentice. Subject: Re: Visit to Lantern House. Wednesday 21 October, 23:06.

Mr Prentice,

I'm not in Wroxbury. I haven't set foot in that flat since March, and I haven't played a note, there or anywhere else, in three years.

I've let you think I was the one playing, from my first email, and I'd like to tell you it was a misunderstanding, but you'd know it wasn't, because you're a man who writes down what time a car went past. I knew the minute I saw Mrs Fenn's diary. One low note, soft, over and over, like it's saying goodnight to someone. Nobody plays it like that. Nobody has ever heard it played like that except one person, and he was a baby, and I made it up for him in that flat in the middle of the night because I couldn't think what else to do.

His name is Yanik. He is twenty-four. He hasn't spoken to me since he was seventeen, and I deserved that, and I'm not going to tell you why. The last time I saw him he was getting up from the middle of a row in a concert hall and walking out while I was playing that same Aria. I came all this way to look for him, and he was in my flat. I didn't know he still had a key, and I didn't know he could play it.

I wanted the council to come after me. That's all. I thought if I was rude enough and grand enough you'd write to me and not to him, and he'd never have to know I'd seen the diary, and he could go on doing whatever it is he's doing at three in the morning, which I think is trying to finish something. It was the only thing I could think of to do from this far away.

Did he look well? You said he didn't look as if he'd been asleep. Did he look as if anybody feeds him?

You said the notice goes by hand to the occupier.

So the notice you're about to serve, Mr Prentice, goes to my son.
