Initial Here

Chapter one. Home Equity Day.

The VFW hall

On the third Saturday in April a company called Stonecrop Home Partners rented the VFW hall in Tullis, Missouri, for two hundred dollars and a promise to mop, and fried catfish for anybody who walked in. They hung a vinyl banner over the door that said HOME EQUITY DAY, and under it, in smaller letters, NOT A LOAN. At one o'clock a man from St. Louis in a headset microphone climbed onto the plywood stage where the VFW called its bingo and told the people of Tullis that they were sitting on money they didn't know they had.

He meant their houses: Tullis, Missouri, had about twenty-nine hundred people, and most of the ones over forty owned their homes outright, because their parents had owned them first and because nobody from anywhere else had wanted to buy a house in Tullis since the shoe factory closed. The houses were worth very little, and the man from St. Louis said so, kindly, and said that this was the beauty of it. Stonecrop would hand any homeowner a check for fifteen thousand dollars today, before they finished their pie. In return Stonecrop would own a share of the house's value whenever it was sold, and if it was never sold, well, then it was never sold. There were no monthly payments and there was no interest, and he said "no interest" so often that some of the older people went home believing it was the name of the company.

By four o'clock two hundred and twelve households had signed. The town agreed, that day and for months afterward, that it was the closest thing to free money anybody in Carrow County had ever been offered.

They signed at folding tables along the back wall, under the photographs of the men who had gone to Korea, and at the last table, with a notary's embosser and an ink pad and a cup of sweet tea she never finished, sat Cora Haskett, attorney at law. She was twenty-nine, and she had never in her life been needed by so many people at once. Stonecrop had hired her as its local closing counsel for a retainer of two thousand dollars, and it paid her twenty-five dollars on top for every agreement she witnessed and sealed. The agreement was nineteen pages long and every page had a short line in the bottom corner with INITIAL HERE printed under it. A courier had brought her a copy on the Monday. She had read the first three pages at her desk over a pimento cheese sandwich, and the definitions had gone on and on, "Appointed Appraiser" and "Maintenance Standard," and then the phone had rung about a speeding ticket and she had put the whole thing in the bottom drawer.

People asked her about it all afternoon. She had grown up on Locust Street and she did their wills for a hundred and fifty dollars. She had a certificate on her wall and she was from there.

Royce Gentry asked her at about half past two. He was a heavy, slow man who had kept the conveyors running at the grain elevator until the co-op sold it, and he stood at her table turning the pen over in fingers that still had catfish grease on them. His daughter, Shay, stood behind him eating hush puppies out of a paper boat. He wanted to know if Cora had read all of it.

"I've been over it," Cora said. "It's fine, Royce, it's a good deal for anybody who isn't planning to sell."

"We ain't selling."

"Well, then."

He initialed eighteen pages and signed the nineteenth with his whole name, slowly, and left a gray thumbprint of grease on page fourteen, and she sealed it. Garrett Shull, Stonecrop's regional man, tall and easy, who had brought her a fresh tea every hour without being asked, leaned over her shoulder to check the date and put his hand flat on the small of her back while he did it. He left it there while he read. She thought about that hand for most of a week.

She made seven thousand three hundred dollars that day. She sent four thousand of it to the student loan servicer, the first payment in two years that had moved the balance, and she paid the drugstore the rent on her office through to Christmas. It was the best day's work she had ever done, and she loved every minute of it.

Municipal court

In November the Tullis municipal court still met on Tuesday nights in the council chamber at City Hall, under a paper skeleton that somebody had taped to the flagpole at Halloween. The judge drove over from Sikeston, a lawyer named Purcell who did it for a small monthly fee and wanted to be home by eight. Most of the docket was speeding and dogs at large, and the defendants sat in folding chairs in their coats and waited for their names.

Cora had one case that night, for a home health aide named LaDonna Fikes, who had been given a forty-dollar ticket for parking in the fifteen-minute space outside the drugstore while she waited on a prescription for a man who could not get out of bed. Cora had gone and looked, and found the fifteen-minute sign lying face down in the dead grass behind the storm drain, where the city's own snowplow had knocked it the winter before.

She showed Judge Purcell a photograph of it. The city attorney, a young man in a fleece vest, said the zone was well known to local drivers.

"So is the ditch," Cora said. "If the city wants people to obey that sign, it can start by standing it up. Right now the only thing it's regulating is the grass."

Somebody in the folding chairs laughed. "Dismissed," Purcell said, and called the next name.

LaDonna paid her seventy-five dollars in the hallway in cash and hugged her hard enough to hurt.

Royce Gentry was standing by the drinking fountain. He had been in the back row the whole evening, she realized, in his canvas work coat, and she had not seen him.

"Royce, hey, is this about the will? Because I've got it in the file, if you want to change anything for Shay."

He held out an envelope. It had been folded in half and in half again, and at some point it had got wet and dried crooked.

"They put this on my door," he said, and when she didn't take it fast enough he pushed it into her hand.

The back room

Cora's office was over the drugstore, up a flight of icy outside stairs. It had been a dentist's before it was hers, and the dentist's chair was still bolted to the floor of the back room, because the man from Poplar Bluff wanted more to unbolt it than she made in a month. Royce walked straight past her desk and into the back and sat in the dentist's chair. It tipped back under him with a hiss, and he lay there in his coat with his boots up and his hands folded on his stomach.

She switched on the dentist's lamp and read the notice.

It was one page on Stonecrop letterhead and it was called NOTICE OF MAINTENANCE DEFAULT AND EXERCISE OF PURCHASE OPTION. It said that Stonecrop's inspector had visited the property on Elevator Road and found it in breach of the Maintenance Standard, namely "roof covering compromised, temporary tarpaulin in place." It said that under Section 14(c) of the Agreement, Stonecrop now elected to purchase the property at its Fair Market Value as of the Effective Date, as determined by the Appointed Appraiser, being $38,500, less the Advance of $15,000, less a Default Administration Fee of $4,250. A check for $19,250 would be issued at closing. The owner had sixty days to vacate.

She asked him what had happened to the roof.

"Shay's teeth."

She waited, but he didn't add anything. She fetched the blank agreement from the bottom drawer, still in its courier envelope, and for the first time since April she turned past page three.

Section 14 was on page eleven and ran for a page and a half. "Effective Date" meant the day the homeowner signed. "Appointed Appraiser" meant an appraiser chosen by Stonecrop in its sole discretion. "Maintenance Standard" was defined on page two in one sentence that ran most of the page, and the roof was in it, and so were the gutters, the porch, the septic tank and "any condition the Appointed Appraiser reasonably considers detrimental to value." Section 19 sent every dispute to binding arbitration in Wilmington, Delaware, and said no homeowner could bring or join a class action. Section 22 said the agreement bound the homeowner's heirs and would be recorded against the house at the courthouse.

Cora read Section 14 a second time with her finger under the words. Then she went and stood in the doorway of the back room.

"Can you stop it?"

The office phone rang in the front room. Clients used that number in the daytime and the courthouse used it when somebody was in jail, and it was neither. She picked it up and pulled the door to the back room half closed.

"Counselor. I'm at the Drury in Cape Girardeau with a club sandwich and forty files, and I'd rather give the files to you than to a title company. Four hundred a closing."

"I saw the governor on the news, Garrett. Out on the river bottoms with a gold shovel and a battery plant."

"So did my boss. He watched it twice and called me during the second time."

"You're buying them back at April prices."

"We're doing what page eleven says. I can read it to you if you've lost your copy."

"You fed them catfish," Cora said. "You could at least have the decency to sound tired about it."

"I've been tired since Memphis," he said, with his mouth full of something. "Is that a yes? I'm going to hear that as a yes."

Through the gap in the door she could see Royce lying in the chair. Sixteen thousand dollars was more than a year of interest on her loans. It was the rent through next summer.

"Send it over," she said.

"Atta girl. I'll see you before Thanksgiving, I hope. I'll buy you a real dinner this time, no catfish."

She said goodnight and hung up. She went back into the back room and sat down on the dentist's rolling stool.

"This isn't a foreclosure, Royce. When you signed, you gave them the right to buy your house if you let it run down, and they say the tarp counts. The price is what the house was worth the day you signed, in April, before the plant, and their own appraiser set it."

"What if I say the tarp don't count?"

"Then you argue it in Delaware. Section 19 says any dispute goes to an arbitrator there, not to a court here. Stonecrop picks the arbitration company."

"Is that a no?"

"It's a no."

The chair hissed as he sat up. He put his boots on the floor and looked at her for the first time since he'd come in.

"You told me you'd been over it."

Cora had known this was coming since the drinking fountain, and she had the answer ready, and it was true as far as it went.

"Royce, I stamped that you were Royce Gentry and that you signed it of your own free will. That's what a notary does, that's all a notary does. I wasn't anybody's lawyer at that table and I couldn't have been. I was there to check people's driver's licenses."

He thought about it for a long time. Then he decided she was right. She was the lawyer, and he had only ever been in a courtroom for his divorce.

"Okay," he said, looking at the floor. "I'm sorry, I didn't mean nothing by it. I know you just do the stamp."

"It's all right."

"I shouldn't have come up here at night. You've got your own things." He stood and folded the notice back along its old creases and put it inside his coat. "What do I owe you for tonight?"

"Nothing, don't be silly."

He shook her hand and thanked her. Then he went down the icy stairs one at a time, holding the rail.

Cora shut the door behind him. She stood in the front room for a while, and then she kicked the bottom drawer of the filing cabinet so hard it jammed half open and would not close again.

"You stupid, greedy idiot," she said out loud, to nobody. "Three pages. You read three pages."

The email came at twenty to ten. It had a spreadsheet attached, forty rows of names and addresses with a column headed OPTION PRICE, and she knew nearly every name on it. She had sat beside half of them in church. The fourth row was Gentry, Royce, Elevator Road. She read all forty rows twice, and she wanted the money more than she had wanted anything since the bar results came out, and she let herself want it. Then she turned off the dentist's lamp and went home and ate cereal standing at the counter and went to bed.

Elevator Road

The Gentry house stood alone on the county road past the grain elevator, a white frame house with a screened porch and a blue tarp over the back half of the roof, held down at the edges with old truck tires. The fire started at the back. Later there would be a great deal of argument about where at the back it started, and by whose hand. The smoke alarm in the hall woke Shay, and Shay woke her father. The two of them went out the front door in what they had slept in and stood in the frozen yard and watched the roof catch.

The Tullis volunteer fire department arrived in two trucks about a quarter of an hour later. They ran a line from the tanker and saved the shed. The house went anyway. By three o'clock it was gone to the foundation, and the kitchen stove stood in the open where the front wall had been.

Royce stood in the yard in his socks the whole time with a garden hose in his hand. The hose had frozen solid sometime in October, and nothing came out of it, and he held it pointed at his house until one of the firemen took it off him. Somebody's wife arrived with blankets. Somebody else took Shay to sit in a warm truck.

Wade Haskett, the deputy on nights, pulled into the yard at twenty past two. He was Cora's older brother, and he had known Royce Gentry all his life. He walked up to him slowly with his hands in his jacket and stood beside him and watched the fire with him for a while.

Then Royce said, loud enough for Wade to hear and for two of the firemen, "Well. They can have it now."

One of the firemen found the gas can a little after three, in the bed of Royce's pickup. It was red plastic and nearly empty. Royce said it was for the mower. The fireman said there was no mower in the shed that he'd seen. Royce said the mower had been sold in August. Then he seemed to understand what they were asking him, and why, and he stopped answering. The sergeant came out from the county seat at four, and Shay was still sitting in the warm truck with the heater running when they walked her father past her to the car.

A call at night

Cora's phone rang at twenty to six. She found it on the floor beside the bed.

"Did I wake you up?" Wade said.

"It's not even six."

"Yeah, listen, is Mom's big coffee urn at your place? The Pentecostal church wants it. They've got Shay Gentry over there, and half the road coming in." She heard a radio behind him and a car door. "The Gentry place on Elevator Road. Is that one of your Stonecrop houses?"

"Yes, it is. What happened to it, Wade?"

"It burned down. They got out, they're both fine, but the house is gone." He breathed out. "Sergeant Tibbs has took Royce to the jail."

"For what?"

"Arson's what Tibbs wants. They found a can. And he said a thing in the yard, Cora, he said it in front of me and two firemen, and I can't unhear it." Wade was quiet for a while. "He talked some out there, and then he quit. He won't say another word to anybody now. He keeps asking for his lawyer."

"Royce doesn't have a lawyer."

"He gave them your name."
