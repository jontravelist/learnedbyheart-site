The organist had been halfway through the second verse when the power went, on a Tuesday evening in February, and because she was alone in the chapel and the dark came all at once, she stood up and felt her way out along the backs of the pews, and left the lights and the organ blower switched on.

Nobody had touched them since. Skelby was full of things like that by October: kettles left on at the wall, a radio still tuned to the station it had been playing, a washing machine of sheets that stayed wet for eight months because nobody could face opening the door. People had stopped seeing them. Esther saw every one.

Islanding

Chapter one. The Blower Switch.

Wood

She split the care home's wood in the dark, before the night carer unbolted the back door, because if she did it in daylight someone would thank her.

The logs were ash and sycamore from the trees that came down on the river path in the March gales, and they had been sitting under a tarpaulin long enough to crack along their ends. Esther set each one on the block, felt for the split with her thumb, stepped back, and let the axe fall from above her head with her hands sliding together on the shaft, which a man on the allotments had shown her in April. Her breath went up white. The frost had come down off the fell in the night and furred the bins and the handrail of the ramp where the residents used to be wheeled out to sit in the sun, and under her boots it squeaked.

Her body had changed since the spring. For years she had sat at a desk through the night with her shoes off under it, eating crisps from a machine and drinking coffee that tasted of the cup, and now there was a hard new muscle across the tops of her shoulders and her hands had split at the knuckles, and when she lay down at night she could feel her own heartbeat in her palms. She was fifty-one. She was stronger than she had ever been and she did not know what to do with it except this.

When the stack against the wall was up to the window ledge she stopped, wiped the axe head on her trousers and leaned it where she had found it. Through the glass of the back door she could see a candle moving along the corridor, low down, carried by someone checking the rooms. She went out through the side gate before it reached the kitchen.

The walk home took her over the bridge and up the steep lane past the chapel, and at the top of the lane, if she lifted her head, she could see the substation on the shoulder of the fell. A square of fencing. Two steel frames standing up out of the bracken like the ribs of something. And between them, black against the paler hill, the shape of the transformer that had burned in February, too heavy to move and too expensive to leave, sitting where it had died.

She did not lift her head.

Visitors

They came at dusk, when she was rinsing jars in a bucket in the yard, and she heard Arthur Pike before she saw him because he was already talking as he came round the corner of the house, telling someone about the gate.

"Hinges are on the wrong side, that's your trouble, whoever hung it hung it from the inside thinking about the wind and never thought about the latch, I said so to Margaret's lad. Evening, Esther."

He was seventy-nine and had wired most of the houses in Skelby at one time or another, including hers, which he had told her the first week she moved back, standing in her kitchen pointing at the sockets. He walked with two sticks now and still wore a tie. Behind him came Oskar Lunn with a rucksack that clinked when he set it down. Oskar was nineteen. He had been at college in the city when everything stopped, and he had walked home along the old railway line, sleeping in a signal box. That was how people described him now, the boy who walked home. He had black under all his fingernails and a burn mark like a coin on the back of one hand.

"There's a meeting," Arthur said. "Half past seven at the chapel. Eamon's had a letter."

"From who?"

"From them. The electric company. A lad brought it on a bike from the depot at Kelderby, God help him, all that way in this, and Eamon won't say what's in it till everyone's there, you know what he's like, he'll want to read it out with his glasses on. And Irene's got her idea she wants to put, the weir idea, with the screw. And we thought." He stopped. He looked at Oskar for help with the rest of it. Oskar was looking at Esther's jars.

"We thought you might come," Arthur said. "Seeing as you were with them."

"I was in billing."

"Well, you were in the building."

"It was an office in a business park, Arthur. I sent people letters about what they owed. I couldn't tell you which end of a fuse goes in first."

It was a good lie because it was boring. Each time she told it, it had been accepted with a small loss of interest that she could see happening in their faces, and she had felt the loss like a draught closing a door. That was what she wanted. She wanted to be the least interesting person in the valley.

Oskar unzipped the rucksack and took out a roll of wallpaper, the cheap sort with a pattern of brown leaves, and turned it over and unrolled it across the lid of the rain barrel, holding the ends down with two of her jars.

On the back he had drawn the town in pencil. The river. The weir by the old mill. The care home, the school, the pumping station that fed the reservoir, the chapel, the health centre, each a careful little box with its name printed underneath. And lines between them, ruled, with arrows, and numbers he had rubbed out and written again until the paper was worn grey.

"That's the screw," he said, putting a finger on the weir. "That's Uma's turbine. That's the school roof, the panels. These are the batteries I've got. If they all go into the same wires and nothing goes out past here," he moved his finger to a line he had drawn across the bottom of the valley, heavy, gone over many times, "then it's ours."

Esther looked at it for longer than she meant to.

It was nearly right. It was a clever child's drawing of a horse, every part there and none of them able to carry weight. He had drawn the screw and the panels and the turbine pouring into the wires like rain into a tank, and they would not, they would sit there dead as the kettles, because every one of them had been built to follow something that was no longer there to follow. She could see it like a misspelling on a sign. She could have put her finger on the exact place. And if she did, he would ask how she knew, and Arthur would turn his head.

"This is a child's drawing," she said.

Oskar took the jars off the ends, one and then the other, and set them back on the wall in the places he had taken them from, and rolled the paper up from the bottom, slowly, keeping the edges level, and put it back in the bag. Arthur looked at the ground.

"Batteries out of bicycles," Esther said. "Wired up in your grandmother's back bedroom. You'll have the house on fire by Christmas and they'll be carrying her out in her nightdress."

"Esther," Arthur said.

Oskar zipped the bag and stood up. He was taller than both of them.

"I'm not coming to the meeting," he said to Arthur. "I'm taking the service cable off the pole by the bridge. At eight. Irene wants the copper for the weir."

"We said Saturday. We said daylight and me there."

"It's dead. Everything's dead. It's been dead since February."

There was a rule, and Esther knew it better than her own phone number, and it was printed on a card on the wall above every desk in the place she used to work. She could have said it now. It was one sentence. And to say it she would have had to stand in her yard in front of Arthur Pike and explain how a woman from billing knew what linesmen were told before they climbed.

She picked up a jar and put it in the bucket and rinsed it. Oskar went off down the lane with the rucksack clinking, and Arthur stood a while longer on his two sticks, breathing, and then said he would see her at half past seven, and that she should bring a candle of her own if she had one, because the chapel was short.

The chapel

She went because not going would be noticed. The chapel was full and very cold. People had come in their outdoor coats and kept their hats on, and the candles they had brought stood along the pew ledges in saucers and jam lids, so that the faces were lit from below and looked older and more alike than they were. Children slept on piles of coats in the side aisle. The breath of a hundred and fifty people hung under the roof and did not go anywhere, and the windows ran with it, and the whole room smelled of paraffin, and of something sweeter underneath that Esther realised, after a while, was apples, because the Hirst farm had brought two sacks and left them open by the door.

She sat at the back. The organ was behind her, up in its loft, a dark wall of pipes she could feel rather than see.

Eamon Coyle stood at the front by the communion rail with his glasses on and the letter held out at arm's length, angled to a candle that his wife held up for him. He chaired the parish council and read the letter twice, the second time more slowly, because he liked the sound of it.

"Following the failure of the transformer at the Skelby substation during restoration operations on the night of the eighteenth of February, a replacement unit has been ordered. Owing to the national position, and to the prioritisation of supply to hospitals and major population centres, restoration of supply to the valley is not expected before late spring. The cause of the failure is the subject of an internal review. We thank customers for their continued patience."

He lowered the letter. "So there it is," he said. "There we are. Late spring."

For a moment the room was only breath and candle smoke. Then a man near the front said, "What's it mean, the national position," and Eamon said it meant the national position, and a woman said it meant they were last, and somebody laughed in a way that was not a laugh.

Esther had her hands in her pockets. She was holding her own fingers. She had been on the other end of that sentence, the internal review, she had sat in a room with no windows on the third floor with two people from the safety team and a recorder on the table between them, and she had answered their questions in full sentences, and then she had driven home with the radio off and three days later she had put everything she owned that would fit into the car and driven up the valley to the little house on Chapel Lane that she had been letting to walkers for ten years, and she had not told anyone in Skelby that she had been anywhere near the eighteenth of February at all.

Ola Brennan from the care home stood up without being asked. She was a big quiet woman with her hair under a woollen hat, and she spoke from where she stood.

"The generator's got nine days of diesel," she said. "That's for the heaters in the two end rooms and the pump for Mr Fairley's bed, and that's all it does now. I've stopped the freezers. After nine days I don't know." She stopped. "We lost Bettina and Edwin and Alma in the cold in February. I'm only saying it so you know I know what it looks like."

She sat down.

Then Irene Shaw got up with a folder. Irene had taught chemistry at the high school for most of Esther's childhood and now chaired the committee that ran the little hydro scheme at the weir, the one that had gone in ten years ago with a grant and a ribbon, a great steel screw lying in a channel beside the mill race, turning slowly in the water and making power for the grid and money for the village hall. It had not turned since February. Irene spoke in the voice of someone reading out the minutes of a meeting that had already happened.

"It is proposed," she said, "that the committee investigate the feasibility of operating the weir installation independently of the network, to supply essential premises over the winter months, those premises to be identified by this meeting. It is noted that the committee does not at present possess the expertise." She closed the folder. "That's the long and the short. We don't know how. We have a screw and a river and nobody who knows how."

"Uma's got her turbine," someone said.

"It doesn't go," said Uma Hirst from the side aisle, with her arms folded. "I've told you. It's sat there in a gale with the blades tied off since February. It goes when the grid goes. Don't ask me why. The man who put it up's in Leeds."

"Esther worked for the electric," Arthur said.

He said it from the end of a pew halfway down, not loudly, and the faces turned. She watched them come round towards her in the candlelight, row after row, and she felt her heart going hard and stupid, and her face was hot in the cold room. A hundred and fifty people who had lost eight months, and some of them had lost more than that, and every one of them looking at her with the same terrible ordinary hope.

"Billing," she said. "I was in billing."

"Then you owe us a refund," said the man at the front, and there was laughter, real laughter this time, loose and grateful, and the faces turned away again, and Arthur did not laugh. He kept looking at her a while after the others had stopped, with his two hands folded on the tops of his sticks. Then he looked at the floor.

She was frightened. She had been frightened since February, and she had made a life in which the fear could be carried quietly, like a stone in a coat, and now she could feel it shift.

Eight

They were arguing about the school when it happened. Whether the school or the health centre should come first, if there was ever anything to come first, and Eamon was saying that they could not decide that tonight, and Uma was saying then when, and the candles were burning down in their lids.

Behind Esther, up in the dark of the loft, something began to turn.

It was low at first, lower than hearing, a shiver in the pew under her. Then it rose into a sound she had not heard for eight months and had stopped expecting, the long soft rush of a motor coming up to speed, and the organ's wind chest filling, breathing in, the old leather creaking as it took the pressure. People stopped talking. A child sat up on the coats.

And the pipes spoke. One note, very deep, the note under a hymn, the note that holds the floor up. Ursula Bray had knocked a hymn book off the music desk when she stood up in the dark in February, and it had lain ever since where it fell, its spine pressing down the lowest pedal, and now the wind came through the pipe it held open, and the whole chapel hummed with it, the pew backs, the bones in Esther's face.

Then the lights came on.

Not fully. The bulbs in their brass fittings down the length of the nave glowed a thick dull orange, the colour of a stove ring, and held there, and somebody cried out, and somebody else began to clap, and then the whole room was on its feet, people holding each other, a woman with both hands over her mouth, Ola Brennan standing very still with tears running into her collar. The children were awake and shrieking. Someone shouted that it was the letter, it was a mistake, it had come back early, it had come back.

Esther stayed sitting down.

She watched the bulb nearest her. It brightened and sank. Brightened and sank, a slow swell, like something breathing through its teeth. The organ note sagged with it, slurring flat and climbing back. Nothing she had ever worked with sounded like that. The grid she had kept was the steadiest thing in the country, it did not breathe, it did not wander, it could not, and this was something small and labouring somewhere in the valley, a machine in a shed pushing current the wrong way down a wire it had no business touching, and every line in Skelby that had been dead since February was now carrying it.

Every line.

She looked at her watch, tilting it to the orange light. It was twelve minutes past eight.

Oskar had said he would start cutting at eight.
