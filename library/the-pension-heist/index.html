The Pension Heist

Chapter one. Plan B.

Plan A

Twenty minutes before the net, there had been a much better plan. Plan A was a door.

It was the side door of the Hobday & Crick Safe Company, at twenty to ten on a Tuesday night, and Viktor Varga was kneeling in front of it in a dinner jacket. He'd come straight from his ballroom class. Viktor used to be Hobday's test engineer, so the firm paid him to break into its own safes and then complain about how easy it had been. He was complaining now. I held my phone over his shoulder for light and ate chips with my other hand.

"Those are my chips," said Ezra.

"I skipped dinner for this, Ezra. I'm the brains. The brains needs feeding."

"Five levers," Viktor told the lock. "On a safe factory! I said to them, a child could open this. A bored child." There was a small click. He stood up and dusted the knees of his trousers. "Three minutes fifty. I'm getting old."

"You're a genius. Open it."

He pushed, and the door moved the width of my thumb and went clunk.

"It's bolted," he said. "Somebody has bolted it on the inside."

"So unbolt it. You're the man who opens things."

"With what, charm? You cannot pick a bolt, Josie. A bolt is a lump of steel in a hole."

"Top and bottom," Ezra said through a mouthful. Ezra was our apprentice welder, twenty-three years old and built like a coat rack. "I put them on in March. Mr Lathom asked for them special."

"And you didn't think to mention that?"

"You didn't ask about bolts. You said lock." He took the chips back off me. "They're good bolts. I got a certificate."

I kicked the door. It hurt my foot and did nothing to the door, and I said a word my mother thinks I don't know.

Hobday & Crick had made safes in Nottingham since 1887. On the thirtieth of June it ran out of money and shut, and a firm of accountants called Orme Addison moved in to sell off whatever was left. Administrators, they're called. I had some other names for them. They'd written to all forty-one of us to say we could collect our belongings by appointment, and they gave a phone number. I rang that number fourteen times in seven weeks. Every time, it screeched in my ear and hung up.

I wanted my things. I was sick of asking nicely, so I'd asked Viktor. Then Viktor told Simone, and Simone told everybody who owned a phone.

A horn went off in the road, twice. Then came a loud steady beeping, and a seven-and-a-half-tonne lorry with a crane on its back reversed into the car park and flattened a traffic cone.

"Oh, for God's sake," I said.

"That will be Yoz," said Viktor.

"I told him to come quietly!"

"He has. He's switched the radio off."

Yoz Demir jumped down from the cab with his arms wide. Yoz drove Hobday's delivery lorry, and he could put a two-tonne safe through a bank's front window without touching the frame. He could not shut a cab door quietly, and he proved it now. "Evening, burglars! Is it open?"

"Why have you brought that?" I said. "I said belongings, Yoz. What did you leave in there, a piano?"

"A cushion."

"You brought a crane to pick up a cushion."

"It's my driving cushion. It's the shape of me. And the car's got no fuel in it, so don't start."

Two more people came round the corner, arguing. Simone Achebe sold our safes to banks, and she could talk her way into any building in England. She was in heels and carrying a clipboard. Nisha Rao designed our electronic locks and had a laptop open on one arm. They were both talking, and neither one had stopped to breathe. "The alarm's already off," Nisha was saying, "there's no power, I wrote four pages and it's just off," and Simone was saying, "Why are we round the back by the bins? I have never gone in by a side door in my life. You go in the front, with a clipboard, and people hold it open for you."

"There's nobody in there to hold it open!" I said.

"Then we should have rung ahead and made an appointment."

"I rang fourteen times, Simone! I told you that on Sunday!"

"Is it open?" said Yoz.

"It's bolted," said Viktor and Ezra together, and Ezra added, "They're good bolts."

Plan B

"The loading hatch," Nisha said.

We all looked up. On the first floor, above the bins, there was a pair of wooden doors where the old hoist used to bring steel plate in. They were about four metres off the ground.

"The catch on that hatch has been broken since last summer," she said. "I reported it. I've got the email. One good pull and it opens."

"Lovely," I said. "Has anybody got a four-metre ladder?"

Yoz coughed. He patted the side of his lorry with one hand, slowly, like a man introducing his horse.

"No," I said.

"I've got a cargo net. It's rated for a tonne and a half."

"No, Yoz."

"Somebody sits in the net, I lift them up, they pull the hatch and go round to slide the bolts. Two minutes. I do it with safes every day."

"Safes don't mind!"

"So who goes up?" said Ezra, and then they were all at it.

"I am not going in a net in my dancing shoes," said Viktor. "These are Italian." Simone said, "I'm in heels, and anyway I'm management," and Nisha said, "I can't, I have to monitor things on the laptop."

"Monitor what? There's no power! You said so yourself!"

"That is exactly the kind of thing that needs monitoring, Josie."

"I'll go up," said Ezra. "I like heights."

"You still owe me for a wing mirror," Yoz told him. "You're not going anywhere near my crane."

Then they all looked at me.

"It's your plan," Simone said. "And you're the lightest."

"I am not the lightest! Nisha is tiny!"

"You've had most of a bag of chips," said Ezra.

So that is how I came to be sitting in a cargo net in my second-best jeans, with my knees up by my ears, rising past the bins.

It was fine for the first metre, and then the net began to turn. The car park went past, then the wall, then the car park again. Yoz had the crane's remote control in both hands and his tongue between his teeth.

"Left a bit!" I yelled.

"That is left!"

"My left!"

"You're going round, Josie! You haven't got a left!"

"Grab the hatch when it comes past," Viktor called. "Timing! It's like a waltz!"

"It is nothing like a waltz!"

The hatch came past, and I grabbed the handle and pulled. Nisha was right about the catch. Both doors flew open towards me, and the left one hit the net and sent me swinging out over the car park. I swung back in again and my foot went through a gap in the net up to the thigh. I ended up hanging upside down, looking at Simone's shoes.

"Don't panic!" said Yoz.

"Get me down!"

"I'm trying! It's stopped!"

"What do you mean, it's stopped?"

"The remote's gone into safe mode. It does that when the load moves about."

"I'm the load! I'm moving about because I'm upside down in a net!"

"Well, stop, and it'll come back on."

I stopped, and I hung there with one leg through the net, turning gently, and the blood ran into my head. Somebody below me was eating the last of the chips.

A light came round the front corner of the building.

It was a camping lantern, and the man carrying it wore a shirt and tie with the sleeves rolled up. He stopped by the lorry. He looked at the five of them, and then he tipped his head back and looked at me.

"Evening," he said. He was Irish, and he sounded like a man trying hard not to enjoy himself. "Would you be here about the belongings?"

Yoz dropped the remote and put both arms in the air.

"We are collecting personal property," Simone said, and held up the clipboard.

"I know you are. I've been waiting for you. Colm Rafferty, from Orme Addison." He pointed the lantern back the way he'd come. "The front door's open. It's been open since six. I'm here every Tuesday evening, six till ten. It says so in the letter."

"The letter said to ring!" I shouted. It is hard to sound fierce upside down, but I gave it everything. "I rang fourteen times!"

"On what number?"

I shouted the number down at him. I knew it by heart.

He winced. "There's a seven in the middle of that. It should be a one. You've been ringing the fax machine in our Derby office."

"For seven weeks?"

"I'm sorry. I didn't type it." He was still looking up at me. He had a nice face, from what I could tell at that angle, and good forearms, and I was furious with both. "Would you like a hand down from there?"

"I'm fine where I am, thank you."

"You're upside down."

"I'm fine upside down!"

"It's back on!" said Yoz, and he picked up the remote and pressed something. The net dropped like a stone. I went into the big bin, the one for cardboard, and the lid came down on my head.

Plan C

Plan C was the front door. We walked in through it like customers, and I picked bits of box out of my hair.

Colm had our things in the canteen, in forty-one cardboard boxes. "Seven Tuesdays I've sat in here with these," he said. "I brought a book and I've read it twice. You're the first people to come, and I had started to take it personally."

"We'd have come sooner if you'd answered your fax machine," I said.

"That's fair. Help yourselves."

Yoz found his cushion and hugged it. Viktor found his leather case of picks and checked each one against the lantern. Nisha got her soldering iron back and kissed it. Simone went through her box twice.

"Where's my 2019?" she said. "My sales award. It's glass, and it's the only one they spelled my name right on."

"Then it'll still be somewhere in the building, and I'll find it for you," Colm said, and he wrote it down.

I liked that he wrote it down. I didn't want to like anything about him, so I went and found my own box. It had my desk fan in it and my emergency chocolate. I ate the chocolate.

"Josie," Nisha said from the corridor. "Come and look at this."

She was standing at the showroom doors with her phone light pointed inside. The others crowded in behind me.

Every visitor to Hobday's got marched down to that showroom to see the Model 40. It was a walk-in vault with a steel door as thick as my arm is long, and that door had stood open every day I'd worked there. We kept the Christmas tree in it. People ate their lunch in there in August because it was the coldest room in the building.

"Who shut the vault?" said Simone.

Then it was everybody at once again. "Is it locked or just shut?" said Nisha, and Yoz said, "My flask's in there!" and Ezra said, "Can we open it?" Viktor walked up to the door on his own. He put his palm flat on the steel and tried the big handle with his weight behind it, and it didn't move.

"Bolts thrown," he said. "Dial spun. You can't do that by leaning on her. Somebody meant it."

"So open it," I said. "You're the genius."

"I spent a month of my life trying to get through that door when she was new, Josie. They gave me drills. They gave me a cutting torch that would go through a bus. The door was fine, and I had a week in bed."

"So that's a no, then," said Yoz. "The genius says no."

"That is a not tonight, and not with what I have in my pockets."

"What have you got in your pockets?" said Ezra.

"A comb and a dance card, and you can mind your own business."

"I was hoping one of you had the combination," Colm said behind us. "She was shut when we arrived in June. Mr Lathom tells me he never knew the numbers."

Viktor blew a raspberry.

Crispin Lathom had bought Hobday's three years back. He wore a scarf indoors and called the safes "product", and I had never once seen him touch one.

"I had a quote from a specialist to drill it," Colm said. "It came to £30,000, and my boss laughed. So the building's being sold as it stands. The buyer gets the keys on the thirtieth of September, and whatever is in the vault goes with them."

"Drill her?" Viktor said. "Over my dead body!"

"My boss said much the same about the £30,000."

While they shouted about that, I went up the back stairs.

The payroll office was at the top, and it had been mine. I went straight to the tall filing cabinet and pulled out the bottom drawer. It was empty, and so was the one above it. I got down on the carpet and felt along the underside of my old desk with my fingers. I found a lump of dried chewing gum.

"It all went to our office in boxes," Colm said from the doorway. "Was it anything in particular?"

I banged my head on the desk. "My stapler."

"Your stapler's in your box downstairs."

"My other stapler. I had two. I'm a very thorough person."

He let that go and leaned on the door frame and held the lantern so I could see to get up. "You're the one who ran the payroll here. Miss Tarrant, isn't it?"

"It's Josie. And yes, I did the wages. What about it?"

"Come down. I've a question, and I think the rest of them should hear it too."

The question

He asked it in the canteen, with the lantern on the table and the six of us round it holding our boxes.

"I wrote to the pension company last month. Kestrel Mutual. It's routine, I ask what's been paid in and I close the file." He took a folded letter out of his shirt pocket. "They tell me they've had nothing from this firm since October."

It took about a second. Then the noise started.

"What do you mean, nothing?" said Simone, and Yoz said, "It comes out of my pay! Every month! It's on the slip!" Nisha had her laptop open again. "That's eight months. October to June is eight months." Ezra asked what a pension company was, and Viktor told him to be quiet. Viktor had gone a bad colour. He sat down on the edge of the table with his case of picks on his knees.

"I'm sixty-one," he said. "That's my pension."

"It's everybody's bloody pension!" said Yoz. "So where's it gone? If it came out of my wages and it never got there, who's got it?"

"That's my question," Colm said. He was looking straight at me. So were the rest of them, one after another, because they knew who did the wages.

I knew, too. On the twenty-eighth of every month, the pension money came out of forty-one people's wages. It had come out in June, same as always.

I was the one who pressed the button.
