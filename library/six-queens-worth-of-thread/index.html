The king's heart is on fire, and I am sewing the flames.

It is a heart the size of my two hands, cut from crimson velvet and caught in a press of silver cloth, and the fire must climb all round it in gold before morning, because tomorrow he rides in it before the whole court. My great-aunt is stitching the heart. I am nineteen years old, and she says I am not ready for hearts.

Tomorrow a thousand people will look at my fire and not one of them will know whose it is. I mind that more than a seamstress in my place has any business minding.

Six Queens' Worth of Thread

Chapter one. Declare I Dare Not.

Greenwich Palace, the night before the tilt, 1526

The wardrobe workroom stinks of the privies under the river wall, but the king's work is allowed wax, and the gold in my lap is too bright to look at for long. It is real gold wound round yellow silk, and it cuts, and my fingertips are raw with it. Every quarter of an hour Aunt Dowse makes me hold both hands in a bowl of cold water until they ache. A sweating hand turns gold thread brown, and she would sooner I lost my fingers than the king lost his colour.

She sits across from me with the heart on its frame, the screw of the press worked in silver above it so that it seems about to turn. Under it run three words she chalked through a pricked paper for me to follow dot by dot, and I could have done them straighter than the chalk, and I have been thinking so all evening with my mouth shut.

A draught from the door flattens every flame, and two gentlemen of the king's chamber come in behind a page with more candles. The page is a boy of twelve or so in a livery coat made for somebody taller, and last week I watched him pocket the chapel's wax ends. The taller gentleman has brought his cup from supper and smells of sack and aniseed comfits. He comes straight round the table and leans over my shoulder, so close that his sleeve drags across the gold and pulls up a loop I will have to sew down again.

"Now here is a riddle with its coat off. Declare je vous. Declare to you, Wat, he would declare himself to you. My father used to say that a man who writes French to the English court writes to be misread, and he was misread all his life, God rest him."

The short one has stayed by the door. "I have no French," he says. "I have a horse."

"It is not vous." The tall one bends closer, breathing sack on my hands. "Nous. Declare je nous. Declare we. The king and who, then? A heart in a press with the screw turned, and he says we. He that tells his secret to the tiltyard, Wat, has a secret he means to have found out."

I cannot bear it. It is my gold he is reading wrong, and by noon tomorrow the whole court will be reading it wrong after him, and none of them will ever know that the seamstress on the stool could have told them. "Nos," I say into the cloth. "Je n'ose. Declare I dare not." I say it no louder than breathing, and as soon as it is out I want it back so badly that my needle stops in the silver.

They both look up. They do not look at me. They look at the page, who is standing at my shoulder with his arms full of candles, because he is the only other mouth in that corner of the room, and because a seamstress on a stool is furniture.

"Well, well," says the tall one, quite pleasantly. "So the candle boy has French."

The page goes red to the ears. "I can't read, sir."

"No more can a magpie, and yet it will have the ring off your sill. Who taught you to read over a gentleman's shoulder?"

The short one, Wat, has come away from the door. He says it slowly, as a man says a thing he has only just seen the size of. "A page that can read the king's device can read the king's letters."

After that nobody is pleasant. The tall one takes the boy by the ear, candles and all, and walks him to the door, and a candle drops and rolls under the table to my feet and lies there smoking. I hear the boy in the passage begin to cry, and the tall one telling him that the gentleman usher will know what to do with a page who has French, and then the door shuts and I cannot hear anything but the river.

I could go after them. I could say it was the woman on the stool, and be turned off myself, and Aunt Dowse with me. I put my needle back into the silver and bring it up a thread's width on. Aunt Dowse has watched all of it. She does not say a word to me. She says, "Pick up that candle," and when I have picked it up she goes back to her heart.

I am frightened enough to be sick, and under the fright, where I would not show it to a priest, I am proud that I knew and they did not.

The tiltyard, Shrove Tuesday

By noon the yard is so loud I can feel it in my teeth, and a dog has lifted its leg on the painted barrier while the heralds shout at it. The wardrobe has a tent behind the king's, and we wait in it with threaded needles in case anything bursts while he arms.

Something does, and a groom puts his head in and says, "The points, the left side, quick," and Aunt Dowse gets up, and her hands will not close on the needle in the cold. She holds them out to me, stiff as roots, and when I take the needle from her she keeps hold of my wrist for a moment. "You have no tongue in there," she says, and lets me go.

So I go into the king's tent and kneel at the king of England's hip, to sew the skirt of my fire to his arming doublet where the lace has torn out. I knew he would be tall. I did not know he would be so broad or so hot, the heat coming off him through the quilted linen as it comes off a horse, with a smell of sweat and civet and armour oil. One slip of my needle into the king's thigh and I do not know what they would do to me. I sew faster and smaller than I have ever sewn, and the needle goes into my own finger instead and leaves a speck of my blood on his linen where no one will see it.

I want him to look down, so badly that I tie off with my face tipped up like a dish held out for alms. He tells the man buckling his greaves that the wind is in the east and will spoil the second course, and somebody moves me out of the way by my elbow.

Back in our tent a woman of about my own age is waiting, in black damask with a gold cross at her throat. She has come for a pin for her hood, she tells Aunt Dowse, and when Aunt Dowse goes out to the brazier to warm her hands she takes a pin from my cushion and stands holding it and does not put it anywhere. She has bitten her lower lip until it is rough.

"I am Ines de Vargas, of the queen's chamber. They told me the old woman's niece made the fire. You are the niece?"

"Yes, mistress."

She draws a strip of crimson sarcenet out of her sleeve, folded very small and warm from her arm, and puts it into my hand and closes my fingers on it with hers. "This is cut from a kirtle of my lady's that she gave me. In the fourth course rides Master Fulke Amory, on a bay with one white foot. You will give it into his own hand." Her English is very correct and very slow, and she says the next part fast, so that it comes out wrong. "You go everywhere. Nobody looks on you. If they find it on you, it is a piece of thread."

"And if they find it on you, mistress?"

She does not answer that. She says, "En boca cerrada no entran moscas," and then, because I stare, "Into a shut mouth go no flies," and lets go of my hand.

The quickest way to the riders' end is under the stands, and I take it. It is dark under there and it stinks, because the grooms piss against the trestles, and the planks overhead drip with spilt wine and shed sawdust down my neck every time the court stamps. Through the gaps I see the court from below, which is a thing nobody is meant to see. Shoes, and the hems of good gowns gone black with tiltyard mud. A wafer someone has dropped, and a small dog licking it. A lady's hand lying on a gentleman's knee inside the fold of his cloak, where above the boards, I suppose, they are sitting a yard apart and looking at the king.

The yard roars, and the stand shakes over me. I have come out under the queen's women, and through a gap no wider than my finger I can see the barrier and the king's horse below it, side on, close enough to smell, and the king himself bending in the saddle in my gold. Above me, at the very edge of the planks, a sleeve hangs down. It is a long hanging sleeve of black, lined with something soft the colour of a fox, and as I watch, the hand inside it lifts to its owner's throat and the whole sleeve falls open over the edge, fur and all, right over the king's head. I cannot see whose it is. I can see the king's bow stop halfway down, as a man stops on a stair. I want to lift my hand in a sleeve like that and have a king forget to finish his bow.

I put up two fingers through the gap and touch the fur. It is warm, and the sleeve twitches up out of my reach, and I run.

Master Fulke Amory is sitting on a barrel at the riders' end with his legs stuck out while a boy buckles his sabatons, eating a pie. He is perhaps five-and-twenty, freckled, with a boil coming on his neck under the edge of his collar, and when I hold out the crimson and say whose it is he stops chewing.

"The Spaniard? With the cross?" He wipes his fingers on the boy's head. "She looks at me in chapel like a hound at a shut door. And I am to wear the queen's own crimson on my arm, am I, on the day the whole yard has money on whose colours the king is wearing? Take it back. Tell her I am flattered past bearing."

Then he puts his hand to my waist, laughing, and his squire laughs, and the hand slides round to the small of my back and stays there, warm through my kirtle. He smells of pastry and horse. "Keep it yourself, mistress seamstress," he says, and pushes the crimson back into my fist. "It suits you better than her. She has a face like a closed Bible." I let the hand stay where it is. I let it stay until he takes it away to finish his pie, and when I walk back along under the stands I am happy, and I know exactly what kind of happy it is, and I do not stop.

Ines is waiting at the canvas. She has not moved, I think, since I left.

"He kissed it, mistress," I tell her, straight to her face, "and put it inside his doublet, over his heart, where nobody would see it, and said he would break a lance on it."

She shuts her eyes, and I watch her throat move. In the fourth course a bay with one white foot comes down the lists, and Master Amory breaks his lance fair and rides back with his arm bare and never once turns his head towards the queen's women, and Ines watches him the whole length of the yard with her hand pressed flat against her own breastbone, where the crimson would be if it were on him. The crimson is in my sleeve.

"Inside, you said," she says. "Inside the doublet, against him."

"Over his heart, mistress."

She smiles at me then for the first time, a crooked, unpractised smile, and asks my name, and when I tell her she frowns at it.

"Kit is for a Christopher. It is a boy's name. I will call you Catalina, which is a name for a Christian," she says, and goes before I can decide whether I mind.

The river, dusk

Jack Lumley is waiting at the palace stairs in his master's wherry, in the green coat he bought at a dead man's sale. It is the green of a pond in August, and he believes it makes him look like a steward. Aunt Dowse is asleep in the stern before the palace wall has slid behind us, with her mouth open and her thimble still on.

"Eight lances, they're saying on the stairs, and a man lost an eye, or both eyes, the eel man would say the Thames ran milk for a farthing. So who is she? There's not a boat between here and Westminster without money on her. My master's got fourpence on a French countess, and I told him, what does a countess want with a man of thirty-four, when she could have a waterman of twenty with all his teeth?"

"I only sewed the fire, Jack."

"If the king's heart's in a press," he says, and drops his voice, and then lifts it again because he cannot help himself, "then it isn't his heart that wants the squeezing."

A man in the next boat laughs, and I go cold all down my back. I tell him about the page with the candles, all of it except whose mouth the words came out of, and that they took the boy away by the ear for knowing three words of French.

"For French?" Jack rows a while on that. "Well, there's no fear of me, then. I've no French. I've only English, and the river's full of that." He grins at me over the oars with his crooked teeth. "And you've none of anything, Kit Ashby. You're a stone with eyes in."

I could tell him. I think of it, with Aunt Dowse snoring and the water going the grey of unbleached holland, and I do not. The Tower goes past on the north bank with the dark coming down on it, and Jack, who talks through everything, stops talking and pulls harder until it is behind us. Why do I keep my head turned to it, when my eyes have been hurting since noon? Ahead the bridge is roaring through its arches, and Jack puts us in at the stairs below, because Aunt Dowse will not be rowed through it for anyone, not for the king.

St Martin's, night

We lodge in two rooms over a glover's in St Martin's, where half the names over the doors are Flemish. Aunt Dowse has not spoken to me since the stairs at Greenwich. She takes off her thimble and sets it on the stool, and then, sitting on the edge of the bed in her shift, she speaks to the wall.

"The candle boy is Hal Fenwick. His mother sells pins at Deptford stairs. They will turn him off without a character, and there is no other house will take a page that was turned off from the king's." She lies down. "Go to sleep."

I wait a long time for her breath to slow. Then I lift the tray out of my sewing chest and take my pricking book from the bottom. The front pages are patterns pricked in lines of tiny holes, roses and vines and a pomegranate I am proud of, and I take care that people see them.

I turn it over and open it from the back.

First the silk. I unpick one thread from the end of Ines's crimson, which was the queen's before it was hers, and take it twice through the calf spine of the book and knot it, one red stitch on the brown. The rest of the strip I fold back into my sleeve. Why do I keep it? I could give it back to her tomorrow with any lie I liked, and I know already that I will not.

Then the crow's quill I keep for marking patterns, cut so fine that I can make a letter no bigger than a pinhole, and from an arm's length the page looks pricked like all the rest. Aunt Dowse taught me with her hand closed over mine and the shutters barred. She hit me then, the first time I read a word aloud in the street, and I thought her hard. I see now she was quick.

Shrovetide, the words of the device and what they mean. The king stopped his bow to the queen halfway, under her women's stand, at a sleeve lined with fox. I never saw the face. Whoever she is, I give her until Easter. I. de V. sent crimson to F. A., and he would not have it, and I told her he wore it at his heart. Lady unknown, hand on a gentleman's knee, third bench. Hal Fenwick, page, turned off for my word. His mother sells pins at Deptford.

I sit with the ink drying and think of the tall gentleman bending over my gold and reading it wrong, and how it felt to set him right, even into the cloth, even with nobody hearing. Is it so very wicked, to want to be caught?

Everyone at Greenwich believes I cannot read. Today that belief cost a boy his place and a Spanish woman her ribbon, and I would not give back either.
