If, Then, Else

Chapter one. Do Not Improve the Instructions.

The car park

Most people who book an assembler want to supervise. They stand in the bedroom doorway holding a mug and tell me step six is wrong. About a third of the time they're right. The rest of the time I build the wardrobe their way, the doors don't meet in the middle, and I get one star. I have a one-star review on my profile that says, "She did EXACTLY what the leaflet said even when it was obviously wrong. Like a robot." I've never asked the app to take it down. I printed it out and stuck it inside the lid of my toolbox.

Petra, the app calls me. Five years assembling, four hundred and twelve jobs, 4.6 stars.

Do not improve them. I read that in the back of the van on Monday night and booked the job before anyone else in Bristol could get it.

On Thursday I parked at Merriott Point at ten to eight. The car park was free until nine and I'd slept in it anyway, in the back on the camping mat between my sheets of offcut chipboard, with my coat over the sleeping bag because the van heater drains the battery. I've slept in the van since October. Nobody knows that. My sister thinks I rent a room over a dry cleaner's, because that's what I told her, and I described the room to her in some detail.

I washed with a flannel and a two-litre bottle of water, and did my armpits under my jumper. Then I sat in the front and looked up at the building.

Merriott Point is fourteen storeys of grey concrete panels on a hill on the east side of the city, with a strip of grass round the bottom and a sign on the grass that says NO BALL GAMES. One balcony has a whole drum kit on it under a tarpaulin. I always check a building has a lift before I quote. Merriott Point has two.

At twenty to nine I took my toolbag and my drill case and went in.

The lobby

The lobby smelled of floor polish and of something fried a long time ago. There was a glass booth by the door with a woman inside it, and a row of post boxes, and the two lift doors side by side, steel, scratched at hip height by pushchairs. Each door had a sheet of paper stuck to it with parcel tape. OUT OF SERVICE. ENGINEER INFORMED.

On the glass of the booth was a poster.

HAVE YOU SEEN WENDELL?

Wendell Saar, 67, Flat 9C. Grey hair, glasses, brown anorak, probably slippers. Last seen in Lift A on Tuesday night at 10.14 pm.

He has a heart condition and he hasn't got his tablets with him.

Anything at all, tell Connie at the desk or ring the police and give the reference number below.

Ref: 5226/0219

The photograph was of a man at a table holding up a piece of cake on a fork for the camera, as if somebody had asked him to show it. He wasn't smiling. He had very clean glasses.

The woman in the booth slid her window open.

"Can I help you? Because if you're not a resident I'll need you to sign in, and I'll need the flat you're visiting and the reason, and that isn't me being nosy, that's the fire procedure, if we have to evacuate I have to know who's in the building, I'm the one that stands on the grass with the list." She held up the visitors' book to show me. "Are you a resident?"

"No."

"So you'll need to sign in."

I signed in. Name, flat, reason. I wrote 9C, assembly, and pushed the book back through the gap.

She read it. Her badge said Connie, Concierge, and under that, in smaller letters, Sallow & Pike Residential, Here to Help.

"9C is Wendell."

"The customer is W. Saar."

"Wendell's missing. Did you not see the poster? You're standing right next to it, you've got your elbow on it."

"I saw it."

"Well, then you can't go up, can you."

It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it. I got my phone out and showed her the job. She read it through the glass with her lips moving, and then she put her hand flat on the counter.

"Monday," she said. "He booked you on Monday night."

"At eight minutes to midnight."

"He never said a word to me. He tells me everything, he told me when he was having his blood taken, he gives me a note for my book if he's having so much as a parcel. And it's not cancelled. If he was going to go off somewhere, he'd have cancelled. He'd have cancelled and then he'd have rung you to say he'd cancelled."

"It says not to improve the instructions," I said. "So I think there are instructions."

That's when I heard the man by the lifts.

Dennis

He was in a wheelchair at the far end of the lobby, facing the lift doors, in a padded coat done up to the chin. He had a carrier bag from the hospital on his lap and a pair of sunglasses on top of his head, the big wraparound kind they give you after an eye appointment. He hadn't turned round when I came in. He was watching the lift doors.

"Is it coming, Connie, or not?"

"It's exactly the same as it was ten minutes ago, Dennis. I've rung them. They've logged it. The engineer has a four-hour window and the four hours started at eight, so we're looking at noon, worst case, which I know isn't what you want to hear."

"I'm fine. I don't mind. I'm waiting for somebody anyway."

Connie looked at me. There wasn't anybody.

"They go off at this time," she said to me, quietly, as if Dennis couldn't hear her across a lobby with a tiled floor. "Not every day. Most days. He's been down there since ten to eight. They brought him back from the eye hospital in a taxi and he put his fob on the reader, and he's been sat there since."

"Was the lift working before that?"

"Yes. That's what I'm saying."

Dennis turned his chair round then, for the first time, with one hand on the wheel.

"You. Are you going up?"

"To nine."

"I'm on twelve. My tablets are on the kitchen table, white box, by the bread. I should've had them at eight." He held out a key on a hospital lanyard. "She can't leave the booth. Rules. It's three floors more than you're going anyway."

I looked at my phone. It was 8.52.

"I have to be in 9C at nine."

"It's three floors."

"It says nine on the job."

"Then be late. Five minutes. Who's going to know?"

"The app knows," I said. "It records when I arrive."

Connie started to say something about the lost property box and a fire marshal on six. I picked up my drill case and went through the door to the stairs. It's a heavy fire door with a square of wired glass in it. On the first half-landing I looked back through the glass, and he'd turned his chair to follow me, and he was still holding the key out at the height of my hand.

The stairs

On the landing of the third floor there was a boy sitting on the steps eating dry cereal out of the box. He was about fourteen, in school trousers and a hoodie and one slider. He got up and came after me.

"Are you the police?"

"No."

"Are you the lift man?"

"No."

"Because basically the police already came, they came Wednesday, and they went in his flat and they had a look and they did his bathroom cabinet, and basically they think he just walked out, but he didn't, because of the camera. There's a camera in the lift, in the corner, and basically you see him get in on nine, Tuesday night, in his slippers, and he presses the button, and..."

"Which button?"

"What?"

"Which floor did he press?"

"You can't see. His back's in the way, he's pressing for ages. Basically the doors shut, and then the next bit of film, same camera, the doors open on the ground floor and there's nobody in it. Nobody. My cousin says he's in the shaft but he isn't, they looked, they had a man with a torch. Are you going to Wendell's?"

"Yes."

"I'm Zeki. I'm off school. I've got a thing with my chest." He coughed. "Is that a drill? What are you going to drill?"

"I don't know yet."

He thought about that for two flights. The window on the sixth-floor landing didn't shut, so the wind came down the stairs at us, and someone had chained a child's scooter to the rail on the seventh with a lock bigger than the scooter.

"I helped him with his laptop once," Zeki said. "He didn't need it, basically, he knew more than me. He just wanted someone in his flat for a bit, I think. He gave me a pound."

9C

The key was under the mat. The mat had no message on it.

Inside it was cold, the heating off, and it smelled of carbolic soap and of toast that had caught. It was the tidiest flat I've ever been in, and I've been in four hundred and twelve. A kitchen through an arch, an armchair by the window, a small table with one chair. A bookcase, tall, along the wall, full of ring binders with the years written on their spines in marker, starting at 1994.

I checked the bookcase. I check all of them. Somebody had built it properly. The cam locks were turned all the way round. The back panel was nailed every ten centimetres down the edges, not just at the corners. The leaflet always tells you to and hardly anyone does. It didn't move when I pushed it. I remember thinking, good, he's had somebody careful.

In the middle of the carpet was a flat-pack box. The brand had been cut off the end with scissors. On the top, in black marker, it said BOOKCASE (1).

"Are you allowed to open that?" said Zeki.

"It's the job."

I cut the tape. There were no panels in it, no fittings bag and no leaflet. There was a hi-vis vest, folded. A torch. A brown envelope, sealed, with 40 written on it. And a ring binder, with one sheet in the front pocket.

ASSEMBLY INSTRUCTIONS. Bookcase (1). For the assembler.

Before you start. Read one step. Do it. Then turn the page. Do not read ahead. Do not leave out a step because it seems pointless. Some of them will seem pointless.

1. Put on the vest.

2. There is a sandwich in the fridge. Eat it. You won't have had breakfast, and there are a lot of stairs later.

3. Sit in the armchair by the window for ten minutes and do nothing else.

I put on the vest. It was too big. On the back, in marker, he'd written ASSEMBLER in capitals, and Zeki laughed at it until he started coughing.

The fridge had half a pint of milk in it that had thickened, a jar of pickle and a plate with a cling-film dome. Under the film was one sandwich, white bread, cheese, cut into two triangles with the crusts off. The bread was hard at the corners and had lifted away from the cheese. The cheese was sweating.

"That's a missing man's sandwich," said Zeki.

"It says eat it."

"That's basically evidence. You can't eat evidence. That's like, if they find him, that's his last..." He didn't finish. "That's disgusting."

I ate it standing up at the counter. It took a long time, because the bread was hard and the middle was soft and wet. He'd buttered it right out to the edges. Zeki watched me the whole time with his hand over his mouth, and once I had to stop and hold the edge of the sink, and then I went on until the plate was empty.

Step 3

The armchair faced the window. From it you could see down over the car park and the grass and the NO BALL GAMES sign, and the main road, and the roofs going down the hill towards the river. You could see my van. From nine floors up you could see the curtain I'd hung across the back windows, and the bucket I keep by the back wheel, and that the van had been there all night, because there was frost on everything except the patch under the exhaust. It made me feel sick, that he could see all that from his chair.

On the windowsill there was a pair of binoculars and a notebook with a pencil in the spiral. The step said do nothing else.

Zeki picked up the notebook.

"Put that down. Step three says do nothing."

"It says you do nothing. It doesn't say me." He opened it. "It's just times. It's the lifts. Look."

He held it in front of my face, so I read it, which wasn't doing anything.

Mon 13 Jan. 07.48 D in at front door. 07.48 A and B out. 40 min.

Thu 16 Jan. 07.51 D in. 07.51 A and B out. 38 min.

Mon 20 Jan. 07.50 D in. 07.50 A and B out. 41 min.

Mon 20 Jan. 18.02 Y in. 18.02 A and B out. 40 min.

Mon 27 Jan. D kept in overnight at hospital. 07.50 both lifts fine.

Thu 30 Jan. Taxi late. D in 08.20. 08.20 A and B out. 40 min.

Always about 40.

Ask Connie for the fob list.

"D is Dennis," said Zeki. "That's him today, basically, that's exactly the same thing. Y is Yusra. She's on seven. She's a chemist. She's nice, she gave my mum..."

"Ten to eight," I said. "They break at ten to eight. It'll be a timer. Something on a timer in a cupboard that somebody set wrong."

"Basically, yeah," said Zeki.

He turned pages. They were all the same, page after page, times and letters, going back to the start of January, in tiny upright pencil. I sat for the full ten minutes, timed on my phone. Zeki read out every line in January and half of February before he got bored.

Then I turned the page.

Steps 4 to 12

They took forty minutes and some of them did seem pointless. I wound the clock on the kitchen wall. I watered a spider plant in the bathroom with exactly one mug of water. I took the batteries out of the smoke alarm, read the date printed on them aloud, and put them back. Zeki said it was like being in a dream where you have to do homework.

Step eleven was to write my own name and the time in the binder, on a line he'd left for it. I did. There was a line above it with nothing on it, and above that, faint, in pencil, another name that had been rubbed out with an eraser, and I couldn't read it.

Then I turned to the last page in the binder.

12. Take the torch. Put the envelope marked 40 in an inside pocket. Do not open it.

14. Go out to the landing. Press the call button for the lifts once. Car B will come in under thirty seconds. Get in. Press nothing.

W.S. · Tuesday, 10.05 pm

There wasn't a step thirteen. I took that for the same joke as the floor numbers over the lift doors in the lobby, which go 11, 12, 14. Lots of buildings do that. People don't want to live on the thirteenth floor, so you call it the fourteenth and they're happy. He'd struck me as a man who'd enjoy that.

I put the envelope in the inside pocket of my coat, under the vest, and went out to the landing with the torch.

The lift doors on nine had their own sheets of paper taped to them. OUT OF SERVICE. I pressed the call button once. It didn't light. Zeki said, "It's not going to come, basically, it's broken, Connie said," and I said the step says thirty seconds, and I stood and counted thirty on my phone with the torch in my other hand. Nothing came. Nothing moved behind the doors. There was no sound in the shaft.

I didn't know what to do then. Step fourteen hadn't happened, so I couldn't do the part after it, and there wasn't a step for this.

Connie came up the stairs. She was breathing hard and holding the rail, and she stopped on the top step when she saw me in the vest.

"Yusra's come in off her night and gone up to twelve for his tablets," she said. "He's still sat down there. He asked me if you got there by nine." She came over and looked at the binder in my hand. "That's his writing. That's how he does his sevens, with a line through. What is this, what's he got you doing?"

"Assembly."

She took the binder off me. I let her. She read the last page standing in front of the dead lift doors, and ran her finger down it to the bottom, and stopped.

"Five past ten," she said. "Tuesday."

The camera had him stepping into the lift nine minutes later.

End of chapter one
