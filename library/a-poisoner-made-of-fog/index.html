I have been asked, now that the business is long over and the Courier has changed its name twice, to set down how I came to know Miss Xanthe Marbury, and I shall do it honestly, though I come out of the first part of it worse than anyone except the man who was hanged.

It began with a fog and a column of print, and the column was mine.

A Poisoner Made of Fog

Chapter one. The Fog Poisoner.

The vestry hall

The inquest on the widow Durrant sat on the ninth of December, 1893, in the vestry hall of Sallowfield, a parish of wharves and gasworks on the south bank below the bridges, and the fog had come in before the jury. It lay along the rafters brown and greasy, smelling of coal tar and the low tide; it turned the gas jets on the walls into sore yellow smudges. The benches were full and the steps outside were fuller, and I had filled them.

Four old people had died in the parish since the fog came down, and each of them had kept a bottle of Pike's Pectoral Linctus on the mantelpiece, as half the parish did. I wrote the police column for the Halfpenny Courier; I had set those two facts side by side in print, given the space between them a name, and watched the Courier sell out before noon. Tomorrow's column was already written in the notebook on my knee, with a gap left for the verdict.

Dr Lomax had the witness chair, the parish doctor and police surgeon both, a broad pink man in a white waistcoat who used a gold pencil to tap his own chest whenever he came to the lungs, and the benches loved him, because he spoke to them and not to the coroner. "A woman of seventy-four, sir, and of a congested habit; the lungs much engorged, the heart labouring, the whole system, if I may so put it, silting up with the season." Asked about the stomach, he folded his hands. "I cannot rule out an irritant, sir, not in conscience, not until the analyst has reported, and with the present press of work I should not look for his report inside a fortnight."

A low sound went along the benches, and I wrote "in conscience" and drew a line under it.

The woman on my right licked her thumb, rubbed something off the varnish of the bench in front of her, and wrote something else there in chalk. She was tall and somewhere past thirty, with a long, sallow, unquiet face, a hat that someone who disliked her must have chosen, and the fingertips of both gloves grey with chalk dust; and on the back of the bench she had written POISON in capitals with a price beside it, which she had just changed from 3 to 1 to 5 to 1.

"That's drifted," she said, not to me but to the coal-heaver on her other side. "Asked him straight and he went all round it, and they only go round when there's nothing in the middle to hit. You had poison at evens, so you owe me."

The coal-heaver, a man named Dunn with black worked into every crease of his hands, considered the chalk. "I never said evens, not in so many words."

"You said near enough evens and then you said go on then, and that's a bet at any rails in England, so pay up, a farthing, and don't look at me like I've had your dinner."

Dunn paid her. She dropped the coin into her glove, chalked a fresh line beneath the first, GIRL CHEEKS CORONER, 2 to 1 on, and offered him the chalk; he folded his arms. Then she stopped attending to the doctor altogether. The jury sat on a form along the side wall, twelve men of the parish in their Sunday collars, whispering behind their hands, and she watched them one face at a time with her lips moving.

What I wanted

"Courier," she said, and turned that face on me so suddenly that I shut my notebook. "No, don't, I've had the heading upside down for ten minutes, I could set it in type. You'd your pencil down all the while he was on about the lungs, and then he says conscience and you're off like a tipster with a telegram. End seat by the door, so your boy has the copy first. A gap on your page the size of a verdict. And when he said a fortnight you smiled, you did, I saw it, at a fortnight of nobody knowing, and there's not another soul in this hall smiled at that, they've all got coughs. You've backed it, not with money, you've no money, you're a Kentish lad on a London wage with your boots soled at the toe, but you've backed him all the same, your poisoner, you've got him running in your colours, and you'd sooner they found arsenic in the widow than nothing, because nothing doesn't sell, nothing never sells, I've watched men lose their shirts on nothing and it doesn't make a paragraph."

"I am from Reading, madam, in Berkshire, and not from Kent," I said, and she frowned at me, having plainly forgotten she had said Kent.

She laughed aloud, so that the coroner looked up. "Then I'm wrong on the county, and I'll stand by the rest at any price you care to name."

"The public has a right to know what passes in its own parish," I said, "and I am paid to tell it."

"Oh, it's paying, there was an old man on the bridge this morning tipped his bottle over the parapet and stood there coughing with nothing for it after." She took up the chalk again. "Here's the girl now; you watch the coroner's face."

The coroner had called Miss Delia Brough, and she came up the side aisle fast, a small, square, dark young woman with a shop apron still on over her dress. On the front bench her employer half rose as she passed: Mr Bartholomew Pike, chemist, a thin, upright, grey man who kept his hat on his knees and both his hands on the hat. In my second column I had called him "watchful", and I had meant something by it.

"You sold the deceased a bottle of linctus on the Tuesday?"

"Yes, sir, the ordinary linctus, the same as everybody has."

"And did you make up that bottle with your own hands, Miss Brough?"

She gave him a look of plain contempt. "It comes in a big jar, sir, and I pours it, I pour it into the small bottles with a funnel, and Mr Pike makes up the big jar, and I've done a hundred of them this month, because the whole parish has a cough, sir. Have you been outside?"

Somebody laughed and more people hissed, and the chalk beside me made a small tick on the varnish. I wrote down "Have you been outside?" and put a mark against it, because I knew already how it would read in print. There was no verdict; the inquest was adjourned until the analyst should report, and the woman at my elbow rubbed out her prices with the side of her fist and sat on, watching the jury file out past us.

The steps

The crowd outside had stood all afternoon in the cold, and the word "adjourned" went down the steps ahead of us and came back up as a noise without any words in it. I got out first, as I had planned, and was on the top step with my notebook open, at arm's length from the door, when Delia Brough came out on Mr Pike's arm.

A woman's voice said, clear over all the rest, "That's her. That's the one that pours it."

"Miss Brough, a word for the Courier, Miss Brough: did you know Mrs Durrant well, and had she ever cause to complain of you?"

She stopped, and that was my doing; she would have gone straight down the steps if I had not given her a reason to turn. While she stood there a woman in a man's cap came up from below, with one of Pike's brown bottles in her fist and the cork already drawn with her teeth, and she held it up under the girl's nose.

"Go on, then, Miss Clever, if it's the same as everybody has, have a sup of it, and we'll all wait."

The people nearest took it up, and then the people behind them, until the whole of the steps was saying it in time: drink it, drink it. Mr Pike said, "Madam, that will do," in a voice nobody could hear, and put out his hand for the bottle, and Delia Brough took it before he could.

She drank it straight off from the neck, with her head back. It was a thick, dark syrup made for spooning, and it would not go down quickly. It came out at both corners of her mouth and ran down her chin onto the bib of her apron; she gagged, stopped, and went on. The crowd watched her throat work. When it was gone she turned the bottle upside down and held it out at them at arm's length, and a last string of it dropped onto the step.

There was no cheering, but a man near the bottom shouted, "Give it a quarter of an hour, then," and there was laughing; then the crowd stayed where it was and looked at her, to see whether she would fall down.

I took out my watch, because I wanted the minute; if she dropped on those steps it would be the paragraph of the year, and I wrote the time in the margin.

She saw the watch, and she wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist and looked straight at me, because I had asked the question, and said, "She come in every Tuesday for her linctus and two ounces of peppermints, and she called me ducky, and she was seventy-four and she'd a chest on her like a set of bagpipes the whole time I've known her, and you can put that in your paper, put every word of it in, and I hope you choke."

I took it all down in shorthand while Mr Pike took out his handkerchief and wiped her chin for her, slowly, and then the front of her apron, and I remember thinking that "bagpipes" would have to come out, because Mr Drage did not like comic words in a serious column. Then the constables got them through to a cab, and the fog closed behind it. She had not fallen down, and I put my watch away.

The woman with the chalk was on the step above me.

"You timed her," she said, "with your watch; you had the lid up, and I was stood right here and saw it."

"I've no notion what you mean, madam."

She held out her hand, palm up, with chalk in the creases. "Sovereign to your farthing there's no poisoner in this parish and never was one. Your farthing in my hand now. I don't lay a newspaperman on credit."

I laughed at her, though not well, since her gloves were darned at two fingers. "And where's your sovereign, Miss, Miss?"

"Marbury, and you'll see it if you win, which you won't." Her hand stayed out between us, and people going down the steps had to go round it, until I found a farthing in my waistcoat and put it there. She closed her fingers on it without looking. "When it's settled, you print it. Your column, your type, same size as today's, and her name in it spelt right, Brough, with the g and the h. And you'll leave out her street tonight."

"Is that a condition of the wager?"

"No," said Miss Marbury, "I wouldn't get a price on it." She pushed the farthing down into her glove with the other two and went off down the steps into the fog, and I stood with the girl's words in my notebook and the time in the margin beside them.

The Courier

At the Courier, in a building that shook whenever the presses ran, I wrote up the inquest with the girl's words in front of me; I left out "bagpipes", and I left out the handkerchief, because it made Mr Pike look kind and I was not certain he was, and I left out the bottle, because I could not find a way to put it in that did not have me standing there with my watch. I took the sheets through to Mr Drage at half past six. He read them, wrote one line in the margin, and turned the page round to me.

"'Of Tanner's Row, Sallowfield,'" I read. "She lodges in Tanner's Row, sir, it's true, but I'm not sure we need to say so, with the temper the parish is in."

"Readers Like An Address, Tarlow, Always Have." Mr Drage was a heavy man who spoke as though everything he said came out set in large type with a rule beneath it. "Readers Like To Know Where The Thing Is. It Makes It Real For Them. We Do Not Say She Did It. We Say She Poured It, Which She Told A Coroner Herself, With Cheek."

"It was an ugly crowd on those steps, sir, and I should not like to think we had sent them anywhere in particular."

"Then They'll Buy The Paper To See Themselves In It. Besides Which, She Said It All In Open Court, So We Are Covered, Tarlow, Covered," said Mr Drage, and picked up the next man's copy. I said one sentence and gave up, and the sentence was about the crowd and not about the girl. The late edition came up from the press a little before nine, still warm, and I read my column standing in the reporters' room, and I was pleased with it.

The Fog PoisonerThe Halfpenny Courier, Saturday 9 December 1893, late edition, Police Intelligence.

HAS THE POISONER A LADY'S HAND? FOUR DEAD AND STILL NO ANSWER FOR THE PARISH.

The inquest on the widow Durrant was this day adjourned, the parish surgeon declaring that he could not, "in conscience", rule out an irritant poison. The young woman who serves at Mr Pike's establishment and who sold the fatal bottle, Miss D. Brough, of Tanner's Row, Sallowfield, answered the coroner with a pertness that drew hisses from the public.

A medical gentleman of the parish, who signs himself MEDICUS, writes to us that "persons of a congested habit, already weakened by the season, are the very persons whom a small and repeated dose would carry off without remark."

We ask, as the parish asks: who pours the bottles?

The Medicus letter had come by hand on the Thursday, in an envelope with no stamp on it, and Mr Drage and I had both been delighted with it: a man of science saying in good Latin what the crowd was saying in English. I took a bundle of the late edition back across the river, still damp, because I wanted to see it read in the parish it was about.

Hawser Street

The fog on the bridge was so thick that I left my cab halfway over and walked, and I was still looking for someone to read my column to when a constable went past at a run, and I followed him into Hawser Street.

Two constables stood on the door of Pike's shop. Through the window, between the great carboys of coloured water, I could see Mr Pike's card of testimonials, grateful letters pinned up in rows, and beyond it, in the doorway of the back room, the broad white waistcoat of Dr Lomax bending over something in a chair. At the shop door stood Inspector Henshaw, a long, stooping man with a grey moustache stained yellow in the middle, chewing a clove for a bad tooth.

"The Courier, yes, I've read you," he said, when I gave my name. "The chemist is dead, of prussic acid by the smell; it's bitter almonds from the counter to the back wall. The doctor puts it at about seven. Shop shut at half past six, and the girl left at half past six, by her own account, and nobody else had a key. That's all you'll have from me tonight, and you'll not stand in my doorway to have it."

Standing in Hawser Street with a dead man on the other side of the glass, I was glad. The poisoner had come for the chemist himself, or else the girl had done for her master, which was better; the Courier had been right all along, and Miss Xanthe Marbury owed me a sovereign.

It was a while before I understood that the light in the street was wrong; it came not from the shop but from the far end of Hawser Street, and it was torches of rag and tar over a crowd larger than the one on the vestry steps, moving away from us. In the middle of it a man stood on the tail of a coal cart that the people round him were pushing, reading in a great hoarse voice from a newspaper held up to the nearest torch, and I knew the words before I could make them out, because I had written them.

"Who pours the bottles?" he roared, and the crowd roared it back. The cart lurched round the corner into the lane that runs down to the tan-yards, which is Tanner's Row.

Henshaw had heard it too, and he threw his clove into the gutter, blew his whistle once, and turned on me. "Did your paper give them a number?"

"Only the street, Inspector," I said, "only the street."

"Then they'll try every door in it." He was already running. I ran after him with the bundle of my own columns still under my arm, and when we came round the corner behind the last of the torches, the man on the cart had got down at the first house in Tanner's Row and was beating on its door with the butt of his torch, shouting her name. Inside, someone was drawing back the bolt.
