Chapter one. Six Missed Calls.

She is breathing.

The ambulance is six minutes out, and I stay with him for all six.

Then I stay for four more, because he doesn't want to put the phone down.

"Is that them?" he keeps saying. "Is that them at the door?"

"That's them. Go and let them in. I'm still here."

My queue is empty. And I know what it is to be alone in a kitchen at night, listening for the door.

When he's gone, I take off my headset and walk out to my car.

The car park is the only place in the building with no camera over your shoulder. I checked. First week, I checked.

Engine on, for the heater. App open.

I look at the number.

It still says the number it says.

What am I expecting? That it'll have changed? That it'll be lower, and I'll finally have to admit something?

Every break, I look. Again at four in the morning, when I get home.

Rob thinks I'm checking the weather.

I should probably mention that now. Rob thinks a lot of things. Most of them, I told him.

Then I see the other thing, sitting at the top of the screen like it's been waiting for me.

Six missed calls: Mum.

My mother doesn't do missed calls. She leaves one voicemail, in full sentences, with a beginning, a middle and an end. Then she sends a text to say she's left it.

Six calls. No voicemail. No text.

Something has made my mother break her own rules.

Ten past four

I ring her back. It rings out.

Again. Same.

So I sit there with the heater roaring at my ankles and do the thing I tell callers never to do.

I imagine.

Mum is seventy-one. She lives alone in Malton, half an hour up the road from York, in a house full of books, with the heating on a timer she checks every night.

Stairs don't get her. She holds the rail every time, even in daylight.

Landline next. Eight rings, then her own recorded voice.

"This is Ruth Ellery. Please leave a message after the tone, speaking clearly."

"Mum, it's me. You rang. Ring me back." I stop. "Speaking clearly."

There's more I could say. I don't. I never leave anything on a tape that I'd mind someone else hearing.

Back inside, then. I'm a professional. The alternative is sitting in a dark car park, writing my mother's obituary in my head.

Dev is at the supervisor's desk, the screens lighting one side of his face.

He looks at me over his glasses. One second, maybe less. Long enough to read something. I'd love to know what.

"Go," he says.

"I've got two hours left."

"And I've got eleven people on the floor and you've got a face like that. Go."

"It's probably nothing. She's probably dropped her phone in the sink."

"Then you'll have a nice drive. Text me when you know she's all right. Not if. When."

"I'm fine, Dev."

"I didn't ask if you were fine. Twenty-six years on this floor, Tess. I know what a bad night looks like." He nods at me. "You came back in with your coat on. You haven't taken it off."

I look down. He's right.

On my way out I think about that. Dev never looks up from his screens. Not for anybody.

Tonight he was watching the door before I came through it.

The road

It's the end of January, which in Yorkshire means dark until nearly eight.

Frost has got into everything. The road out of the city is empty except for me and a lorry spreading salt on the ice. For a mile I sit behind its orange light, because it feels safer than overtaking.

There are headlights behind me from the ring road onwards. Two lengths back. When I slow, they slow.

At the Malton turn they go straight on.

Of course they do.

I'm good in an emergency.

I want you to know that about me first.

It's literally my job. Twelve years on nights, answering the phone to the worst moment of strangers' lives. When the new starters panic, they put them next to me.

I'm also very good at hearing a lie down a phone.

You get a feel for it. The pause before the answer. The detail that's a bit too neat.

"How much have you had to drink tonight?"

"Two glasses."

It's never two glasses.

So I'm telling you this so you'll understand. In all of what follows, I wasn't worried about myself. I was worried about my mother.

That's all.

Remember what I said about neat details.

Earlier tonight, at ten past one, Frank rang.

Frank is eighty-eight. He lives alone and rings most nights at about one. It's never an emergency, and we're supposed to keep those calls short. I never do.

"The robin's back," he told me. "On the bird table. Bold as you like."

"Did you give him anything?"

"Bit of cheese. He's got expensive tastes, that one."

"Like you, Frank."

"Like me." He laughed until he coughed. "Am I keeping you, Tess?"

"No," I said. "Nobody's waiting."

We talked about the robin for nine minutes.

Frank's phone bill is paid every month from my bank account. He doesn't know. He thinks his phone company has a special scheme for the over-eighty-fives.

I'm telling you that so you'll like me. I'm aware of that. Keep it in mind.

Malton

The kitchen light is on when I pull up.

If this were a call, I'd be typing it into the log by now. Kitchen light on, six a.m. Mum goes round the house at nine every night switching things off.

Front door. I try it before I knock. It opens.

I'd type that too. Unlocked. Occupant seventy-one, lives alone.

"Mum?"

Mum is sitting at the kitchen table with her coat on.

Not just on. Buttoned to the neck, good scarf, handbag on her knee with both hands on top of it. Like someone waiting for a bus.

Her phone is face down on the table. Next to it is her notebook, the one with the red pen clipped to the front. She was a primary headteacher for twenty-two years. She still writes everything in red, right down to the shopping list.

Every curtain is drawn. Even the little one over the sink, which never shuts properly. She's pinned it closed with a clothes peg.

"You didn't leave a message," I say.

"No."

"You always leave a message."

"I didn't know what to say." She says it to the table. "I've been trying to decide since half past ten."

I crouch by her chair. I don't think about it. It's what I'd tell a caller to do: get down beside them, get level.

"Are you hurt?"

"No."

"Is it your chest? Your arm? Any pain at all?"

"Don't be dramatic, Tess."

There she is.

I breathe out.

Then she says it, very precisely, the way she used to read the register. "I've done something very stupid."

I have never once heard my mother call herself stupid. She banned the word at her school.

"Okay," I say. "Tell me."

I've dropped my voice. I only notice afterwards. Like there's someone on the other side of that peg.

Sarah

She tells me in order, because she can't tell anything any other way.

"At eight o'clock my phone rang. It was the bank's number. I checked it against the back of my card while it was still ringing." She looks at me hard. "I'm not an idiot, Tess."

"I know you're not."

"It was the same number. A woman called Sarah. From the fraud team."

"What did she want?"

"She knew my name and my address. She knew which bank. She knew the last four numbers on my card." Mum's hands tighten on the bag. "She said criminals had got into my accounts. She said they were moving money that very evening, and I had to act quickly."

Four numbers. An address. I file it all, the way I would on shift. Where would a stranger get those?

"And you believed her."

It comes out too fast, and I hear it land.

"She was kind, Tess." Mum's chin comes up. "She had a lovely voice. She asked about my hip. She kept saying, take your time, Ruth, I'm not going anywhere."

There was a safe account, Sarah said. A new one, in Mum's name, set up specially. Move the money there tonight and the criminals couldn't touch it.

"How much?" I say.

"All of it."

"Mum."

"Sixty-one thousand pounds. In three goes, because there's a limit." She says it to the notebook. "Everything your father left. And everything I saved after."

My dad died when I was nineteen. A heart attack at work, on a Tuesday afternoon. No warning.

We nearly lost the house that year. I remember Mum at this same table with the bills in piles, doing sums in red. I remember her face when the sums didn't work.

She spent thirty years making sure she never had that face again.

That's what the sixty-one thousand was for.

"She said she'd ring back at ten to confirm it had all gone through," Mum says. "She didn't. So I rang the number she gave me. The fraud team direct."

"And?"

"It says it isn't in service."

Sixty-one thousand pounds, gone to a number that doesn't exist.

And somewhere, a woman with a lovely voice is having a very good night.

Work voice

Something happens to my voice when things go wrong.

Rob calls it my work voice.

Jonah, my stepdaughter, calls it my hostage voice.

She isn't wrong.

"Okay," I say now, in the work voice. "Where's your bank card?"

Out of her purse it comes, handed over without a word.

I ring the number on the back. My phone, not hers. Why? I couldn't tell you. My hands just do it.

Hold music. Violins. A recorded voice tells me my call is important.

On speaker, on the table between us, next to the red pen. We both stare at it.

Nineteen minutes.

Then a young man called Kieran, who sounds like he's been awake as long as I have.

Mum explains. Slowly, in full sentences, from eight o'clock onwards. He doesn't interrupt once.

When she's finished, he goes quiet.

"Okay," he says. "I'm really sorry, Mrs Ellery. Let's get this logged right now."

"Thank you, Kieran."

"I'll need to ask you a few questions first, to check it's you. Is that all right?"

"Perfectly all right."

I dialled him myself. I know I did. I still listen to Kieran the way I listen to callers, waiting for the pause.

It doesn't come. She answers every one without a pause either. He opens a fraud case and reads her a reference number. She writes it in red and underlines it twice.

"Someone from our specialist team will call you back today," Kieran says. "And Mrs Ellery? Please don't move any more money for anybody. Whoever they say they are."

"I shan't," says Mum. "There isn't any more."

The whole time, she doesn't look at me. Not once. She looks at the phone and her own hands, and the gap in the curtain where the dark is going grey.

I want to say it. How? How could you? You, of all people.

I don't say it, because I've got my own reasons for not saying it.

I'm not going to tell you them yet.

Instead I text Rob.

At Mum's. She's ok. Bit of a mess with her bank. Tell you later x

I read it back with my thumb over the button. She isn't okay. It isn't a bit of a mess.

I send it.

I'm out of practice.

No, I'm not.

Before

It's three in the morning and the car is the warmest room I've got.

The screen lights up my hands. I've typed the same message and deleted it four times. Like a teenager.

Callum: You're up late again

Me: I could say the same about you

Callum: I'm in a different time zone, what's your excuse?

Me: I don't sleep much

I don't tell him why. I don't tell him much at all. That's the nice thing about him.

He never asks the question you're dreading. He asks what I had for tea, and whether the frost has come yet. He sends me a photo of a harbour at sunset somewhere hot.

Which harbour, he never says. And I never ask.

For twenty minutes I'm a person nobody needs anything from.

Callum: Won't R mind you texting a strange man at 3am?

Me: R's asleep. He sleeps through anything.

Callum: Lucky R

I put the phone face down on the passenger seat and hold the wheel with both hands, as if I'm going somewhere.

I know how this looks.

It's only talking.

I pick the phone up again.

Daylight

By half past seven it's light, a thin white light that makes the kitchen look older than it is.

Mum takes her coat off at last and folds it over the back of the chair, sleeves in, the way she taught me when I was six.

Then she sits down and puts both hands flat on the red notebook, as if it might open on its own.

"The bank will sort it," I say. "That's what they're for. And you did everything right afterwards. You've got a reference number."

"You did everything right. I sat here in my coat."

"You rang me. Six times."

She almost smiles, and then it goes.

I go round the house checking windows. Back door, bolted. Landing window, shut. The catch on the bathroom one is loose, and I stand looking at it longer than I need to.

She watches me do all of it from the kitchen doorway. She doesn't ask why.

That's the wrong detail. My mother always asks why.

The question has been sitting in my head since I walked in.

"How did she know so much?" I say. "Your address. Your card. That's the bit I don't get. How did a stranger know all that about you?"

Mum turns her phone over. Looks at the blank screen. Turns it back.

And for the first time since I got here, she looks straight at me.

"That isn't the part that frightened me," she says.

I wait.

"She knew your name, Tess."
