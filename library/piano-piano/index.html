Piano, Piano

Chapter one. Yes.

The bus

She had been sick at the sixth bend. There was nowhere to do it except the tote bag on her lap, so she did it in there, quietly, among her tuning things. Then she sat with the bag clamped shut and the window open, breathing through her mouth. Her best tuning lever was in that bag. So was a roll of new felt she could not afford.

The snail woman held out a tissue, already used and folded small. Cass took it, because what else could she do? She said grazie, which she was sure of.

She got her phone out and read the email again, because she had never in her life been able to leave a thing alone.

Gentile Signora Pryor, it began. Graziella Pieroni, of the culture office of the town of Collevento, was very happy. She was very happy in every email, and Cass had pasted every one into a translation app and read the polite, slightly mad English it gave back. We are with great joy waiting for you. The piano is waiting also.

Under it was her own reply. She had typed it in English at two in the morning at the kitchen table of a flat that would not be hers by September, pasted it in, pasted the Italian back out and pressed send before she could read it twice. The Italian looked lovely. It had accents on it. It said that her Italian was fluent and she had lived in Florence.

She had been to Florence for one weekend, for a hen party. They had worn sashes. She had been sick then as well, in a bin by a church.

The Italian she actually had was the Italian printed over the top of sheet music. Piano, soft. Forte, loud. Lento, slow. Allegro, cheerful and quick. None of it was any use for buying bread.

And one sentence. She had typed it into the app on the plane and whispered it into the tray table until the man beside her put his headphones on. She whispered it again now, to the window. Sono molto felice di essere qui.

Her phone buzzed. The hospital in Bristol, reminding her about an appointment at the hearing clinic that she had cancelled in May. She deleted it. The bus swung round the last bend; there was the town, brown brick on a brown hill with a bell tower stuck in the top.

The piazza

It was smaller than the photographs and much hotter. The bus stopped in a square of bricks so old their corners had worn soft, and the doors folded open on a wall of heat and the smell of someone frying onions. Above her a bell was striking the hour, very slightly flat. Back when both her ears worked she would have heard nothing else all day.

She climbed down with her suitcase in one hand and the bag held well away from her in the other, and a young woman came out of the shade of the bar at a run.

"Signora Pryor? È lei? Sì?"

"Sì," said Cass.

"Benvenuta, benvenuta a Collevento."

This could only be Graziella: about thirty, small and quick, a lanyard round her neck and sunglasses shoved up into a great deal of hair. She started talking before she had finished running. Cass caught the shapes of words going by. Il viaggio. Il pullman. Something about the bends, with a hand swerving through the air. Then email, and perfetto, perfetto, and a squeeze of the arm.

Then Graziella reached for the bag.

"No," said Cass. "No, no, I've got it."

"Ma si figuri." Graziella had both hands on the straps. She was a woman who carried things for guests and the guest had no say in it. "Dia a me, dia a me, la porto io."

But the word for what was in it was not one Cass had, and Graziella was stronger than she looked. She swung the bag on to her bare shoulder and set off up a steep alley talking about the theatre. Halfway up she stopped. She sniffed. She lifted the flap with one finger, looked in, and lowered it again with great care.

"Oddio," she said quietly.

She said something else then, long and gentle, with a question on the end. Cass nodded and said sì. Graziella's face fell an inch. She picked the bag up again by one strap and carried it the rest of the way at arm's length.

Ettore

He was under the stage when he heard Graziella's voice in the lobby, and he lay on his back on the trolley in the dark with the hammer on his chest and said his sentence to the joists one more time.

How do you do. My name is Ettore. I am a carpenter.

It came from his niece's old school book, under a drawing of a man in a hat. Before that he had what was left of four months in a London hotel kitchen at twenty-three: yes, chef, behind, hot, sorry, service. It had been enough to tell the bar that London was wonderful. It had been enough, in May, when Rosanna looked down the council table at the three men bidding for the stage and asked which of them could work with the English specialist, to put up his hand.

"You're her voice, Ettore," Rosanna had said that morning, in the stalls, in the tone she used for the regional inspectors. "Everything goes through you."

The green door opened. He rolled himself out into the light.

The theatre

It was a horseshoe, three tiers of boxes curving up and round, painted milky coffee, the gold mostly flaked away, a family's name painted on the front of each in curly letters. The ceiling was a painted sky with a crack across it and a crowd of cherubs, one of whom had lost his face to a stain where the rain got in. The front of the stage was torn up, a long black hole with raw joists across it, and at the back, under a builder's sheet grey with dust, stood a shape she would have known anywhere.

Out of the hole, on a trolley, came a man. He sat up and got to his feet. He was a man of about forty, dusted pale all over, down to his eyelashes, with a grey plaster round one thumb.

Graziella was introducing him, Ettore Canestrari, with a great deal of hand, and then two words Cass did understand.

"Parla inglese. Ha vissuto a Londra."

He stepped forward, stood up very straight, and said, "How do you do. My name is Ettore. I am a carpenter."

He sounded like a man reading the news in 1950. In a minute he would hear that her Italian was a handful of words and a hen party.

"Hi," she said. "Cass. Sorry. I'm the piano."

Graziella was delighted. She said something to Ettore with inglese in it again, and spiegale, and waved at the hole in the stage.

Ettore looked at the hole for some time. Then he turned to Cass, pointed at it, and said with great seriousness, "Stage. Is behind."

"Behind schedule?"

"Yes." He seemed relieved, and then braver. "August."

"Right," said Cass. "Fine. So we'll be working round each other."

"Yes."

Graziella looked from one to the other, glowing. Then she turned to Cass, and her whole face changed into a question, and she began.

"Allora, Firenze. Quanto tempo ci ha vissuto? Le è piaciuta? Dove abitava, in centro?"

Firenze. Everything after it was a wall.

"Sì," said Cass.

Graziella waited. "Sì... cosa?"

The theatre was very quiet. A pigeon walked along the edge of the second tier. Cass looked at Graziella's expectant face, and then, because there was nowhere else, at Ettore, and he was looking at her with his eyebrows drawn together, as puzzled as Graziella.

Then he said, to Graziella, "È stanca. Il pullman." He drew the bends in the air with one finger, the bus going up and round, up and round. "Lasciala respirare."

Cass knew pullman. She clutched her stomach and nodded hard. Graziella's face cleared at once into pity, and she patted Cass and said several soft things, and then asked something else, lighter, with a smile, nodding sideways at Ettore. Cass caught his name, and inglese, and a hand rocking side to side, so-so.

"Perfetto," said Cass.

Behind Graziella's shoulder, Ettore let out a long breath through his nose, bent down, and picked up his hammer as though it weighed a great deal.

Graziella said something about il furgone and the suitcase, and kissed her on both cheeks, and went, holding the bag well out to one side.

"Suitcase," said Ettore, pointing after her, then at himself. "I have van."

"So did I," Cass said. "It's still got my name on the side, under his. He says it's too expensive to paint out."

He looked at her carefully. "Good van?"

"It was a very good van."

The piano

The way across the stage was along one of the new joists over the hole. It was narrower than her foot, and the dark underneath went down further than she liked. Behind her Ettore said, low, "Piano, piano."

Soft, soft. So she went across on tiptoe. Halfway she felt it dip as he stepped up behind her, and she understood, too late to stop doing it, that he had meant carefully and she was creeping across a plank like a cartoon burglar.

Then she was over, and she pulled the sheet off, and forgot him completely.

A grand, in dark wood with a pale stripe inlaid round the edge. The gold letters on the fallboard said London. The case was split down one side and the pigeons had been all over the lid. She lifted it anyway and held it up on her arm, because the prop had snapped off years ago. The strings were brown with rust. Down in the bass a mouse had nested in the hammer felts.

She played a quiet chord in the middle. It came out as a long groan, every string wrong in its own direction, and under the wrongness there was a sound, big and round, the sound it had been built to make.

She wedged the lid on the handle of her suitcase, found her spare lever, turned her head so that her right ear was to the strings, struck her fork on her knee, then the A, and listened for the beats.

Under the stage, the hammering started.

"Sorry," she called. "Ettore? Could I have some quiet? I need to listen."

His head came up out of the hole. "Yes," he said kindly, and went back down and hammered.

She tried again. She could hear the fork and she could hear the string, and she could not hear the beats between them, the little wobble that tells a tuner how far apart two notes are. The beats sat high, and since the spring the high part of the world had been draining out of her left ear. The tuning app was on her phone. She took the phone out, looked at the hole, and put it away again.

A saw, now, whining up and down, straight into her fillings. She stood up. She went to the edge of the hole and looked down at the top of his dusty head, and she reached for the only Italian she owned.

"Piano," she said. Then, because he hadn't looked up, "Pianissimo."

The saw stopped. He looked up at her, and his whole face opened. "Piano," he said, and put a dusty finger to his lips. He climbed out, fetched a block and a sheet of sandpaper, and sat down on the far side of the stage and began sanding a board by hand, without a sound.

She struck the A, and caught the beat, faintly, and brought it true. The next string was easier. She worked up through the middle of the keyboard with her head on one side, and the theatre was so quiet she could hear the sandpaper.

At the top of the treble it went. She struck the two strings of a high E together and heard one clean note in her right ear and nothing in the left, no wobble at all, and she did not know if that meant it was in tune or that she was deaf to it. She struck it again. She put her head down nearly on the strings.

The sanding had stopped. Ettore had come across the stage without a sound and was standing at the curve of the case. After a moment he laid his palm flat on the wood and shut his eyes.

"Again," he said. It wasn't a kitchen word. He must have picked it up in the last hour, from her muttering it at the strings.

She struck the E. His forefinger lifted off the wood and came down, lifted and came down, tap, tap, tap, quick and even, in time with a beat she could not hear.

She turned the pin a hair. The taps slowed. She turned it again, and they slowed further, long and lazy, and then his finger stayed down on the wood and didn't lift.

He opened his eyes and raised his eyebrows at her. Yes?

She put her right ear to it and struck the E once more, and it was true.

They did the next one the same way. He never tapped until he was sure. Twice he shook his head when she thought she had it, and she went back, and he was right. Then the next string, his eyes shut, her hand on the lever, the taps slowing under his finger towards nothing.

The mayor

Cass didn't hear the green door open at six. She was sitting on the piano stool with the lever forgotten in her lap, watching him plane a new prop for the lid. Each shaving came off the blade in one long curl. She had been watching for some time when Graziella said, from the aisle, "Ehi."

Graziella's eyebrows were right up in her hair. She had come in without the bag, and behind her came a tall woman in a linen suit with a sash across it in the colours of the Italian flag, and a man with a tray of small glasses.

"Buonasera, signora." The mayor came down the aisle with both hands out and made a speech. It lasted some time, and at the end she turned, smiling, to Ettore.

He stood up from his board. He wiped his hands on his trousers. He faced Cass.

"She say," he said, "welcome." He thought. "Very welcome. Is hot."

"Thank you," said Cass. They were all waiting. She folded her hands and said her sentence, slowly and very clearly. "Sono molto felice di essere qui."

The mayor put her hand on her heart. Graziella clapped. The man with the tray said "Brava" and poured. It had worked, and it went on working: the mayor answered her warmly, at length, and her voice went up into a question. Cass heard Ferragosto, which meant nothing, and tutto il paese, which meant nothing, and suonerà, which sounded like the start of a sonata.

She looked at Ettore. Graziella followed her look, and a small line came between her eyebrows.

Ettore walked over to the piano, laid his fingers on the closed fallboard and rippled them along it, playing nothing. He pointed at Cass. Then he spread his arms wide at the boxes, all three tiers, as if they were full.

Cass understood perfectly. They wanted to know if the old thing could be made to play again, in front of people.

"Sì," she said, and then to Ettore, with all her heart, "Tell them by August it'll sing."

"Yes, chef," said Ettore.

Graziella shrieked. The mayor kissed Cass on both cheeks. Glasses were handed round. Graziella cried "Il concerto, il concerto" and played an invisible piano in the air with great feeling, pointing at Cass, at Cass's hands, at the keys.

Ettore bent his head to Graziella's ear. "Ma sa suonare?"

Ettore

Graziella had laughed when he asked her whether the Englishwoman could actually play. Of course she could play; she had written so.

The rooms over his workshop had been Graziella's idea, with the comune paying. He had painted them himself the week before, white, twice. At eleven he was still downstairs at the bench with his niece's school book open at the page about the weather, and above him the boards creaked as the Englishwoman walked about.

She was talking to someone. He could hear her through the ceiling, a low voice, a pause, the same voice again. The same words.

Sono molto felice di essere qui.

A pause. Then again, slower, every syllable laid down separately.

He put the book down and listened.

She was practising.
