Small Numbers, Big Lies

Chapter one. Zero.

The Coldacre Courier is one room above a dentist on the High Street now. When he has somebody in the chair you can hear the drill through the carpet, and on a bad day you can hear the patient. I write the paper. Farid, who is twenty-six and had his own lanyard printed, sells the adverts, does the website and still thinks we have a future.

SAFE MILES MARKS A MILESTONE

10 years, zero deaths on the Greenway.

Coldacre's bypass has now gone a full decade without a single life lost. Since Safe Miles took over road safety across the county, run in partnership with Thornby Wray, deaths and serious injuries on our roads have fallen by 50%.

Leo Wray, chief executive of Thornby Wray, said: "Zero is the only number we have ever cared about."

I read it out loud, because I wanted somebody else to have heard it.

"Oh, that's nice," Farid said. "That's actually really nice. Thornby Wray have got the back cover till June, so if we did it as a positive news thing, page five, nice picture of Leo by the road, that's basically..."

He stopped typing. He had been in Coldacre long enough for somebody to tell him.

"Oh. No. Nick, sorry. Is that the same road? Obviously it's the same road, there's only one. Maybe they mean from a certain date. Councils love doing things from April, I did a whole module on it, there's a reason, it's to do with the tax year and..."

"Farid."

"Yeah. I'll stop."

My sister Becca died on the Greenway at 23:41 on 14 March, six years before that Tuesday, beside the service area. She was thirty-four. Her car left the road and no other car was involved, and that was all the police could tell us. I have had six years to make more of it than that and I haven't.

She worked for Safe Miles. Counting the county's crashes was her job. If anybody had sent her this release she would have rung them up. She'd have read it back to them in the voice she used on me when I put the bins out on the wrong day.

So I rang them.

"Safe Miles, media, Carys."

"Nick Sorrell, Coldacre Courier. It's about the Greenway release."

"Oh, brilliant, you're the first. Nobody rings any more, they just lift it, which, fine. Leo can do photos Thursday. He's got a hi-vis he likes."

"It says zero deaths in ten years."

"Zero, yeah. Isn't it great?"

"A woman died on the Greenway six years ago, by the services. We reported it."

I heard her keyboard. "I've got nothing on here for the Greenway. Are you sure it was the actual Greenway? People say Greenway for the whole..."

"She was my sister."

"Oh God. Oh no. I'm so sorry, I wouldn't have said isn't it great." She breathed. "The figures are independently verified, though. I'm meant to say that. They are, though."

"Who by?"

"The data unit."

"Where's the data unit?"

"Fourth floor." Then, smaller: "We're on third."

She said she would put a request in. She said it could take a few days, it was normally a few days. Then she said goodbye twice and put the phone down in the middle of the second one.

I had barely put mine down when it rang again.

"Mr Sorrell. Stuart Kell, Thornby Wray. I look after assurance on the Safe Miles contract, which means that when somebody is worried about one of our figures, I'm the person who rings them back. First, I'm sorry about Rebecca. I knew her a little. She was very well thought of in the building."

I hadn't said her name to Carys. I went back through the call in my head and I was nearly sure.

"Then the building knows your release is wrong," I said.

"It isn't wrong. That's why I wanted to reach you before you wrote anything down. Rebecca's accident was not on the Greenway."

"I've stood on the spot."

"You've stood where you were told. Those are different things, Mr Sorrell. It would be a painful way to find that out: in print, in the week of her anniversary, with her name in the paper again and a correction underneath it."

"Is this the bit where you say it'd be a shame if anything happened to the Courier? Because honestly, have a look at it."

"It's a courtesy. A threat would come on a solicitor's letterhead." He left a gap for me to laugh in. "How is Nina? Sixteen now, I think. Exams this summer."

My mouth went dry. I opened it and there was nothing in there, and he waited, quite kindly, to see whether anything would come.

"Give her my best," he said.

Farid was standing up. "Who was that? You've gone a really bad colour."

I rang Nina. It went to her voicemail: "It's Nina, don't leave a message, I literally never listen to them." I texted her instead, you ok, and the answer came while I was still holding the phone. ?? im in chemistry. Then: ur being weird. Then: bring my charger back.

When I put the phone down my hand had left a wet print on the back of it.

The Courier keeps every issue it has ever printed, bound by the year in green cloth volumes that need two hands. I lifted down the one from six years back. It was on page three, "Woman dies in Greenway crash", and I hadn't written it, because the editor we had then took it off my desk and did it himself. Rebecca Sorrell, 34, of Larch Close. Near the service area. Police not looking for anyone else.

There was a photograph of her from a work do, with half of somebody's arm cropped off the edge, and she was laughing at the arm. I put my hand over her face before I knew I was going to, and then I couldn't take it off.

Farid came and stood behind me. "She looks really nice," he said.

I couldn't do anything with that. I shut the book on it and took my car keys off the desk. He was still telling me the budget had to be at the printers by four when the door closed.

The barrier

You can't stop on the Greenway. You pull into the services, leave the car by the lorry park and walk back along the verge to the bend. The traffic goes past your shoulder at seventy and every lorry drags at your coat.

There were already flowers on the barrier.

White chrysanthemums, the cheap ones from a bucket, fixed on with green garden wire that somebody had twisted tight with pliers. There was rain in the folds of the cellophane and no card. Every year somebody gets here before me. I had always decided it was a woman from her office, on her way in, and been grateful to her.

It was 10 March. I wasn't due for four days.

Somebody had put flowers on my sister in the week a press release took her off this road, and I knew who. It was them. It was Thornby Wray, tidying.

I pulled them off. The wire wouldn't come and I wrenched at it until the barrier rang, and the stems snapped and stayed in the twist, so I tore at the heads instead and threw what I had at the road. Most of it blew back at me. A lorry went by with its horn going. A man in a van slowed right down to have a look. And then I was on my knees on the wet grass, picking white petals off my trousers and putting them in my coat pocket because I couldn't leave them there either. I was crying in the way that makes a noise.

"I want you back," I said to the barrier. "Becs, I want you back. I can't do this on my own."

When I stood up I saw the car.

It was a grey estate, parked nose-out at the far end of the lorry park with its engine running and a dog guard in the back. A man sat at the wheel. He wasn't on a phone, and he had watched all of it.

I got my phone up and took his number plate, and the car moved.

It came down the access road at walking pace and stopped on the verge with its wing mirror level with my elbow. The driver got out and came round the bonnet without hurrying. He was fifty-something, grey hair cut close, a good navy coat. He was in front of me before I had worked out that I ought to move, and he took the phone out of my hand.

I let him.

He thumbed through it with the screen light on his chin. He smelled of the hand gel they have in hospitals. He deleted the picture, and then the one before it, which was Nina in a bin bag at Halloween, and he looked at that for a while before he deleted it too. Then he opened my coat pocket, found the petals, and pushed the phone in on top of them and patted it flat.

He didn't say a word. He got back in, pulled out onto the Greenway and went. I was standing with the barrier hard against the backs of my legs, and I couldn't have told you the first letter of his plate.

The woman on the till at the services was eating a Scotch egg out of its wrapper.

"Flowers? Last night, about six. Man in a suit, little blue car. He sat in it after with the light on, ages, I nearly went out to him, only I'm on my own Tuesdays and you hear things. Not your mate in the grey. Your mate in the grey's been in twice for coffee and never drinks it."

"Blue," I said.

"Little blue thing. Why, what's he done?"

I didn't know. I had his flowers in my pocket.

My phone went against them. It was Nina, ringing, which she doesn't.

"Nick." Her voice was low. "There's a man at the door. He says he's from Mum's work and you sent him. Did you send him?"

"No. Don't open it."

"I'm not thick, I've got the chain on. I'm talking to him through the gap with my foot against it like a..." She stopped. "He can hear me."

Then, louder, in a voice that wasn't hers: "He's really nice, actually."

She hung up. I ran across the forecourt saying her name into a phone with nobody on it.

Larch Close

I live in my sister's house. I moved in the week she died to look after Nina. She was ten, and she wouldn't come out from under the dining table for two days, and ate her tea under there off a tray.

I did the four miles in a time I'm not going to write down. The street was empty and there was no grey car in it. Nina had the door open on the chain before I was out of mine.

"I'm fine. Stop doing your face."

"What face?"

"The hospital one." She took the chain off and let me in, and let me hold on to her for about three seconds. "Okay. Okay, you smell of petrol station." She got out from under my arm.

"What did he want?"

"Mum's stuff. Did she leave a laptop here. Memory sticks. Papers from work. He said you'd rung his office about her things and he was passing so he'd save you the trip. He kept saying my name. Nina this, Nina that, like a dentist."

"What did you say to him?"

"I said my uncle doesn't send people round, he goes weird and quiet for a week and then does it himself, badly."

"Christ, Nina."

"What? It was that or cry on him, and he'd have liked that." She held out a card. "He posted it through the gap. Like I was a letterbox."

It was thick cream card: Stuart Kell, Assurance Director, Thornby Wray.

"What did he look like?"

"Old. Like, fifty. Grey hair, nice coat. Smelled like a hospital." She thought. "Grey car."

I was more frightened than I had been at the barrier. At the barrier it had only been me.

I put the chain back on. Then I went through the house and locked the back door and the kitchen window and the side gate, and Nina followed me from room to room with her arms folded and watched.

"What did Mum have that they want?"

"Nothing. It's a mix-up at her old office."

"You just locked the side gate. We don't lock the side gate. The bins live out there."

"Then the bins are safe."

"You're such a bad liar. You do a thing with your ear."

"I don't do a thing with my ear."

"You're doing it now."

I took my hand off my ear. "Why did you even go to the door? You never go to the door. You let the Amazon man leave things in the hedge."

"Because it's my house," she said.

She went upstairs, and I stood in the hall with my mouth open on the next thing I'd been going to say, and her door didn't slam. I'd have preferred it to.

I took the card out to the back step and rang the mobile number on it. He answered on the first ring.

"Mr Sorrell. I thought I might hear from you."

"Stay away from my house."

"I was never in your house. Your niece was very sensible about the chain, and you should tell her so from me. I'd keep it on, in your position. I'd write up the council budget. I'd take some nice flowers out there on Saturday, since the others seem to have gone." His voice was as warm as it had been that morning. "Has she really got nothing of her mother's from work? Nothing in the loft?"

I hung up. My hand was shaking so much that I put the phone down on the step rather than try for my pocket. I sat beside it until the security light went off and left me in the dark.

23:41

Nina went to bed at ten and left her door open. I sat in the kitchen with the lights off and watched the street until my phone buzzed on the table.

You have 1 saved message. This message will be deleted in 24 hours. To keep it, press 2.

It comes round every thirty days. I pressed 2 without listening, the same as every other time, and opened the laptop before I could think about that.

The county keeps a crash map that anyone can look at. Every crash on a public road where somebody was hurt gets a dot, and the dots where somebody died are black. I had never opened it. I had never wanted to see my sister as a dot.

I set the dates to that March and dragged the map across to Coldacre. The Greenway ran round the east of the town in a clean grey line. I made the map bigger until the service area filled the screen, the pumps drawn as little squares and the bend where the barrier is. There was no dot.

Kell had said it wasn't on the Greenway. So I pulled back and looked for a black dot on any road within five miles, on any night that month.

There wasn't one. They hadn't moved her to another road. They had taken her off the roads altogether.

Outside, a car door shut.

I was at the window before I knew I had stood up. The street was parked cars and wet hedges and nothing moving. I stood with my hand flat on the glass and my heart going so hard I could hear it in my ears. I was still standing there when the laptop pinged behind me.

It was an email to my work address from a sender that was only a string of digits. It had no subject and no message, only one attachment: a photograph of somebody's screen, taken in a hurry and at an angle, showing a single row cut out of a much bigger table.

The clock in the corner of my own screen said 23:41. I watched it go to 23:42.

Date: 14 March, 23:41.

Casualties: 1, fatal.

Location: Greenway Services, access road (private land).

Status: Excluded, not a public road.

Last amended: 17 March, 09:02.

Amended by: SORRELL, REBECCA.

The access road is the lane the grey car had come down that afternoon. It is forty yards long, perhaps less. I had been tying my flowers to the wrong side of the barrier for six years.

Then I got to the last two lines, and I read them until the words stopped meaning anything.

It was Becca's login. She never let anybody use it. She wouldn't even give me her Netflix password.

She died on 14 March.

According to the file, my sister had been dead for three days when she logged in.
