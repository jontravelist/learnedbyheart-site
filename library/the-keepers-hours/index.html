The woman who came to Pennick in the October of my last year was sick over the side of Merv Ostrander's water taxi before he had a line on the landing.

She went on being sick while I stood above her on the wet concrete with the boathook. When she had finished she wiped her mouth on the sleeve of a work jacket that was far too big for her and looked up at me. "Don't stand there like a funeral," she said. "Grab the cooler."

The Keeper's Hours

Chapter one. Unauthorized Landing.

The landing

Merv had not radioed ahead, which was not like him, and I learned later that she had asked him not to. Visitors need written approval from the district to land on a federal lightstation, and since the newspapers had taken up the destaffing we had been sent a good many people without it. There had been a church group from Victoria with a banner, and a man from a radio station who wanted me to sound the fog alarm for him in clear weather.

So when she had her breath back I gave her what I had given the others. I told her the decision about the lights belonged to the Coast Guard, that keepers were federal employees and could not comment, and that any concerns should be put in writing to the district office, whose address I would be glad to give her. I said I was sorry she'd had a rough crossing. I said Merv would take her back as soon as she felt able.

She held on to the ladder and looked at me for some time. She was a short, broad woman of about fifty, with dyed hair growing out grey at the parting, and there was sick down the front of the jacket.

"Concerns," she said. "Merv, did you hear that? I gotta put my concerns in writing." She laughed, and it turned into a cough, and she spat into the sea. "I'm not from any committee, mister, I don't give a shit about your light. Honest to God, they can switch it off tomorrow and stick it in a museum for all I, no, sorry, that's rude, you're obviously a very, you've got a very proper sort of a face. I'm Donna Kowalchuk. I'm Darcy's mom. Darcy off the Cassie Dee. The one that came up here."

I knew the name. I had typed it on the report to the district, and on the statement for the police, and I had spelled it right both times without needing to check.

"I'm sorry for your loss, Mrs. Kowalchuk," I said.

"Yeah, everybody says that. The kid at the gas station in Port Alberni said that, and he sold me the wrong size propane." She let go of the ladder with one hand and pointed at the boat behind her, at a blue cooler, a tent in a bag, a box of groceries gone soft in the rain and a folding lawn chair with one of its plastic straps hanging loose. "That's all mine. I'm not getting back in that thing. I've been sick since the harbour, I've been sick on Merv's shoes, haven't I, Merv, and I'm not doing it twice in one day, I'll die, and then you'll have two of us."

Merv, in the wheelhouse, said something to his own hands that I didn't catch.

I told her again, more slowly, that I was not able to let her stay. I believed she had come to blame me, and I thought she had every right to. A keeper who cannot stand and hear a thing out has no business on a rock, and I had decided on the way down the path that whatever she said I would stand there and hear it.

Kevin Nadeau came down the path at a run, in gumboots and no coat. He had seen the boat from the engine room. Since the letter came in September, Kevin had been looking for something to fight, and he had mostly been fighting me.

"Who's this? Hi, hey, who's, is this the newspaper, Lyall? Because I'll talk to the paper. I'll tell them plenty, is what I'm saying. I'll tell them what Ottawa can..."

"This is Mrs. Kowalchuk," I said. "She was just going."

"I'm Darcy's mom," she said, to him this time, and she said it differently, as though it were a password she'd been told would work on the second man if it didn't work on the first.

It did. Kevin's face went slack and then went red, and he jumped down onto the boat and had the cooler on his shoulder before I could say anything. He was a big man, and he liked to be seen to lift things. "You'll have the end house," he said. "Nobody's in it since they cut us to two keepers, and there's mice, but there's a bed."

"That house is Coast Guard property, Kevin."

"Everything here is Coast Guard property, Lyall. I'm Coast Guard property. You're Coast Guard property till October and then they're going to sell you for scrap, is what I'm saying. She can have my bed if she wants it, Shelley won't care, Shelley'll be thrilled."

I let him take the cooler up. I did not give permission, and I want that understood. I simply could not see, standing there, how to stop it without a scene, and I have always disliked a scene more than almost anything. Mrs. Kowalchuk came up the ladder on her hands and knees and stood on the landing with her legs apart and her eyes shut, swaying.

"Thursday with the mail," Merv said from the wheelhouse, "if she wants."

He backed out of the gut without looking at either of us.

The Nadeaus' kitchen

I did the one-forty weather and the four-forty. In between I wrote up the landing in the log as "unauthorized visitor, Mrs. Donna Kowalchuk, landed by water taxi", and radioed the district, where the person who handled visitors had gone home with a cold. At six, Shelley Nadeau came across in the rain in her husband's coat, which was the only one that still closed over her stomach, and told me supper was moose stew and I was having some.

"Kevin's going to talk about the letter," she said. "I'm just telling you, I'm not stopping him."

The Nadeaus' kitchen was always too hot, because Kevin kept the oil stove turned up as high as it would go, and it smelled of wet dog although they did not have a dog. Mrs. Kowalchuk had changed into a sweatshirt from a logging camp up the Nimpkish valley and had put a jar of dill pickles in the middle of the table. She told us she cooked for a logging crew in a trailer kitchen and could certainly cook for herself on a rock, and she did not want anybody going to any trouble.

Shelley ate half the pickles before the stew was served. "These are the good kind, with the garlic, I can't get these anywhere," she said. "Did you make these?"

"I made them. I make them by the case. The men put them on everything, on porridge."

"On porridge." Shelley laughed far too loudly, with her mouth full, and could not stop, and had to put her head down on the table. Shelley laughed at things that weren't funny and kept a perfectly straight face at things that were, and I was never sure which was which.

Kevin talked about the letter. He had brought it to the table. He kept it in an envelope in his shirt pocket and took it out at meals.

"Registered," he said. "They sent it registered, so Vic had to bring it out special on the helicopter and stand there while I signed for it. I had to sign a piece of paper to say I got told. That's how Ottawa thinks, is what I'm saying. They're so scared somebody's going to say, oh, I never got the letter, I never knew, so they make you sign for your own, for the, the thing where they tell you you're finished."

"You're not finished," Shelley said. "You're thirty-eight."

"I know how old I am, Shell."

"Then don't say finished."

"Lyall here thinks it's fine." He pointed his spoon at me. "Lyall thinks the Coast Guard knows best, don't you, Lyall, you're going to go quietly. You know what he said, when I showed him? He said, well, that isn't in our control, Kevin. Like it's the weather."

"It isn't in our control," I said.

"Lyall found your son," Kevin said to Mrs. Kowalchuk. He said it loudly, and he watched me while he said it. "Did you know that, in the north gut? He never told you that, did he. He never says anything."

Mrs. Kowalchuk put her fork down.

"The police said a keeper," she said. "They said a lightkeeper found him. I thought you'd be older." She looked at me across the pickle jar. "I don't know why I thought that. I thought you'd have a beard."

I said that I had made the report, yes.

She picked up her fork again, and then she started to talk, and I understood after a minute or two that she was talking so that nobody else could.

"He stole a bus once. Did anybody tell you that? No, why would they. He was fourteen. The camp's got a crew bus, a big old yellow school bus, you know, they get them secondhand from the school board, and he takes it, this is at night, he takes it to drive a girl to a dance down the valley. He can't drive, he's never driven, he's watched the guys, is all, so he gets it going and he gets it about a mile, and he puts it in the ditch on the corner by the gravel pit, on its side, and walks the girl home, and leaves it there. And in the morning there's forty men stood in the yard in their spiked boots with their lunch buckets and no bus, and the boss is going crazy, he's out on the radio, he thinks it's been stolen by, I don't know who he thought, Communists, and I'm making eggs, and I look out the cookhouse window and here's Darcy walking up the road, covered in mud, eating a muffin. Eating one of my muffins. And he walks right in and says, Mom, technically I didn't steal it, because it never left the camp road, and I said, technically you're grounded till you're forty, you little shit, and he says..."

She was laughing, and Shelley was laughing with her. Then the laugh she was doing changed, partway through, into something else, and she pushed her chair back from the table and got halfway up, and her knees went, and she sat down hard on the floor between the chair and the stove.

I have heard a great deal of crying, on the radio and off it. This came from somewhere lower down, low and then high, as though something were being pulled out of her by the roots, and it did not stop for breath. She lay down on her side on the linoleum with her knees drawn up and her face against the floor, and she said, in between, "I want him back, I want him back, I want him back," and she hit the floor with the flat of her hand, and the dishes jumped on the table.

Shelley got down beside her, which was not easy for her at five months, and got an arm under her head. Kevin stood up with a dish towel in his hand and said, "Okay, okay, okay," over and over, and did not move. His face had gone white. I saw that he had not meant any of it, the letter or the north gut, and that he would have given a good deal to be anywhere else.

The clock over the stove said twenty-five to eight. The weather was due at twenty to.

I said that I had the weather. Shelley was talking to her and Kevin was saying okay, and I don't believe either of them heard. I took my coat from the hook and went across the yard in the rain to the radio room in my own house. I read the barometer, which was falling slowly, and wrote it down. I went out onto the porch and checked the wind on the anemometer dial, southeast twenty knots, gusting higher. I looked for the light on the outer reef, which I use for visibility, and it was there, so visibility was better than four miles. I estimated the sea at four feet and the swell at moderate from the west. I keyed the microphone at twenty to eight exactly and gave the report to the Coast Guard radio station in the voice I have used for every report since I was a young man. The operator said, "Thank you, Pennick," as he always did.

I could still hear her from the porch, across the yard.

I did not go back. I sat in the radio room with the light off and the receiver turned down low. On the desk was the paperback I read from most evenings, the private notebook of a Roman emperor, much underlined in pencil. I did not open it. After a long time Shelley came and stood in my doorway with her husband's coat held shut over her stomach and the rain dripping off her hair.

"You left," she said.

"The weather was due."

"I know what time the weather is, Lyall. I've lived here four years, I can do the weather, Kevin can do the weather. The fucking dog could do the weather if we had a dog." She stood there breathing. "She was on the floor."

I had nothing to say to that, and she went away again.

I was frightened of her. It took me a long while to be able to put that down in writing. I was more frightened of that woman on the linoleum than I have been of any sea I have seen from the gallery.

The north gut

I make a round of the station before I sleep: the lantern, the generator shed, the fog alarm, the landing. It was after eleven, and the rain had thinned to a drizzle, and when I came round the top of the island by the helipad I saw a flashlight down in the north gut, where nobody has any reason to go.

The north gut is a cleft in the rock on the exposed side of the island, steep and black and full of weed, and the sea brings things into it. Floats come in there, and lumber, and crab traps with the lines cut, and once a refrigerator.

She had carried the lawn chair down. She had a blanket round her and a bottle of rye whisky between her feet. She was smoking, with the cigarette cupped inside her hand against the drizzle, like a deckhand. The flashlight was wedged in a crack, pointing at the sea.

I climbed down. I am not young, and the weed was slick, and I came down the last part on my backside, which she watched without comment.

"Kevin showed me before, don't be mad at Kevin," she said. "He said it was about here. Is it here?"

I should have told her I couldn't say. Instead I looked at where she had put the chair, on the big flat rock at the bottom of the cleft, and I heard myself tell her that it was the next one up, the tilted one to her left, with the crack across it. I did not have to think about it.

She looked at me. Then she got up and picked up the chair and tried to set it on the tilted rock, and it would not stand. One leg kept sliding off into the crack. She tried it every way she could, and then she sat on it anyway, crooked, holding the rock with one foot. The loose strap gave under her, and she went partway through the seat and stayed there, folded, with her knees up by her chin and the bottle rolling away down the weed toward the water.

I went and got the bottle, because it seemed the only useful thing.

"Don't you dare help me," she said.

I didn't help her. She worked herself out of the chair on her own and sat on the bare rock instead. I gave her the bottle, and she drank from it and did not offer it to me.

"I didn't come to yell at you," she said. "I know you think I did. You've got a face on you like you're waiting to be hit. I was yelling at Kevin earlier about the boat, and the skipper, Ron Halvorsen, and I yelled at Ron's wife on the phone in August, and I yelled at the priest, and I yelled at my sister for bringing a lasagna. I yelled at a lasagna. I'm so angry I could spit all the time, I can't, it's like having the flu. But I didn't come for you. I just wanted to sit where he was. Nobody would let me see him, you know, at the funeral home. They said I shouldn't, after three weeks in the water. They said, remember him how he was. So I don't know what he looked like at the end. I keep making it up. I make it up worse every night."

The light went over us, a long white sweep across the top of the cleft, and in the dark between the sweeps there is only the sound of the water working in and out of the weed.

"You saw him," she said.

"I made the report."

"Don't do that. You came down here and he was here and you saw him." She put the bottle down very carefully on the rock. "Did he look scared? That's all I want to know. That's all I came for. Did my boy look scared?"

"No," I said.

I had not looked at his face.
