The Cold War in Aunt Rita's Kitchen

Chapter one. One Plate Too Many.

Berlin, February 1947

The transports came in on the far side of the river, in the Soviet sector, and Ilse walked there most afternoons now that the schools were shut for want of coal. Mama thought she was at the canal looking for firewood. Mama would have forbidden the station, and Mama would have been right. The platform stank of wet wool and of the white powder they dusted the men with against lice, and a girl on her own got shoved about by grown women who were all shouting names.

She held the photograph up anyway. It showed a man in rolled shirtsleeves outside a printing works, laughing at whoever had the camera, with a great deal of dark hair.

That was all she had of him, and she was ashamed of it. She could not remember her father's face. She had been six when he was last home, and when she tried to see him now she saw only the photograph, flat and shiny and no bigger than her hand. At supper she told Mama that of course she remembered Papa. She said it easily, and every time she said it she felt sick. What kind of daughter forgot?

The men today were worse than last week's. Their faces were puffed tight with water, so that the eyes had almost closed, and they walked on feet bound in strips of blanket. Some of the women found the one they wanted and screamed. Most of them did not, and they stood there with their cardboard signs while the men shuffled by with their heads down.

One man had stopped close to her. He had sat down on a wooden suitcase, right where people needed to walk, with his legs stuck out in front of him like a small child's, and he was staring up at her photograph. His mouth hung a little open and there was a sore at the corner of it. Even in that cold he smelled of something sweetish and rotten that made her swallow.

Go away, she thought. Please go away.

He lifted a hand towards the picture. She thought he meant to snatch it, because people snatched anything now, and she clutched it to her chest and pushed off through the coats until there were a dozen backs between them. She did not stop until she was out on the street. Then she ran for the canal to find some wood, so that there would be something to show for the afternoon.

The kitchen

Rita's kitchen was the one warm room left in the house, and the whole house knew it. All winter the neighbours had carried their pots up the stairs to borrow a corner of her stove, and they paid in what they had, a cup of barley or a candle end or a turn at the washing. The stairwell smelled of carbide lamps and boiled turnip. Inside the kitchen it smelled of people.

Half the window was real glass. The other half Rita had mended with old X-ray films from the hospital, soaked clean in the sink, and when the light was right somebody's ribs still showed faintly in the pane above the tap.

Supper was turnip soup thickened with the Kaminskis' barley, and bread that was a third potato peel and went sour by its second day. To drink there was the brown water Rita brewed from roasted acorns and called coffee, daring anyone at her table to call it anything else.

Rita was talking when Ilse came in, and she went on talking. She was a big, loud, tired woman of thirty-five. In the autumn she had traded her wedding ring for a sack of potatoes, and she had told the whole staircase so herself, before anyone could pity her behind her back.

"So I said to him, that is not soap, that is a piece of the riverbank with a stamp pressed in it, I could plant beans in that. And he says take it or leave it. And what am I to do, leave it? So now we wash with clay and I have no stockings. Ilse, shut that door. Is that the wood? That isn't wood, that's kindling for a doll's house. Lotte, look what she's brought. No, don't look, it will only make you cry. Where was I?"

"The soap," Mama said.

"The soap, yes. Well, that was all of it, the man's a thief."

Mama had been out two nights running at a birth that went badly. She sat at the table with her coat still on and her eyes half shut, unpicking an old sweater for the wool, because her hands did not know how to stop. She had not asked where Ilse had been. She was too tired to ask, and Ilse was glad of it and hated being glad.

Rita laid the table as she always did, with one more plate than there were people. She had begun it the week the telegram came about Uncle Karl and she would not be argued out of it. "For whoever is missing," she said, if anyone asked. After a while they had stopped asking.

Ilse had decided long ago that the plate was Papa's. Uncle Karl was dead and could not need one.

She looked at it now and saw the man on the suitcase, with his wet open mouth, and her stomach turned over. What if they all came back like that? What if hers did?

Then I don't want him.

The thought was there before she could stop it. She sat down and pushed her hands under her legs, and she felt wicked right through.

The landing

The knock came while Rita was ladling out the soup.

Mama went, because a knock after dark was usually for the midwife. Ilse heard the door drag across the boards. She heard a man's voice, very low, and after that a sound she had never heard her mother make, a kind of cough with a cry caught inside it.

Rita dropped the ladle into the pot and ran, and Ilse ran after her.

The smell reached her before she saw him. It was the man from the platform. He stood on the landing with the wooden suitcase at his feet, and Mama had hold of the front of his quilted jacket in both fists and was saying his name over and over into it. "Emil, Emil, oh, Emil." He did not put his arms round her. He stood with them hanging and peered over her head into the flat.

"Where is she?" he said. "Lotte. Where's the little one?"

"She's here. She's right here, Emil, she's in front of you."

His eyes came down to Ilse and rested on her without any sign of knowing her. He was looking for somebody who came up to his belt. She watched him work it out, and she saw that working it out hurt him, and she could not stand to be the thing that hurt.

"I knew you right away, Papa," she said. Her voice came out high and false.

"No," he said.

"Emil," said Mama.

"No. She didn't." He was breathing hard from the stairs. "You were at the station. You had my picture. You held it up beside my face and you looked for me in it, and I wasn't there. I watched you make up your mind. You pulled it away from me like I'd dirty it."

"I didn't know it was you."

"That's what I'm telling you."

He said it flatly, without any anger she could hear, and that was worse than shouting. He saw me. He was sitting right there and he saw me do it. She had clutched his own face away from him and run, and he had carried that up all those stairs to say to her before he would let anybody be glad.

"She is ten years old," Rita said. "Get inside and shut your mouth. You're frightening her."

"I sat in the tram shelter." He said it to Mama, and he had begun to shake. "I couldn't come up. I sat there till it got dark. I thought, let them keep the picture. They've managed all right with the picture."

Ilse's face burned. She wanted to say that she was sorry. More than that, she wanted him to pick up his suitcase and go back down the stairs and be missing again, so that she could go on waiting for the other one. It was a terrible thing to want about her own father, and she wanted it with her whole body.

The floor

He would not take off the jacket. He sat down in it at the head of the table, in front of the extra plate, and when Rita filled the plate he did not wait for the others or for a spoon. He picked it up in both hands and drank. Soup ran down his chin into his collar. He wiped the plate round with his fingers and sucked them clean, and he reached across and took the bread from in front of Ilse and pushed it inside his jacket.

"Slowly," Mama said. "Emil, slowly, your stomach won't hold it. I've seen it at the hospital. They come home and they eat too fast and it kills them."

"Is there more?"

"There's more, but you have to wait a little."

"Give me the more."

Rita filled the plate again with her mouth shut tight. It was her own share that went into it, and Ilse's. He drank that too, and then he sat very still while the sweat came out on his forehead, and he was sick. It went over the table and his knees and Rita's scrubbed floor.

What he did next Ilse would see for the rest of her life whenever she shut her eyes. He got down on his knees in the mess and began to pick out the barley.

"Don't," Mama said. "Emil, don't, leave it."

"It's good food. It's still good, you can wash it."

"Leave it!"

He had not cried at the door, or at the sight of his wife, but now he knelt on the kitchen floor and sobbed over a spoonful of barley while his swollen hands scraped at the boards. Mama had to get behind him and haul him up by the armpits like a drunk. Rita stood with the floorcloth in her fist and did not help. Ilse saw the disgust on her aunt's face, plain disgust, and it was a relief to her, because it meant she was not the only one.

She was frightened of him, and that was the plain truth of it. He was her father and she had prayed for him every night for four years, and now he was here he frightened her and turned her stomach, and she did not know how she was supposed to love him.

Mama washed him at the sink with the clay soap that would not lather. She cut the rags from his feet with her midwife's scissors. Ilse had been told to lie down on her mattress and face the wall, and she did not. His legs were thick as bolsters up to the knee and like broom handles above it, and when Mama pressed her thumb into his shin the dent stayed there.

She made herself keep looking. That is my father, she told herself, and she tried to make the words fit the body at the sink, the loose skin of his back and the ribs she could have counted from across the room, and they would not fit. Nothing in her rose to meet him. She lay rigid on the mattress and wondered whether there was something wrong with her heart.

"Does that hurt?"

"No. Nothing down there hurts. That's how you know it's bad."

Afterwards Mama went down to beg a shirt from the Kaminskis, since Uncle Karl's had all gone for food, and Rita carried the bucket to the yard. Ilse was left alone with him. He sat on the stool by the stove, wrapped in a blanket. He was much smaller without the jacket.

"You can look," he said. "I'd look."

She said nothing, because there was nothing she trusted herself to say.

"I was after a small one. The whole way on the train I had it planned, how I'd pick her up. I forgot you'd go on without me. That's stupid, isn't it? A child grows. Any fool knows a child grows. I had you with a ribbon. Did you ever have a ribbon?"

"No."

"No. I don't know whose ribbon that was, then."

"I forgot your face." It was out before she had decided to say it. "Not today. A long time ago. I only had the picture, and I made up the rest, and I told Mama I remembered."

He was quiet for so long that she thought she had made everything worse.

"Well," he said at last. "Well. I don't suppose I'd have kept it either, this face." He rubbed his hand over his shaved head, back and forth. "Put the picture in the stove."

"No."

"I don't want him in the house, looking at me."

"No, Papa."

She kept it inside her coat. She did not love the man on the stool, not yet, and she knew that she did not. But she was sorry for him, so sorry that it ached under her ribs, and she thought that might do to begin with. She got up from the mattress and went to sit on the coal box, which was nearer.

The key

By the time the others came back Rita had got hold of herself. She sat down with her sleeves pushed up and began to arrange his life.

"Tomorrow we register you. The ration office opens at eight, and with your trade they might give you a worker's card. You'll sleep in the back room. It has no window at all, but neither has half the street."

"Rita."

"And there's a printer near the church who has paper again, I know his wife, she owes me for..."

"Rita, I'm registered. They did it this afternoon."

From the pocket of his trousers he brought out a folded paper and a key on a loop of string, and he put them on the table.

"I had to report first. That was before the tram shelter. They've given me work, Lotte, at a printing works that sets a newspaper, and rooms over it that go with the job. Two rooms and a kitchen, with coal."

Mama did not touch the key. "Who has given you this?"

"The comrades."

"The Russians," Rita said.

"Germans."

"Russians, Emil! Who do you think owns the Germans over there? I was here. You weren't here. I was here in forty-five when your comrades came up those stairs, and Lotte was here, and you can ask your wife what..."

"Rita." Mama's voice was very quiet. "Not in front of Ilse."

"I know what they did," he said. "I know what we did to them first. I walked back through it, Rita. Village after village with no roofs, and you could walk for days."

"So now you'll print their newspaper for them."

"I'll print something true, for once in my life."

"Oh, true. He's been home three hours, he's been sick on my floor, and he knows what's true." She was on her feet. "I fed your wife. I fed your child. I sold Karl's coat and his boots and his stamp album and my own ring, and I laid a plate for you every night like an idiot, every single night, with the Kaminskis laughing at me. And now you walk in here and take them over the river to those people?"

"Rita, stop it," Mama said.

"I won't stop. Karl would never have done this to me."

"Karl is dead."

"Yes! Karl is dead! And look what came back instead!"

Ilse did not see her pick up the plate. She only saw it leave Rita's hand and heard it break in the sink.

The pieces slid down among the turnip peelings. It was the extra plate, the one that had been set out every night of that winter, and Ilse understood that her aunt had not been keeping it for a man who looked like this or talked like this. Neither had she. We wanted the other one, she thought. Both of us did.

They all stayed where they were. Papa had flinched down over the table with his arms across his head, and it took him a moment to straighten up again. Rita was crying with her mouth open and making no effort to hide it. Why did Mama not go to her? Mama always went to her.

"When?" Mama asked.

"The first of March."

Mama picked the key up off the table and put it in the pocket of her coat.

"Then go," Rita said. "Go on. Go and live with them."

Papa wiped his face with the heel of his hand. He sounded worn out, and gentle, and quite sure.

"It's eleven streets, Rita," he said. "It isn't another country."
