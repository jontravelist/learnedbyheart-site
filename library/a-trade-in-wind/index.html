Learned by Heart

The summer the board came to Highwater, everyone in Porthmerrow learned to read one number the way our grandparents had read the sky. Highwater was the great house on the cliff above the town, and the board up there showed that number all day long: the price of a share in Merrowlight, the company that was making us rich.

We checked it before we checked the weather. We said it to each other in the queue at the bakery, quietly, as if it were the name of someone who might overhear. A number up meant a fine day. A number down meant a squall, and squalls always passed.

I was thirty that June. I had come home with one suitcase of clothes and another full of the tools I use to mend old paper. I believed what everyone believed: that the town's luck had turned at last, and that this time it would stay.

A Trade in Wind

Chapter one. Midsummer at Highwater.

Porthmerrow is a small town at the end of a long road on the north coast of Cornwall, and for most of my life it had been the kind of place people leave. The fishing went first, then the mine on the hill, and after that even the Sunday bus stopped coming. Then Merrowlight came, and everything in the town changed its price: the cottages, the boats, the shares, all of it climbing week by week.

You could see Merrowlight's turbines from anywhere, twelve of them offshore, white and slow, turning in the fast water off the point. They made electricity out of the moving sea, which everyone had said could never be done cheaply, until an engineer did it in a shed on the old fish quay. Now the shed was a factory with glass walls, and the company was worth more, the newspapers said, than every boat and house in the county. In the low evening sun the blades flashed as they came round, one after another, and it was easy to believe that the money was rising up out of the sea itself, as silver comes up shining in a net. A fisherman's cottage with two damp bedrooms was four hundred thousand pounds.

The flats in the old sail loft were sold before they had roofs and sold again before the paint was dry. My brother Jory, who was twenty and had never in his life opened a letter from a bank, kept a chart on his phone, a thin bright line that climbed from left to right like a lark going up, and he showed it to strangers in the pub with the shy, shining pride of a boy showing the photograph of a girl he means to marry.

I walked along the front that morning with my own phone in my pocket, and every few minutes I touched it, as if to make sure it was still warm. I had fourteen thousand pounds in Merrowlight, every penny I had saved in eight years of mending other people's paper, meant for the deposit on a flat. In March I had put the whole of it into the shares in a single evening, on Jory's advice; and by June it was worth nearly twice what I had paid. I cannot say what that number did to me, except that it seemed to be made of light rather than of money, that it rose without effort or sound, and that when I looked at it I felt for a moment that I had been forgiven for something. Nobody knew, not my mother, not my oldest friend, and not the man at the credit union who would have looked at me, I was sure, with a kind of patient sorrow. I told myself I would tell them all when the number was high enough to be a happy story, and I believed it, lying awake at night while the sea went in and out below the town.

At the end of the front there is a café whose window is always fogged in the mornings, and through the fog you can see the shape of a woman setting out cups with every handle turned the same way, as she was taught in a hotel forty years ago. I never went in. On the first of every month I paid a sum of money into the account of a man I had never met, and I had never told anyone why; and walking past that window without turning my head, was as close as I let myself come to the reason.

The call

I was living in my old bedroom at my parents' house, because a room of my own in Porthmerrow now cost more each week than I earned. The call came at lunchtime, from a young man with a London voice and beautiful manners who said he was ringing from Ms Marchant's office at Highwater. "We've found some documents during the restoration," he said, "old ones, inside a wall, and Ms Marchant asked for the best paper conservator in the west of England. Three different people gave us your name." The museum I had worked for had closed its conservation studio in the spring, and I said I would come and look. "Wonderful," he said, "and tonight, if you can; there's a small gathering, and Ms Marchant would like to show you herself."

When I came downstairs my mother was at the kitchen table doing the crossword in pen, and when I told her where I was going she put the pen down. "Highwater," she said, "that's her house." Everybody in Porthmerrow said it like that, her house, her party, her boat, because nobody was sure where Juno Marchant had come from; some said London and some said Monaco, and some said she had simply risen one night out of the sea mist and the share price. What everybody knew was that she had paid four million pounds for the great grey house on the cliff and two more to put the glass back, and that she had put money into Merrowlight when it was still a shed and a drawing.

"It isn't a party, Mum," I said, "it's work; they've found something in a wall and they want someone to tell them whether it's worth keeping." She said of course it was, and looked out at the garden, where my father was rebuilding a stone wall that had not needed rebuilding. "Don't mention it to your dad," she said; but I didn't need to, because he had heard. Through the glass I watched him set down the stone he was holding, very carefully, as though it might break, and walk into his shed and close the door behind him.

My father had been a builder, and in his heart he still was, though nothing he built any more was for anyone to live in. Sixteen years before, at the end of the last boom, when I was fourteen, the work had stopped all at once, as a tide stops at the full and hangs there, glittering, before it turns. He had never really started again; he did not talk about that time, and we had long ago learned not to ask. I think now that a great deal of that summer would have gone differently if any of us in that house had been brave enough to ask each other one plain question.

Highwater

I walked up at nine that evening, because it was Midsummer's Eve and the light would last for hours, and because I did not want to arrive in a borrowed car. The cliff road was lined with parked cars. Women from the town walked ahead of me in pairs, carrying their shoes, in dresses that still had the creases from the shop; and somewhere above us music was playing, which the wind brought and took away again, so that the whole hill seemed to be breathing in and out.

Then the road turned, and there was the house. Highwater was built in 1720 by a merchant who had made a fortune very quickly, and it shows: it is too big for its cliff, square and serious, grey granite with tall windows in rows, a house built to be admired from the sea by people who would never be asked inside. But on the side that faces the water, where a sensible house would have put a wall, the merchant had built a long room made almost entirely of glass, and for most of three hundred years that room had been bricked up and blind. Now the glass had been made again, pane by pane, and the evening poured through it, so that from the road the room seemed to hold no furniture and no people at all, only sky, as if the house had been built around one piece of a summer to keep it from ending.

I went in with the crowd, and for a moment the Glass Room was so bright and so loud that I could see nothing at all; then I could see everything at once. There were long tables of food that nobody was eating, and a string quartet playing something quick and silvery that nobody was listening to, so that the music ran over the top of the talk like wind over water. The sea on three sides was turning from gold to pewter; and high on the wall at the far end of the room there was the board. It was an old departures board from a railway station, the kind with rows of small black flaps that turn over with a sound like rain arriving on a glass roof, and it no longer showed trains. It showed one word, MERROWLIGHT, in white capital letters, and beside the word a number.

While I watched, the board shivered; the flaps rattled and turned, all together, and the number changed, and it was higher than before. The room cheered, one rising sound like a wave breaking further up the sand than all the others, and then everyone went back to their conversations, and the quartet, which had never stopped, played on. I know now what I was looking at. That night I only thought it was the most beautiful room I had ever stood in, and I stood in it, with my hand on the phone in my pocket and the last of the sun on the glass, and was happier than I have been since.

"So you came after all," said a voice at my elbow. "I thought you'd stay at home with a book and be superior about it." It was Tamsin Rowe, my friend since we were eleven, who runs her father's boatyard. She wore a black dress and the expression of a guest at a wedding she had advised against. "I'm working," I told her, and she said, "So am I, Elin. I'm counting how many of these people have ordered boats from me that they can't pay for. Him, and him, and the man by the window with the new watch, who keeps checking it against the board." She took a glass from a passing tray and did not drink from it, and watched the room as a boatbuilder watches the weather.

Beyond her, by one of the tall windows, stood Cyril Daunt, who has run the auction rooms on the quay for fifty years. He was in a bow tie, studying a gilded mirror with his head a little on one side, and in the old glass behind him I could see the whole party repeated, smaller and dimmer and further away, as though he were already holding it up to the light to judge what it would fetch.

By the door to the terrace, already holding his jacket, was the man from the credit union. Theo Pascoe had grown up two streets away from me, worked in London, and come home to lend people money for washing machines at a fair rate from two rooms above the bakery. Most of the town thought him kind and a little dull; I had always noticed that his hands were steady.

"Are you leaving already, when it's hardly dark?" I said, and he said, "I always leave at ten, Elin, because I open at nine," and then he looked past me at the board, which was shuffling again, and said more quietly, "and I find it hard to watch." He looked at me a moment longer than he needed to, and said, "Good night, Elin. Don't let them sell you anything," and went out into the long blue evening. I felt, for no reason I could have named then, that I had been caught at something.

From the sea

At ten o'clock the lights in the Glass Room went down and the quartet stopped, and the whole crowd turned towards the sea as an audience turns towards a stage. Below us, coming round the point in the last of the dusk, was a small wooden boat. A man was rowing, and a woman sat in the stern in a pale dress, perfectly still, as if she were being brought to us from very far away and across a great deal of time. We watched the boat slide into the cove beneath the house, and the woman step out onto the rocks without help, gather her skirt in one hand and begin to climb the long stone steps up the cliff. It took her some time, and nobody minded. At last someone opened the tall glass doors at the top, and the night came in, smelling of salt and cut grass, and then she was standing in the doorway with the hem of her dress dark with seawater.

The lights came up, and everyone applauded, and Juno Marchant laughed as though she were as surprised as we were. She was not beautiful, exactly. She was very still in a room where everyone else was moving, and she carried herself with the care of a dancer who has been told that tonight the floor is made of glass; her hair was short and very fair, and her voice was low and clear and carried without being raised, like a cello heard from another room. She crossed the floor, stood beneath the board and touched the wall under it lightly with two fingers, like someone blessing a child, and as if it had been waiting for her the board rattled and the number rose.

"To the sea," she said, lifting a glass that someone had put into her hand, "which has waited a very long time for us to notice it, and is about to be thanked." The room said "To the sea" and drank. Outside, the first fireworks went up over the water and came down in long threads of gold, and the threads were caught and doubled in the glass all around us, so that for a moment we seemed to be standing inside a falling fortune. All that evening I never once saw her look at the board, though everyone else in the room looked at it all the time. I noticed it as I notice a tear along a fold, without knowing yet what it would cost to mend.

The letter-book

At eleven the young man with the beautiful manners led me through the house to the library, where the party came through the walls as a faint and endless murmur, like the sea heard inside a shell. On a long table under a lamp sat a tin box about the size of a family Bible, brown with rust, its lid beside it. I did not touch it. I bent over the box and breathed in damp, and old mortar, and leather, and under those, faint and sweet, the smell of very old paper, which is the smell of summers that ended before anyone now living was born. Inside lay a book bound in brown calfskin, swollen with water at one corner, so that its pages had opened slightly and stiffly, like a hand that has been closed for a very long time.

"It was in the wall," said a voice behind me, "when the builders took the bricks out of the Glass Room. Somebody had hidden it behind one of the old shutters, and bricked it up, and gone away." She had taken off her shoes and stood in the doorway in her stockings, holding two glasses of champagne, and she gave me one without asking whether I wanted it. "It's a letter-book," I said. "Merchants' families kept them; you copied every letter into the book before you sent it, so that you had a record of what you'd said, and to whom." She asked, "Can you read it?" in a voice that was suddenly not a hostess's voice at all.

I put on my gloves and lifted the cover with a thin steel spatula, very slowly. The first page came up with it, brown at the edges, the ink faded to the soft brown of a dead leaf held up to the sun; but the hand was good, young and careful and sloping, the hand of someone who had been made to practise. Inside the cover it said Hester Trewin, her book. Highwater, and on the first page, Highwater, the third of March, 1720. My dearest Kitty, Papa says we are to be rich. I read it aloud, and the room went very still around the words.

"Somebody else's summer," said Juno Marchant softly, and came and stood beside me, and I could smell the sea on her, and something expensive. "I want to know who Hester Trewin was, and why someone bricked her into my wall. And I want her in a glass case for the masquerade in August, so that everyone can see what people used to think rich meant. Can you do that for me?"

"I can make it safe," I said, "but reading it will take weeks, and some of it may never come back," and she said, "Then give me what comes back," and named a fee that was twice what I would have asked, when I would already have asked too much. "You'll work here, in a room upstairs with good north light, which my people tell me matters; and please don't thank me, because I like old things that turn out to be worth something."

That was when I saw her hand. She had set down her glass and rested her left hand on the table, and across the base of the thumb ran a scar, thin and white and curved like a fish hook. I knew it at once, with the dizziness of hearing a song from my childhood come suddenly through an open window: a rope swing over the quarry pool above the town; two girls of eleven; one of them holding the rope too tight and the other letting go too late. I had been the one holding the rope.

I looked up at her face and took it apart, piece by piece, as I lift dirt and old glue from a page; and underneath it, very faint, like the first writing on a sheet that has been scraped clean and written over, was a thin girl with a dark plait and a gap in her front teeth, who had sat beside me in every class for nine years and who said the name of our town the old way, with the long, slow vowel in the middle that only people born here use.

"You still do it," she said quietly. "You hold your breath when you touch old paper. You did it with your mum's recipe book, the one with the jam on every page, and you used to go dizzy, and I used to have to thump you on the back." She picked up her shoes, and at the door she turned and said, in a voice I had not heard for sixteen years, "Porthmerrow," with the long vowel in the middle, and then, "Hello, Elin." Somewhere in the house the board shuffled and the room cheered, and I understood that her name was not Juno Marchant at all. It was Julie Marsh, whose mother wiped tables in the café on the front; and the last time I had seen her we were fourteen, and I had crossed the road so that I would not have to say hello.

"Three people gave my office your name because I asked them to," she said, and her voice was a hostess's voice again, light and quick, as if she were telling me which wines to try. "And I know about the fourteen thousand. Your brother showed me his chart in the pub in April and told me all about his clever sister, who put in every penny she had and told nobody; Jory always did tell everybody everything. Keep it where it is, Elin. I want you in all the way up; and this time I want you to watch, right to the end, from my side of the road."

She went back to her party, and I stood alone in the library with a dead girl's letters under the lamp, and every penny I owned riding on a number that hung over Julie Marsh's head; and I did not know whether she had brought me up the hill to make me rich or to watch me lose it.
