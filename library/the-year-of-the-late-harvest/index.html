The Year of the Late Harvest

Chapter one. Prunings.

The burn pile

Rhona was burning her father's prunings when I went down to fetch her in for the food. We'd buried him that morning behind the hall at Carrow, in a wind off the ranges that tore the minister's notes out of his hand, and then the whole valley had driven back up to the house, and the women from the hall had found every plate in the kitchen and put a sausage roll on it.

She was down past the machinery shed in her black dress and her father's oilskin coat, with her good shoes off and a pair of gumboots on, feeding vine cuttings into an old drum with the top cut off. They were the cuttings from Dora's Block. Alec had been pruning there on the Wednesday morning he died, on his own, and he'd got halfway down a row and left the canes lying in the mud where they fell, and Rhona had gone up before the funeral and raked them up and brought them down in the back of the ute. They were wet and they wouldn't catch. Every bundle she pushed in sent out a thick yellow smoke that smelled of wet sacks and got into the back of your throat.

"Food's out," I said.

"Right. I'll just. These won't." She jabbed at the drum with a steel fence post. "He always said you had to."

She didn't say what you had to. Rhona hardly ever finished a sentence. She started them and then her mind went off ahead of her to the next job, and you were left standing there with the first half, and in all the Christmases I'd spent up there I'd learned to wait and I'd learned that mostly the end wasn't coming.

She'd been kind to me all day. She'd kept me a seat in the front pew with the family instead of leaving me to find one behind the cousins, and at the graveside she'd handed me a tissue before I knew I needed it. Now she took off the oilskin and held it out to me, because I was standing there in a thin black jacket I'd bought for a work dinner, with my arms wrapped round myself.

"Go on. You're."

"I'm all right."

"You're shaking. Go on."

I put it on. It was warm from her, and under the smoke it smelled of him, of diesel and the menthol lozenges he ate by the packet and swore were for his chest. I had to turn away and look at the fire for a while.

I loved that old man. I'd loved him more than I loved most of my own family, and I couldn't say so to his daughter, because as far as Rhona knew I'd barely said a word to him in fourteen years that wasn't about the weather or the drive up.

I thought it was grief that had softened her. I thought that standing at a grave takes the edges off things, and I remember wondering whether this was how it would be with Rhona and me from now on, now that the man we'd both been so careful around was gone. I stood in her father's coat beside her father's fire and I was nearly happy about it.

"Has Hamish said anything to you about," Rhona said. "He hasn't. Has he?"

"About what?"

She pushed another bundle of canes down into the drum and held them there with the post until they hissed.

"Doesn't matter. Go and get warm."

The front room

The house was stone, with a tin roof and a verandah on three sides, and every generation of Brigstocks had added a room in whatever they could afford, so you went from sandstone into weatherboard on the way to the toilet. The front room was full of people from the valley in their funeral clothes, standing with plates, and the heater was going and the windows had fogged over, and in the middle of it all, by the sideboard, my husband was opening his father's wine.

Hamish sells wine for a living. He sells it to restaurants and bottle shops all over Adelaide, and he's good at it, and when he's nervous he can't stop. He'd opened the good Shiraz and the bottles from the back of the cellar that Rhona kept for the wine show, and he was pouring them for people who would have been happy with a cup of tea.

He had Gerard Mott backed up against the piano. Gerard managed the big vineyard over the road for a company out of Melbourne that had bought up half the valley in the last ten years. He was a tall, careful man, and he was holding a plate of his wife's lemon slice with both hands.

"Now this is the oh-nine," Hamish was saying, "and you'll remember the oh-nine, Gerard, because of the heat, February, the week it was forty-four degrees four days running, and the whole valley picked in a panic, you lot included, no offence, and Dad didn't, Dad sat on his hands, he said leave them, and everybody thought he'd lost his mind, and this is why, this right here, because you get this sort of, when you've got it in the glass, there's a, I always say to the chefs it's like the difference between, oh, what is it, it's like when you've got the, you know."

He looked down into the glass. Gerard waited.

"Anyway," Hamish said. "It's very good."

"It's very good," Gerard agreed.

I stood by the door with a sausage roll. At the end of June, at our kitchen table in Adelaide, I'd told Hamish I was leaving him. He'd asked if there was someone else and there wasn't. He'd asked if it was something he'd done, and it wasn't, not exactly, and in the end we'd agreed that we wouldn't say anything to his family until we'd worked out how. Then his father died. So nobody in that room knew, and I was standing at a wake with tomato sauce running down my wrist, still his wife.

"Can I ask you something, though," Hamish said. "Just between us. Ballpark. For the whole place, with Dora's Block in it. Because you lot paid the Faulkners, what, for theirs, and theirs is nothing, theirs is flat, and you'd know, it's your job to know, and there's going to be probate, a valuer coming through, all that, and somebody in this family is going to have to be the one who's realistic about it, and it's not going to be Ro, is it. And it's not going to be Lachie."

"With respect, Hamish," said Gerard, "I don't think today."

"No, of course. Of course. Just roughly."

The back door went, and the cold came through the house, and the smell of smoke.

Lachie came in from the verandah in his suit trousers and a pair of football socks with no shoes, mud to the ankles. He was the youngest. He ran the vines, and he'd found his father in the row when Alec didn't come down for lunch. All day people had been holding his arm and asking, and all day he'd told them the same thing, that Dad looked peaceful, that he must have just lain down between the vines for a rest. I'd been picturing it all week, the old man on his own in the cold, and I was grateful to Lachie for that. It helped.

He was telling it again now, to the woman who'd done the flowers. "Honestly, he looked so peaceful. Like he was having a nap."

Paz was going round behind him with a tray. Paz had come from Chile for one vintage and stayed on as Rhona's assistant, and she spoke a flat, clear English she'd learned mostly from Rhona.

"No," Paz said to the flower woman. "He was on his face. There was mud in his mouth. Lachie turned him over and he was already cold. Sausage roll?"

The flower woman didn't take one.

"She means," Lachie said. "It was peaceful in the sense. Like, in his face. I've only had two of these." He was holding the glass with the dregs of what must have been his fifth. "Two and a bit."

Paz moved on with the tray. Lachie looked down at his socks, and the flower woman said she must find her husband, and went.

What Gwen said

Rhona was in the kitchen doorway. I don't know how long she'd been there. She was standing in her funeral tights, with a ladder up one leg from the knee and black from the fire on her hands, and she was looking at Hamish.

"Realistically," Hamish was saying to Gerard, "an old block like that, eighteen-nineties, own roots, you'd be looking at, I'd have thought, for the block alone."

"You can't sell what you," Rhona said.

The room went quiet from her end first, and Hamish was the last to hear it.

"Sorry, Ro?"

"It's not yours to. Gwen rang me on Monday." Her voice was very steady. "He changed it. In June. He went in to Gwen's office in June and he."

"Changed what?"

"It's in three. The place. It's me and Lachie and."

She looked at me. Everybody in the room looked where she was looking.

"And Shannon," she said.

Hamish laughed. It was the big laugh he does at work when a chef makes a joke that isn't funny, and it went on too long, and then it stopped.

"That's not funny, Ro."

"I'm not."

"I knew," Lachie said from the hall. We all turned round. "I didn't know. I mean I thought he might do something. I didn't know that. Nobody told me anything."

Hamish put the bottle down without looking, and it went over, and the oh-nine ran across the lace runner and onto the carpet, and he didn't pick it up.

"What did you do?" he said.

"I don't know anything about it."

"What did you do, Shan? Because I'm his son. I'm the only, I was carrying buckets in that block when I was seven years old, I got this wine into every decent restaurant in Adelaide when nobody in Adelaide had ever heard of Carrow, I drove it round in the boot of a hatchback for two years on my own petrol, I stood in kitchens at midnight waiting for some bloke in a hairnet to try it, and you, you don't even, she puts ice in it, Gerard. She puts ice in the Semillon. She puts ice in Dad's late harvest, the sweet one, the one he only makes when, I've seen her do it, she has it with lemonade in it at Christmas, in a tumbler, and I'm standing here in my own, in my own father's, and he's, and you were never even. When were you ever even up here?"

It was true about the ice. I like it cold, and the late harvest is so sweet it makes my teeth hurt unless I water it down, and Alec used to laugh at me for it and fill the glass up himself.

"I didn't know," I said again. That was true.

Every Sunday since March, I thought. And I didn't say it.

Gerard put the lemon slice down on top of the piano. "I'm so sorry," he said, to the room in general. "I'll. Margaret's in the car. I'm so sorry, everyone." He went, and the people from the hall began finding their coats, saying sorry to Rhona on the way past and not looking at me.

"Well?" Hamish said.

"I don't know, Hamish."

"You don't know."

"No."

He took another bottle off the sideboard and went out of the front door into the dark, and left it open.

Paz was on her knees with a tea towel, getting the wine out of the carpet. Rhona hadn't moved from the kitchen doorway.

"You were nice to me all day," I said. "Because you knew."

She wiped her hands down her dress. The fire black came off on it.

"I thought you'd. I thought he'd at least have told you. I didn't know what else to."

She went back into the kitchen and ran the tap. I stood there in her father's coat with sauce on my wrist, and I felt stupid, so stupid my face burned with it, and I wanted to go home, and I didn't know any more which home that was.

Dora's Block

I went after him because I was still his wife. I knew where he'd go.

Dora's Block is the oldest ground on the place, a slope of red-brown dirt above the creek, with the broken stone cottage at the top where Dora Brigstock lived on her own and planted these vines in 1892, after her husband died owing money to half the district. The vines aren't on wires like the rest of the vineyard. Each one stands on its own, a thick split trunk as wide as my thigh, bent over and knotted and bound with fencing wire where it's cracked, with the arms going up out of it like a fist that's opened. In July, pruned back, they look dead. The first winter Hamish brought me up I thought the whole slope had died and the family couldn't bear to pull it out.

The smoke from Rhona's drum was lying low between the rows, and the frost was starting. My good shoes went in to the ankle.

Alec had lost his licence in February. He'd had a small stroke, a bit of a wobble, he said, and the doctor wouldn't let him drive again until he'd passed a test with an examiner, and he'd failed the first one at the stop sign at the bottom of the Carrow road. He was ashamed. He didn't want his children knowing he'd failed anything. So he'd rung me, because I teach people to drive for a living, and because, he said, I was the only one of the lot of them who wouldn't make a fuss.

Every Sunday from March I'd driven up from Adelaide, two hours each way, and we'd gone round the vineyard tracks in his old ute doing hill starts and three-point turns at the ends of the rows. Hamish thought I was at Pilates and then lunch with the girls from work. I'd never been to a Pilates class in my life.

I found Hamish halfway down the row where his father had died. You could see the place. The mud was trodden into holes where the ambulance men had stood, and one of the last canes Alec cut was still hanging from a vine where it had caught.

Hamish was on his knees with the bottle. He'd been sick. It had gone down the front of his good shirt and onto the roots of the vine, red, and he was crying in a way I had never seen a grown man cry, with his mouth wide open and his nose running into it and a noise coming out of him like something being pulled out of a drain. I put my phone torch on him and he turned his face away from it.

"Turn it off."

I turned it off.

"He didn't even like you," he said in the dark. "Did you know that? First time I brought you up. He said she's a nice enough girl, Hame, but she won't stick it. That's what he said. She won't stick it up here." He wiped his face with the bottle hand and wine went over his shoulder. "Well, you don't have to now, do you. Stick it. You can sell your bit to Gerard and go back to Kalgoorlie with my dad's money and do whatever you. Buy a house. Buy a pool. You hate the heat. You always said you hated."

He lost it there and sat back on his heels.

Then he said, in a different voice, a small one, "Don't go, though. Shan. Not now. Please. Not now."

He reached up and got a handful of the oilskin, his father's coat, and held on to it.

I didn't help him up. I stood in the row with my hands in his father's pockets and let him hold the coat and cry, and I didn't kneel down, and I didn't touch his head, though I wanted to. I was ashamed of myself and I was more frightened than I've ever been, and I still didn't go to him. After a while he let go of the coat. After a while he stopped asking.

I left him there. I walked back up between the old vines with the frost coming down and the smoke in my hair and I could hear him behind me the whole way, and I didn't turn round.

The kitchen

Rhona was at the kitchen table with a mug of tea she wasn't drinking, and Lachie was asleep on the couch next door in his muddy socks. Paz was at the sink washing the good glasses and holding each one up to the light.

"Is he?" Rhona said.

"He's in the old block."

"Right."

I sat down across from her in the oilskin. I didn't take it off. I didn't want to give it back, and she didn't ask for it.

"When did he go in?" I said. "To Gwen."

"A Thursday. Second week of June." Rhona turned the mug round on the table. "She said he rang her first thing and wanted to come in that same morning, he was in a real hurry to, she said she'd never known him."

The Sunday before that Thursday we had parked at the end of the Grenache rows with the engine running and the heater on, after an hour of hill starts, and he'd asked me why I was so quiet, and I'd told him.

I told his father I was leaving before I told his son.

Out in the yard an engine turned over, coughed, and caught.

I knew that engine. I'd sat beside it every Sunday since March. Rhona was up before I was, and we got to the back door together in time to see the headlights come on inside the machinery shed and the ute come out of it backwards, much too fast, and clip the burn drum so that sparks went up all over the yard.

"He always left the keys in it," Rhona said. "Dad always. I told him and told him."

"Hamish drank one bottle and a half," Paz said, behind us, with a glass in her hand. "I counted."

The ute swung round and went off down the track with the lights jumping over the rows, down towards the Carrow road, where there's a stop sign at the bottom of the hill that you can't see in the dark until you're right on it, and past the sign the trucks come through to Adelaide all night with their high beams on and no reason to slow down for anybody.

I ran for my car keys.

I was in the front room with my hand in my jacket pocket when, from the bottom of the hill, the ute's horn started up, one long flat note, and it didn't stop.
