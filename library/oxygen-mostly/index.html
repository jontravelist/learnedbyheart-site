Oxygen, Mostly

Chapter one. The Last Bus.

It was 16:20 on a Thursday in July, which in northern Chile is winter. (Southern hemisphere, keep up.) The summit of Cerro Tolavi was doing what it does every afternoon: sitting 5,100 metres above the sea and being smug about it. The sky was that very dark blue you only get when half the atmosphere is underneath you, and the staff bus was idling in the car park with 6 annoyed people on it.

I was lying on my back under Receiver Three with a torch in my teeth, swearing at a valve.

I'm Teodora Marsh, everyone calls me Teddy, and I keep things cold for a living. A receiver is the part of a radio telescope that does the listening, and ours only work at about minus 269 degrees Celsius, just 4 degrees off the coldest temperature the universe allows. Isn't that wonderful? We get there with liquid nitrogen and liquid helium, and most of my job is persuading them to stay inside the pipes.

Cryogenic valves are very forgiving, right up until you lean on one with your elbow while reaching for a dropped spanner. Then they hiss, and the gauge on a receiver worth more than every house I've ever lived in starts to climb. Fix it now, or explain it on Monday?

Which would you have picked, honestly? I picked now, because I'm an idiot with a spanner and a lot of pride.

Aurelio leaned in the doorway. He's the safety officer and the bus driver, and he has a moustache that does most of his talking.

"Bus," he said.

"Two minutes."

"You said 2 minutes, 20 minutes ago."

"And I was wrong then, too. At least I'm consistent."

The moustache moved by about 2 millimetres. With Aurelio, that counts as a laugh.

He looked at the frost on the pipe. "What happened?"

"Sensor fault," I said. "Probably the cold."

It was not a sensor fault. It was my elbow.

Aurelio said nothing for a good 5 seconds. Then he went away, and a minute later I heard the bus engine stop.

Should that have bothered me? Yes! Did it? No, because I had a valve to fix and I was winning.

16:26

With the engine off, the bus heater was off too, and it was minus 8 degrees outside. So the 6 annoyed people came inside to be annoyed at me in person.

Hamid came first, holding up his phone with the stopwatch running. Hamid Rostami, our electrical engineer, measures everything in watts, and I mean EVERYTHING.

"That's 26 minutes," he said, and rubbed his forehead hard with his free hand.

"Headache?"

"A small one. Maybe 4 watts."

"Is that a lot?"

"For a headache? It's a fridge light. I'll survive."

Kino came next: Joaquín Ferrer, site paramedic, carrying the love of his life, a little plastic clip that goes on your finger and shows a number.

"Give me a finger," he said.

"I'm busy."

"You have 10. I only want to borrow 1."

"I don't let people measure me. It's a policy."

"Everybody lets me measure them in the end," said Kino cheerfully. "Some of them are lying down at the time."

"Is that meant to be reassuring?"

"It's meant to be funny. Reassuring costs extra."

Bastien went straight to the kitchen with his saucepan in a cloth bag. Bastien Roux is our chef, 20 years an army cook, and he brings his own saucepan up every day because our kitchen has, he says, no soul. (It has a microwave and a kettle. He may have a point.)

"Teddy!" he shouted down the corridor. "At base camp there is a lamb in a very low oven since 6 o'clock this morning. Since 6!"

Priya sat on the spare stool, reading. Dr Priya Lindqvist is an astronomer and weekend freediver, which means she swims down very deep on a single breath for fun. She is the calmest person I've ever met, and you'd find it maddening too.

"Take your time," she said. "Bad work in a hurry is still bad work."

Is there anything more annoying than that, said kindly, while you're upside down under a bench? There isn't, and believe me, I've checked.

The last one in was Abena. She's doing a PhD on the gas clouds where stars are born, and she runs 100-kilometre races through deserts, on purpose.

"Sorry," she said. "Would it help if I held the torch? Sorry, is that weird?"

"It's the best offer I've had all day," I said, and took it out of my teeth and gave it to her. "Mind the dribble."

The panel

By the door of the cryogenics lab, at head height, there's a grey box with a little screen. All 6 of them had walked past it without a glance. Well, who looks at a grey box?

"Abena," I said from under the bench. "What does the panel by the door say?"

She leaned back to look. "It says 20.9. Is that good?"

"That's exactly what it should say."

"But 20.9 what?"

"Hold the torch still, please."

I didn't explain. I tap that panel every time I walk in or out, and there's a worn patch in the paint about 1 centimetre across to prove it. Everybody thought it was a nervous habit, and I let them.

No, I'm not telling you about it yet either, sorry. It matters later, and you can be cross with me then.

Aurelio's radio crackled.

"Aurelio, it's Gordon. Where's my bus?"

Gordon Pike is the observatory director, down at base camp at 2,900 metres, and I have never once got away with anything while he was paying attention.

"Waiting," said Aurelio.

"Waiting for what?"

A pause. I could feel Aurelio looking at me through the wall.

"Engineering," he said.

"Well, tell engineering there's weather coming in from the west, and I want everyone off that summit before 5 o'clock. The forecast keeps changing, and I don't like it. Over."

"He doesn't like it," said Kino.

"He doesn't like anything," said Hamid. "He has a procedure for liking things."

16:38

I got the valve shut at 16:38, and the hiss stopped. The needle on the gauge hovered, had a little think, and began very slowly to sink back where it belonged. Do you know what that feels like? Best feeling in my whole job, payday included.

Hamid stopped his stopwatch. "And done at 38 minutes."

"Thank you, Hamid."

"I'm only saying the number."

"You're saying it in a tone."

"Numbers don't have tones, Teddy. That's what I like about them."

Then I did something I still can't fully explain. I unscrewed the brass handle from the top of the valve, the one I'd leaned on, and put it in my coat pocket. I told myself it was so nobody else could lean on it by accident before Monday.

Was anybody else going to lean on it? Come on, we both know who leans on things around here.

I stood up, banged my head on the bench and got a round of applause, so I bowed. What else can you do?

16:40

The lobby has a vending machine full of crisps and a front wall made entirely of glass (on a mountain, in an earthquake zone, but nobody asked me). Through it you could see the car park, the bus at the far end facing downhill, and the 12 white dishes tipped up at the sky. Beyond them was the edge.

Far below, on a clear evening, you can see the town of San Teobaldo, a 40-minute drive past base camp. After dark its lights sit small and orange at the bottom of the valley.

Every other Saturday, I drive down to San Teobaldo for the hot springs. That's what I tell everyone, and for now it's what I'm telling you.

That evening the lights were just coming on when a cloud slid over the valley and took them. Something small and white drifted past the glass.

"It's snowing!" said Abena, delighted. "It never snows up here."

"It does," said Aurelio. "Not often."

16:41

It started as a sound, a low rumble like a heavy truck going past. There are no trucks anywhere near the top of Cerro Tolavi.

Then the floor moved.

I've been through small earthquakes. You can't work in northern Chile for 6 years without a few, and in my experience they're over in about 10 seconds. This one hadn't read my experience. It was a long slow roll, the floor lifting and tilting under my boots, then rolling back, then lifting again. The glass wall boomed. The vending machine walked out from the wall on its corners.

"Earthquake," said Priya, in exactly the voice you'd use to say "Thursday". She got under the reception desk and pulled Abena in after her by the sleeve.

Kino steered Hamid under the desk by the collar. I ended up flat on the floor with my cheek on the doormat, thinking that this was a stupid place to put a building.

From the kitchen came the sound of every plate we owned leaving its shelf, and then Bastien, shouting in French.

For the first few seconds Kino, who grew up with earthquakes, was actually grinning at me from under the desk.

Then it kept going.

Hamid started counting out loud without knowing he was doing it. Somewhere around 20, Kino stopped grinning. Around 30, the lights went out, and the little emergency lights came on over the doors. Around 50, something big and metal went over outside with a long scraping crash. Hamid's voice had gone thin and high, and he got all the way to 81 before the floor stopped.

It rocked a few more times, each one smaller. Then the building just ticked and creaked around us.

Have you ever timed 81 seconds? Try it, sitting still. It's MUCH longer than you'd think. Now try it with the floor moving.

"Is everybody okay?" said Kino, in his paramedic voice. "Say your name."

We said our names. Bastien said his from the kitchen and added that he was not okay, because of the plates.

"How many plates?" Kino called.

"All of them!"

"Then you're fine. Plates don't count."

"They count to me!"

16:43

Aurelio got up first and went to the glass, and we followed him.

The car park was the wrong shape.

"That's not right," said Abena. "Is that right?"

"No," said Aurelio.

A slab at the far end, about the length of 2 buses, had broken away along a straight crack and slumped about 1 metre (my guess), tilting towards the drop.

The bus was on the slab. Our bus, sitting there at an angle, quite gently, in the falling snow.

Then it began to move, so slowly at first that I thought I was imagining it. It moved a centimetre, then another. Gravel ran out from under the slab, and I remember thinking, in a calm, nerdy, useless way, that I was watching friction lose an argument.

"Aurelio," said Hamid. "Your handbrake."

"Is on," said Aurelio.

It didn't matter, because the bus wasn't what was moving. The slab let go all at once, and the bus slid backwards, tipped and went over the edge with its headlamps pointing at the sky.

A second later we heard it, a long tearing roar, and then nothing.

For a while we just stood there. Then Kino said quietly, "We were all sitting on that 15 minutes ago."

"It was 16," said Hamid. On any other day somebody would have thrown a crisp at him.

We went out into the wind, testing every step.

Normally you can see the first 6 bends of the road from here. They weren't there. Where the road had been was a fresh scar of grey rock and red dust, still sliding. Near the bottom, very small, the bus lay on its roof.

I'd love to tell you I had a clever thought then, but all I thought was: I did that, my elbow did that. Which isn't even true (my elbow did not cause an earthquake), but try telling the inside of your own head.

"The pylon," said Hamid, pointing. The nearest power pylon was lying on its side. That had been the crash. On our roof, the radio mast was folded over in the middle, its top half hanging down the wall.

Aurelio unhooked the radio from his belt. "Base, summit."

Hiss.

He tried 5 more times, then gave up.

"Satellite phone," said Hamid. "Where's the satellite phone?"

Aurelio looked over the edge, down at the bus.

"Dashboard," he said.

"Then we walk," said Bastien. "It is downhill. Downhill is easy."

"It's 40 kilometres," said Hamid, "of a road that isn't there any more."

"Then we walk carefully."

"Nobody is walking anywhere," said Aurelio, and went back inside, and that was the end of that.

17:10

The control room has a long window over the dishes and a big whiteboard normally covered in schedules and somebody's drawing of a cat. It was already colder than an hour before.

Kino went round all of us with his clip, writing the numbers on his hand.

"Finger," he said when he got to me.

"I'm fine."

"I didn't ask if you were fine. I asked for a finger."

I kept my hands in my pockets. Kino looked at me, then at my pockets, and wrote something on his hand that wasn't a number.

Bastien was hugging his saucepan, which had a fresh dent in the side. Of everything in the building, that's what he'd rescued, and honestly? Respect.

"If we'd left at 16:20," said Hamid slowly, "we'd have had 21 minutes on the road before it hit. Aurelio does 35 kilometres an hour on the bends. So, 12 kilometres. Below the bends. Below all of that." He waved a hand at the window. "We'd be at base camp by now, complaining about the lamb."

I checked it, because I can't hear a sum and not check it. Call 21 minutes a third of an hour. A third of 35 is just under 12. Was he right? Of course he was. He's always right, and you have no idea how annoying that is.

"The lamb," said Bastien faintly.

Not one of them looked at me. Why would they? As far as they knew, a sensor had failed and then an earthquake had happened, which is just bad luck twice, isn't it? Hold that thought.

Then Aurelio looked at Hamid and me. "What do we have?" he said.

Not what had we lost: what did we have. That's an engineer's question, and I could have kissed him for it, moustache and all.

17:25

The plant room is down 6 steps at the back. Hamid went first with his phone torch. The diesel generator is supposed to start by itself when the mains goes. It hadn't. He looked inside for a long minute and put the cover back.

"Not tonight," he said. "Fuel line, maybe. Maybe worse. I need daylight."

So much for the generator, then.

That left the battery bank, a row of grey cabinets along the wall. It's there so the telescope's computers can shut down tidily in a power cut, and it was never meant to keep anybody alive.

When I'm frightened I make lists. When I'm really frightened I number them.

Step 1: what's in it? The label said 10 kilowatt-hours when full, and the display said 61%. (A kilowatt-hour is 1 kilowatt running for an hour, so think of a bucket of energy.) That's 6.1, and I rounded down, because rounding down is the only optimism I trust.

"Call it 6 kilowatt-hours," I said.

"About 6," Hamid agreed. "And nothing coming in."

Step 2: what's drinking it? Only the computers, still shutting down, and Hamid cut those off at the breaker.

Step 3: what do we want it for? Under Priya's desk there's a fan heater she uses for her feet. You can see where this is going, can't you? I could. My toes could.

"It's 2 kilowatts," said Hamid, before I could ask. "So, 6 kilowatt-hours at 2 kilowatts. That's 3 hours."

"And then?"

"And then no heater, and no battery for anything else, for however long this is."

"So we don't use it."

"So we don't use it yet." He found a roll of tape in his pocket and put a strip across the heater switch. I didn't argue, though I wanted to, very badly.

17:40

The cryogenics lab has an old dial thermometer, because I don't trust anything in that room that needs a battery. At 16:30 it had said 21 degrees. Now it said 18.

Step 4: how fast are we cooling? The heating went off at 16:41, so that's 3 degrees in about an hour. At that rate the 18 degrees we had left would last 6 hours, and the inside hits freezing around midnight. Is that right? No! Cooling doesn't run in a straight line. This was a GUESS with a ruler on it, but it was the only ruler I had.

Step 5: water. The pipes run inside the walls, and after midnight the walls would freeze too.

"Bastien," I said. "How much bottled water have we got?"

He counted it with great sorrow, like more plates. "We have 4 crates," he said. "With 6 bottles in each, 1.5 litres a bottle."

"That's 36 litres," said Hamid. "With 7 of us, a bit over 5 each."

"For how long?" said Abena.

I didn't answer, and neither did anyone else. There was a mountain of snow outside, and snow is water if you can melt it. But melting takes heat, heat takes power, and power was 3 hours of fan heater with tape on the switch. You see it? Every answer was standing on another problem.

Step 6: air. On my way out of the lab I tapped the panel by the door, and it still said 20.9. Big building, 7 people. I didn't even write air down. It was the one thing on that mountain we had plenty of. (Keep an eye on me here.)

I wiped the schedule and the cat off the whiteboard with my sleeve, found the one blue marker that still worked, and wrote in big letters at the top:

Problem 1:

Then I stood there with the marker in my hand, and nothing came out of it, or out of me.

What do you write first, when everything is first? Heat needed power. Water needed heat. Power needed Hamid and daylight. The road was gone and the radio was dead. Every time I picked one, the one underneath it pushed in front.

Behind me, Kino stood up.

"Write 'everything'," he said. "Then we can cross it off."

"That's cheating," said Hamid.

"It's medicine," said Kino.

Somebody laughed, a horrible little laugh, but it counted. I put my free hand in my coat pocket, closed my fingers round the brass valve handle and held on to it hard.

The bus had waited for me. Now it was 200 metres down the mountain, upside down, with our satellite phone in it.
