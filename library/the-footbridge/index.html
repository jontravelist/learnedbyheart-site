The wallet was brown calfskin, worn soft at the fold, and it was lying on my desk in the porters' lodge of St Ebba's College. I was the college's night porter, it was ten past three on a March morning, and I had just come in from locking the chapel.

In four years on that desk I had never found anything left on it that I had not watched someone leave. The quad was empty, and the air over the blotter smelled faintly of cedar; nobody on the staff could have afforded to smell of anything. There was a great deal of money inside, in new notes, and no card, no name, nothing but a ticket for a London train that had already left.

I took two hundred pounds, folded it into the side of my boot, and put the wallet in the lost-property drawer without writing it in the book.

The Tollan Room

Seven months later, in the October I was twenty-four, I walked in through the front gate of St Ebba's as a student. My gown still smelled of the shop, and Euan Docherty, the day porter, got to his feet for me.

"There she is," he said. "Onwards and upwards. The sky's the limit. I always said, didn't I always say."

"You never said."

"Well, I'm saying it now, so that's the same thing, near enough." He opened the book and wrote me in: MISS E. DOW, FRONT GATE, in the square capitals he used for everything, the gap in his teeth showing while he concentrated. "First time for everything, every cloud."

Upside down, out of a habit I did not have the right to any more, I read the rest of his day: coal for the Master's Lodging; a bicycle chained to the chapel railings; a scarf handed in; and in the early afternoon, MR F. MALLORY, OLD RANGE 4, ITEM CLAIMED. I took it for an umbrella. Fabian Mallory lost an umbrella a week and had never in my time come down to the lodge to ask for one,

On my way across the quad I stopped at the bin store, bent as if a buckle had come loose, and felt under the lip of the second bin for the boiler-room key. It was there, cold and gritty, where I had left it on every hard night since my first winter so that a man called Dougie Fyfe could sleep against the pipes instead of in a doorway down at the harbour; Euan knew, and had left it where it was, and I put it back and climbed the tower.

I had carried the key to that stair for four years, on a ring of forty, and I knew the room at the top better than anyone who had ever been invited into it. I knew it at two in the morning: the wax trodden into the boards that I scraped up with a palette knife; the north window whose latch had to be lifted and dropped before it caught; the decanter that was always empty and always put back with its stopper out. I knew that the tower key, T9, had been missing from its hook on the lodge board for most of the Lent term and had come back one morning in May without anyone signing for it. And I knew that on a night in April, the spring before, I had come up on my round and found the window wide, the candles guttering, a glass broken on the floor and one of the five chairs lying on its back with its legs in the air, and I had written it down, Tollan Rm: chair over, glass, window open, 02.40, and set the chair on its feet and told no one, because telling people things was not what I was paid for.

Now I climbed in daylight, with the rope bolted to the wall greasy under my palm and the slit windows full of grey water and gulls, and halfway up the cedar met me coming down. I stopped on the stair with my hand on the rope. Then I decided, standing there, that every boy in the Old Range must wear it, that it was bought by the bottle in Edinburgh and handed out at school, and I went on up.

The four of them were already there, and I knew every one of them by the hours they kept. Fabian Mallory never rang the night bell. He came in through the yard door of the Old Range, which was alarmed, and the alarm never went off, and in four years I had not found out why. Declan Rourke came in at five, by the kitchen yard, carrying his shoes, smelling of other people's soap, and gave me his name and college every single time as if I had stopped him in the street. Fergus Imrie I had found one January asleep against the bins with his camera buttoned inside his coat to keep it warm, and I had walked him to his staircase with his arm round my neck. Eugénie Aubrac had never once come in after ten, and I had never had to write her name in anything.

Declan let his chair down off two legs with a crack that rang round the stone. "Name and college, please," he said, in my voice, my night voice, flat and Aldercairn and bored, which I had not known anybody listened to. Then, in his own: "God, that's good, isn't it, I've been doing that for a fortnight, I did it at a party in the Chaplain's garden and a man from the Classics department nearly wept, and the thing is, it's the vowels, you sit on the vowels like they owe you money, and I said to Fergus, that's a woman who's read more than any of us, you can hear it in how bored she is, didn't I, Fergus, or something with the same shape..."

"Dow," Fabian said, from the window. "Sit. Not there."

I had gone towards the chair nearest the door. Fergus half stood, and his camera slid off his knee, and he caught it against his chest.

"That one's, it was Esther's, it's, sorry. It's yours. Obviously it's yours. I didn't mean, it's only a chair."

"It is a chair," Eugénie said, without lifting her eyes from her book.

I sat in it, and it was warm, from the sun or from the last person, and it was the one I had set back on its feet in April.

Fabian came away from the window then. I had held the lodge door for him once at four in the morning while he carried Fergus across the quad over one shoulder, and he had said, "Thank you, Elspeth," going past, though I had never told him my name and it was not on any badge. Close up he was lightly freckled and tired, and kinder-looking than I had braced for; he sat down opposite me and smiled, and the whole room leaned towards him and I leaned with it.

"We read your essay," he said. "The one about whether a promise made to a dead man still binds you. Declan wanted it framed. Eugénie thought the third section was wrong."

"It is wrong," Eugénie said, and the door opened again and Professor Hallam came in.

She was tall, with cropped grey hair and a cardigan that had been expensive a long time ago, and she carried nothing, no book and no papers. She took the chair with its back to the window, so that she was a shape against the light and we were not, and she looked at me.

"Miss Dow," she said, and when I had answered, "You were the night porter. The others nominated you. I didn't."

Over the harbour a gull hung on the wind, beating and beating and staying in the one place, and I watched it because I could not watch her.

Then she said Fabian's name, and for two hours they took apart a page he had written about a drowning child and a pair of good shoes, in a language I had only ever read alone at the night desk with my lips moving. I had not known it could be fast, or that you could be cruel in it, or that Fergus, who could not get to the end of a sentence about a chair, would build an argument so clean and so terrible that Hallam shut her eyes until he had finished. I said one thing in all that time, because Fabian had used the word obviously about something that wasn't, and when I had said it Fabian laughed out loud and Hallam opened her eyes.

"Granted," she said.

Hall

On Thursdays St Ebba's dined by candlelight, and the college's insurers, who had never eaten there, required a porter to stand in the screens passage for the length of the meal with an extinguisher between his boots. That night it was Rennie, the new night man, whom I had trained in September and who had cried in the boiler room on his second shift; he saw me come in wearing a gown and fixed his eyes on the fire door. I knew the code for the alarm panel behind the fourth Master's portrait, and the hour the kitchen bolted its yard door, and which of the young men under the portraits had been sick in the fountain in Freshers' Week. Every one of those things had been useful to me for four years, and I sat down at the corner of the long table nearest the fire, where the Tollan Seminar always sat, and found that not one of them was any use to me at all.

Declan sat on my left. During the soup his knee found mine under the table and stayed, warm through two layers of cloth, pressing and easing and pressing again, and I could not have told anyone what the soup was. I held my spoon level and looked straight ahead at Fergus, who was filling his glass for the third time, and I let it go on.

The main course was pheasant. The kitchen never got all the shot out of the pheasant, and I knew it, and I bit down on a pellet anyway and felt it ring in the root of a tooth, and I swallowed the meat round it and put the lead on the side of my plate with my fork, where it lay like a seed.

I knew her hands before I knew her: the knuckles thickened from a life of cold water, the wedding ring she still wore though there had been nothing to call a marriage for twenty years. They set a plate of potatoes in front of Fabian. They set a plate in front of Declan. Then they set a plate in front of me, and stopped. My mother was standing at my shoulder in her black kitchen tunic with her hair scraped under a net, and she was looking at the gown.

She had known about the scholarship. She had cried at the kitchen table when the letter came and been sharp with me for a week after to make up for it. She had not known I would be here, tonight, at this table, because I had not told her; I had told her I was in the library. I watched her work it out, and watched her pleasure come up through her face, and her mouth open on my name.

"Do you know her?" Fabian said, pleasantly, across the candles. "She's been looking at you since the soup."

"No," I said. I said it in my new voice, the one Declan had not learned yet. Then I put two fingers to the edge of the plate she had just set down and pushed it an inch towards her.

"These are cold," I said. "Could I have some that aren't?" They were not cold; I could see the steam coming off them.

My mother picked the plate up. "Very good, miss," she said, and carried it away down the length of the hall, past the portraits and the black gowns and Rennie with his extinguisher, and through the servery door, and I sat with my hands in my lap and waited, and she came back. She came back with the same potatoes on a different plate, hot enough that she had carried it in a cloth, and she set it down at my place without letting her sleeve touch mine, and went away again, and did not come out to serve the pudding; another woman did, a woman I had known since I was seven, who put my bowl down from as far off as her arm would reach.

Declan was laughing at something and Fergus was looking at his pheasant, and Eugénie laid her knife and fork side by side on the cloth. "That was untrue," she said, to no one, and picked them up again.

I ate all of it, every potato. I wanted to die, and I sat at the table I had wanted all my life and ate the food she had carried to me twice, and when Declan's knee came back under the cloth and pressed against mine, I pressed back.

Lost Property

Old Range 4 was on the first floor above the yard door that never rang. I had been in it once, on a fire-alarm sweep, and found it empty with the grate still warm. Now it was full of firelight and smoke-coloured whisky and books open face down on every arm of every chair. Fergus set his camera on the mantelpiece, Declan lay down on the floor with his ankles crossed, and Eugénie took the hardest chair. Among the invitations on the mantelpiece, between a card from a London chambers and a programme from a play, there was a brass key on a loop of red string with T9 stamped on its tag, which was how we had always tagged the tower keys, and which I had written up as missing in the lodge book morning after morning all through Lent.

Fabian poured the whisky and gave me the first glass, and I drank it too fast, and he filled it again. "Your mother does the puddings," he said.

The fire shifted and I held on to the glass. What else did they have?

"Fran, who does the crumble on Tuesdays. Fergus would die for that crumble. Don't look like that, Dow; nobody will ever say a word about tonight, and what you did at dinner is the most interesting thing that has happened at that table since we started sitting at it. We make a point of knowing about the people we choose. You'd do the same."

Then he went to the desk under the window and opened a drawer and took out a brown calfskin wallet worn soft at the fold, and dropped it on the low table in front of me, between my glass and his. I would have known it in the dark.

"Six hundred and forty pounds went into that," he said. "On the night desk, a little after three, a night in March. Fergus stood out by the sundial and froze. You'd have seen him if the lamp had been working."

I had logged that lamp. Sundial lamp out, 02.50. I had thought it was the frost.

"We'd spent the week on whether honesty is a habit or a sum," Fabian said, "and we wanted a subject who didn't know. We call it a Practical. Four hundred and forty came out the other end, in the lost-property book. What I've wanted to ask you since March is why two hundred."

"We did a man at the bus station once with a fiver," Declan said, from the floor, "he had a little dog, and he..." Fabian said his name, and he lay back down.

I could have lied; I had been lying to my mother since March about where the money for the meter had come from, and I was good at it. But I had sat in that tower all afternoon among people who said exactly what they thought in a language I had taught myself in the dark, and I wanted them to know me more than I wanted to be safe.

"The meter," I said. "Her meter. She was behind."

"A key meter," said Fabian gently, "on the estate on the hill, set to take back most of whatever she put in, so that she was sitting with the lights off in the evenings to make it last. A hundred and ninety-three pounds in arrears. We had somebody look."

It was the cedar: it had been on the stair and in the lodge and on the blotter at ten past three, and I sat in it now with my glass in both hands and understood that it had been all round my mother's front door.

"Seven pounds," I said. "The bus into town to pay it. That's the rest."

Fergus had his hand over his mouth. Eugénie was looking at me for the first time all day, with her pencil stopped.

"Not three hundred," Fabian said. "Not all of it. Exactly what you needed, to the bus fare, and the rest left for a stranger you'd never meet. You're the reason we asked for you, Dow. You're exactly the reason."

I believed that was the finest thing anyone had ever said to me, and I went on believing it until January.

"Tell her the rest," Eugénie said.

Fabian picked the wallet up and turned it over in his hands. "I went down to the lodge this afternoon and claimed it. I told them there had been six hundred and forty in it when I lost it, which there was, and they got out the book." He put it down again, gently. "The entry's in Docherty's hand. Four hundred and forty pounds, logged at ten past seven in the morning. He's the last person on record who touched it."

I could see the entry without being shown it: the square capitals, the crossed sevens, the pound sign he always made too big, a morning in March, with my two hundred pounds already folded into the meter at my mother's house. ITEM CLAIMED. I had read it upside down that afternoon and thought of an umbrella.

"Fabian," Fergus said. "You said we'd only, you said it was only..."

"I know what I said."

Declan had sat up and was watching me, and I could not tell what he was hoping to see.

Eugénie folded her hands in her lap. "At nine o'clock tomorrow morning," she said, "the Bursar is going to sack him for it."
