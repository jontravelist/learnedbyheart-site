The house at 11 Underhay Road had been for sale for six weeks, at a little more than half of what the houses on either side of it would fetch, and in all that time nobody had offered a penny for it.

I took this for luck. I have since read a great many documents about that house, most of which it would have preferred I didn't, and I can tell you that the word luck does not appear in any of them.

Subject to Survey

Chapter one. Three Bedrooms.

I should explain that I read for a living, which sounds grander than it is. I proofread instruction manuals; the booklet that came with your boiler or your bread machine has very possibly been through my hands, and it is thanks to me that it tells you to switch the thing off before you reach inside it and not afterwards. Fourteen years of this work have left me unable to read anything carelessly, so that I go through a bus ticket twice and a menu with a pencil. I read the listing for that house properly, more than once, and the first thing I noticed about it, before I had even come to the price, was that there were no mistakes in it.

This is rarer than you would think. Estate agents, who sell houses on behalf of their owners and write the advertisements for them, are as a profession the most reckless writers in England, and their listings had been my evening reading for years. Every flat in them is deceptively spacious and every garden is mature; apostrophes are thrown over the page like seed. I used to correct them in the margin of the free newspaper, for nobody, and feel a good deal better afterwards. The listing for 11 Underhay Road gave me nothing to correct and very little to read; I have it beside me now, and this is the whole of it.

11 Underhay Road, Sallowby. £150,000.

A house, vacant, being one half of a pair. Three bedrooms, two reception rooms, kitchen, bathroom, garden to the rear.

The seller asks that those who come to view the house come alone.

There was a single photograph, of the front door, which was shut. Sallowby is a market town in the English Midlands with a good many more people than houses, and the houses on Underhay Road change hands for something like £270,000. I knew this in the way you know the price of a coat in a shop you have never gone into. I had lived in the town for fourteen years, at eleven addresses, every one of them rented, and for nine of those years I had been saving; what I had saved would have bought me the garage of a house on Underhay Road, and I had gone on reading the listings regardless, most evenings, with my pencil.

I telephoned the agent the next morning, expecting to be told that the price was a misprint; a dropped figure is the commonest error there is, and I would have enjoyed catching that one. The young man who answered said that the price was correct, in the voice of someone reading out a temperature. "I can show you the house on Thursday at four," he said, and I heard him deciding how to put the rest. "You'll have seen the line about coming alone. That would be all right, would it?"

There was also the matter of the letter in my coat pocket. It had come nine days earlier from my landlord, and it gave me two months to leave the flat because he had decided to sell it; it was the seventh such letter of my life and by some way the best written. I had folded it into quarters and put it in the pocket of my coat, where I could feel it whenever I reached for my keys, and I had told nobody. Paloma, who has been my friend since we were eleven and who would have had me on her sofa by the weekend, lives in one room and a half with a dog the size of an armchair; so when she asked why I was suddenly sending her pictures of houses, I said that I was only looking. I said the same to Lionel, who lives in the flat below mine and notices things, when he met me on the stairs with the property pages under my arm.

Thursday, four o'clock

The viewing fell on the last Thursday of October, when the light in the Midlands starts to go at about the time the schools come out. Underhay Road is a long, straight road of houses built in the 1930s in pairs, each pair joined down the middle and each half the mirror of the other, with a bay window below and another above and a strip of garden in front. Number 9 had acquired a glazed porch with a child's scooter in it. Number 11, joined to it, had no porch and no scooter, and its front door was the door in the old photographs of the road; it was what number 9 would have been if nobody had ever lived there, and its windows were very clean.

The agent was waiting at the gate. He was the young man from the telephone, Kelvin Dacey, of the firm of Dacey & Lund on the market square, and he wore his suit like something he had been left in a will. He shook my hand and looked past me down the road. "You came on your own," he said, and when I said that the listing had asked me to, Mr Dacey nodded with what I can only call relief. "People bring somebody anyway. Then I have to ask the somebody to wait in the car, and everyone's cross before we've started."

He unlocked the front door and pushed it open as far as his arm would reach; then he stepped back onto the path. "I'll leave you to it," he said. "I don't go in on the viewings." I said, "Is that usual?" and he considered the question honestly, which I was to learn was his habit with every question. "No, it isn't usual, but the seller prefers it. And I'd as soon be out here, if I'm truthful. Take as long as you like." He looked at his watch. "People generally don't."

So I went in alone, and the first thing I have to tell you about the inside of 11 Underhay Road is that it was warm. The house had stood empty through six weeks of a wet autumn; the radiator in the hall, when I put my hand flat against it, was as cold as the gate had been; and yet the air was the air of a room that somebody has only just walked out of. I have moved into a good many empty homes and I know the smell of them well, a compound of drains and other people's dust, and this house smelled of nothing whatever, which I had not known a house could do. I stood on the doormat and looked down, from habit, for letters.

There was not one letter on it. Any house left empty for six weeks ought to have a drift of post behind the door, takeaway menus and letters for people who moved away years ago, and here the mat was bare and brushed. I turned round to look at the letterbox. From the street it had been an ordinary brass flap; on the inside a second flap had been laid over the slot and fastened down with four screws, very neatly, and then painted over along with the door, so long ago that the paint on the screws had yellowed to match.

The hall had three doors besides the front one, and all three opened outwards, towards me. I don't know whether you have ever met this in a house; I never had. To go into a room you pull the door towards you and then walk round it, and with all three standing open there was scarcely room left in the hall for me. The light switches were worse. In every room the switch was on the hinge side of the doorway, behind the open door and level with my shoulder, so that in the dark you would have to go right in and pull the door shut after you before you could feel along the wall for it. I wrote "doors" and "switches" on the back of my printed listing, and told myself that a builder in 1934 had hung everything from the wrong drawing.

The front room and the back room were bare and swept. Their carpets were old and good and had kept the print of the furniture that used to stand on them, so that I could have told you where the sofa had been and which way the table was turned. In the back room there were the marks of four chair legs, deeper than the rest, where one chair had stood in one place for a very long time. It had stood a foot from the side wall and facing it, with its back to the window and the garden; the side wall is the one that divides number 11 from number 9, and there was nothing on it to look at but the paper.

In the kitchen I did the thing I always do, and since nobody knows that I do it I had better explain. When I leave a flat I write a note for whoever comes next and tape it inside the door of the cupboard under the sink. It says which window sticks and where to turn the water off, which neighbour will take in a parcel and which should not be trusted with one; I have left eleven of these, and I have never signed one or had an answer. So in any kitchen I open that cupboard first. The cupboard at 11 Underhay Road was clean and held nothing, and on the inside of its door, at the height where I would have put a note myself, was a rectangle of paler paint the size of a postcard, with a tag of old tape at each corner.

Someone had left a note there for whoever came next, and someone had taken it down. I stayed crouched on the linoleum for some time. The sensible explanation was that the seller had tidied before the sale, and I supplied it to myself in full; but a person who tidies away a note does not usually leave the tape, and I found that I minded, on behalf of whoever had written it, more than the occasion deserved.

Upstairs

The stairs made no sound under me. The landing was long and lit by one window at the far end, and off it opened a bathroom, a front bedroom with the upper bay, a back bedroom over the garden and a small bedroom over the hall, each with its door swinging out at me and its switch hidden behind. That made three bedrooms, as the listing had said; it is my habit to check a figure against the thing it describes, and they agreed. I measured the front bedroom for a bed I did not yet own. From its window I could see Mr Dacey on the pavement, and I noticed that he was standing with his back to the house, like a man at a bus stop who would rather not see the bus come.

The bathroom had a bath on feet and a basin with separate taps, and when I turned the hot one it ran warm over my fingers at once and then properly hot. I have corrected the manuals for a great many boilers, and not one of them will give you hot water at the tap when the radiators are cold; I looked for an electric heater under the basin, and did not find one. The small bedroom over the hall was papered with a pattern of leaves, quite unfaded, and it was the only room in the house where the carpet showed no marks at all.

It was on the landing, coming out, that I looked properly at the carpet there. It was worn pale in a path from the head of the stairs past each of the doors in turn, as you would expect. What I did not expect was that the path went on. It ran beyond the last door, the full length of the landing, to the blank wall at the end beside the window, and there it stopped, in a patch the size of a doormat worn nearly through to the backing. There was nothing on the wall. I considered a mirror, and then a bookcase, and settled on the mirror, because people do stand in front of mirrors; and I did not go and stand there myself.

I took out the listing to add the landing to my notes, and could not read it. The afternoon was nearly gone, so there was nothing remarkable in that; I carried the page to the window and held it up to what was left of the light, where the printing seemed fainter than it had on the bus. My own pencil notes on the back, made not ten minutes before, were so pale that I had to tilt the paper to find them. I put this down to the dusk and to a cheap printer, and wrote the word "landing" underneath them, pressing hard.

I ought to have been uneasy, and I will not pretend to you that I was. What I felt, standing at that window with the garden going dark below me, was something I had not felt in any of the places I had lived. In every one of those flats there had been a person, somewhere, who was entitled to write me a letter. Here there would be nobody, and I could put a nail in this wall if I chose, and stay until I was old. The house was warm, and it had been waiting six weeks for somebody, and I am ashamed to tell you how quickly I decided that the somebody was me.

On the pavement

It was nearly dark when I came out; I had been inside for forty minutes and would have sworn to fifteen. Mr Dacey turned round when he heard the door, and I asked him the only question there was. "Why is it so cheap?" He put his hands in his pockets. "I'm obliged to tell you it's priced to sell." I said, "And if you weren't obliged?" and he looked up at the bedroom windows before he answered, and then away from them.

"I'd say I don't know. There's nothing wrong with it that anybody's ever found. The roof's sound and the neighbours are nice. I've sold the other half of the pair twice, at the full price, and never heard another word about it." He nodded at number 11. "This one comes back."

"Comes back where?" I said. "It comes back on the market. I've had it four times in nine years, and my father had it before me, more often than he liked to say." I asked whether the owners ever told him why they were selling, and he shook his head. "I've never had one of them in the office. It's all done by telephone and by post. I've not met Mrs Marle, the lady who has it now. I'll say this for her, though; she's always in when I ring."

I had meant to go home and think, and to come back in daylight with somebody who knew about roofs. Instead I put my hand in my coat pocket for my gloves and found the letter there, folded in four, and heard myself say that I would like to offer the asking price. An agent ought to look pleased at that. Mr Dacey looked at me with an expression I took then for surprise, and have since decided was pity. "You don't want to go away and think about it?" he said. "I have been thinking about it for fourteen years," I said.

He took out his telephone and walked a few steps up the path to make the call, and I stood at the gate and looked at my house, which I was already calling it. It was while I stood there that a telephone began to ring inside the house, behind the shut front door: an old-fashioned bell, quite loud, somewhere towards the back. I had been in every room of that house not ten minutes before, and I had opened its cupboards. There was no telephone in it, nor any socket in the wall where one might have been plugged in; I would have written it down.

It rang twice and stopped, and Mr Dacey said, "Mrs Marle? It's Kelvin Dacey." The two halves of a pair of houses share a wall, I reminded myself, and there was no reason on earth why the people at number 9 should not own a telephone with a bell. I had this ready before he had finished his first sentence, and I held on to it while he talked, as you hold on to a rail.

He did not talk for long. He told her that there was an offer, and that it came from a Miss Pettifer who had viewed the house alone; then he listened, and then he put his hand over the telephone. "She says yes," he said. "She says she's glad it's you." I said, "She can't possibly know who I am," and Mr Dacey seemed to turn this over. "No," he said. "No, I don't see how she could."

I had read enough guides for first-time buyers to know that there was a thing you said next, and I said it, feeling for the first time in my life like a person conducting business. "Subject to survey," I said. "Tell her the offer is subject to survey." He repeated it into the telephone exactly as I had given it to him. This time he listened for longer, with his eyes on the upstairs bay, and the light had gone so far by then that I could not see his face when he lowered the telephone and turned back to me.

"She says she'd rather you didn't."
