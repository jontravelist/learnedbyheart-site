A Very Good Copy

Chapter one. Her Own Hand.

The Patrons' Gallery

It had rained every day since they arrived, and the Patrons' Gallery smelled of it. Half the Season had come in out of the wet to look at old masters, and the long room steamed.

The pictures climbed the red walls to the ceiling, frame against frame. The proudest hung on the line, at the height of a standing person's eyes.

Lettice loved it from the doorway. How had she forgotten this? Forty faces looking down at once, every one painted by somebody long dead, and every one more alive than the crowd.

"I can't see anything," Bel said. "There's a man's back in front of everything."

"Then look at the man's back. Somebody tailored it with great love."

"It's wet. It's got rain all down it in a sort of shape. Like Norfolk." Bel was nineteen and had been told all her life that she was the beauty of the family, and took it as a plain fact of no interest. "Sir Lucius said there'd be gentlemen. Where are they?"

"They're here. They're looking at pictures."

"Why?"

"Because it's the fashion. You stand beside one in front of a Rubens and say something clever about the flesh."

"I don't know anything about flesh," Bel said, so clearly that a lady in a turban turned round.

Sir Lucius Pettigrew, who was paying for Bel's Season, had found the only bench and fallen asleep on it with his chin in his cravat. Somebody had propped a wet umbrella against his knee.

Then the crowd shifted, and a gap opened along the line, and Lettice saw the Magdalen.

The saint sat half turned to the light, her eyes lifted and full of tears, her hair coming down over her bare shoulders in a great loose fall. One hand lay on her breast. The other rested beside an alabaster jar.

Lettice knew the tear on the lower lid that had taken a whole afternoon. And she knew the hand on the breast, because it was hers. The little finger bent outwards at the top joint, where the vestry door had shut on it when she was eight.

Under the frame a card was pinned to the wall.

TITIAN. The Penitent Magdalen. Lent by His Grace the Duke of Mardale.

That's mine.

She sat down without meaning to, on the end of Sir Lucius's bench, and on the umbrella. It folded under her with a crack of whalebone and a gush of cold water that went straight through her petticoat.

She had painted it the winter she was nineteen, in the long gallery at Wrensham Hall, where the housekeeper let her in when the family was away. She had finished the hand at home in the attic, holding her own hand to her chest in front of the looking-glass, because Titian's wouldn't come right and hers was the only one in the house that would sit still. A man from London had paid her five guineas. That winter they had eaten meat on Sundays.

Over the next five years the man from London had bought eleven more.

Get up and walk out. Who else in this room knows that hand?

She got up. Then she went and stood in front of it, because it was the first time anyone had hung a thing she'd made on a wall.

The Umbrella

"Oh, no," she said aloud. "Oh, no, no."

"You don't care for it."

The man beside her had a cold and a dripping umbrella. He was tall enough to see over the ostrich feathers and was choosing to look at her instead.

His coat was brown and had been good ten years ago, and there was ink on his cuff. The rain had made his dark hair curl against his collar, and he held his jaw a little to one side. She could smell cloves. Toothache. A tutor, or some lord's secretary, sent to look at the pictures his master is too idle to look at himself.

"I care for it enormously," she said. "That's the difficulty."

"It's considered the best thing in the room."

"Is it?"

"The newspapers are expected to say so." He put two fingers to his jaw and pressed, hard, as though he could push the tooth back where it belonged. "I've never seen what's good about it. A great many people have explained it to me. One of them used a stick."

"Then you haven't looked at it. Look at her. She's been crying so long she's tired of it. She's lifting her eyes because there's nowhere else left to look. And somebody painted that. Somebody sat in front of the most beautiful picture they'd ever seen and tried so hard. It's a very good copy."

The man stopped moving entirely.

"It's a Titian," he said.

"Yes. Of course. I mean only that it's very like. My father has a print of a Saint Jerome in the vestry, and the damp has got into it, and when I said copy I meant it has the same feeling as his print." She heard how that sounded. "We're from Norfolk."

"Titian didn't paint in Norfolk."

"More's the pity. He'd have liked the skies."

"And I have looked at her, madam." His voice had an edge on it now, and his ears had gone red under the wet hair. "I have looked at her across my breakfast since I was ten years old. She has watched me eat several thousand eggs. If she's tired of anything, it's me."

The duke's secretary, then, or some poor tutor kept on after the boys grew up. It explained the coat.

"If she were in Ovid," he said, more quietly, frowning at the saint, "she'd have been turned into something by now. A spring, most likely. They always become springs when they cry that much. Byblis did. Niobe became a rock that wept, which is worse, because a rock can't go anywhere."

A large party came through from the next room, and the crowd folded inwards, and she was pressed against the front of him from shoulder to knee. He put out his free hand to steady her. It didn't find her waist. It found her bare left hand, and his thumb came to rest on the bent joint of her little finger. It moved over the joint once, slowly, testing it. The crowd eased. He didn't let go. She could smell cloves on his breath and wet wool, and there was nowhere at all to put her face.

She looked at his mouth, and then, very firmly, at the saint.

I am twenty-seven years old. I am a chaperon. I came here to look at the pictures.

She pulled her hand away. His fingers closed on nothing, slowly, as though she were still in them.

"You've seen the original," he said.

"I'd never been out of Norfolk in my life until Tuesday. I wouldn't know an original if it bit me. I'm told they don't, generally. Excuse me. My sister is being looked at."

The Back Stairs

Bel was reading the catalogue. Lettice took her arm and steered her towards the far end of the room, away from the saint, and that was when she saw the waistcoat.

It was under a great dark battle full of horses. It was striped, cream and plum, and it was stretched over a stomach that had not been there eight years ago, when it had stood in her father's hall dripping on the flags and its owner had counted five guineas onto the hall table, one at a time, so she could watch each one land.

Mr Josiah Marchbank laughed at something, and she knew the laugh too.

"Bel. Stay here. Stay by Sir Lucius."

"He's asleep."

"Then stay by him asleep."

She went along the wall with her back to the pictures and waited by a pillar while he bowed a young lady in an enormous bonnet on towards the next room. Then he looked at his watch, put it away, and slipped out through a small door at the end that she would have taken for a cupboard.

Lettice counted to ten and opened it.

It was a service stair, narrow and cold and smelling of lamp oil. He was halfway down. She said his name, and it came out much too loud, and it went bouncing down the stairwell ahead of him.

He stopped. He turned. For a long moment he looked up at her with his head on one side and no idea at all who she was, and she thought, He's forgotten. He's sold so many that he's forgotten me. Then his eyes went down to her hands.

"Miss Darrow. Miss Darrow of the vicarage. Well, here's a happy thing. In town for the Season? And looking so well."

"You sold it as a Titian."

"I sold a very great gentleman a very great pleasure. He loved that picture, God rest him. He'd sit in front of it of an evening with his port."

"You told me it was for an old man in the north who couldn't travel."

"Northamptonshire is the north, from where I stand." He came up a step, and another, until their faces were level. "My dear lady. You did a charming thing, once, for a very fair price. Your little studies gave a great deal of joy. Let's leave it there, shall we, and you enjoy your Season."

"I'll go to the duke."

"And tell him what? That a parson's girl in Norfolk painted his Titian?" He smiled. "He'll laugh. And then, being a careful sort of young man, he'll ask how many. And I'll have to tell him. A dozen, wasn't it? And I'll have to show him the receipts, in your own lovely hand. 'Received of Mr J. Marchbank, five guineas, for a Magdalen.' Did you ever once write the word copy? I've looked, dear lady. You never did. Not once in five years. I'd call that a very trusting nature, and a lovely thing to find in a young woman, and I'd be sorry, very sorry, to see it held against you."

She hadn't. She had been so proud of her handwriting.

"Your father's bishop is a great man for pictures. Did you know? He has a sweet little Saint Cecilia from me. He'd be heartbroken. And your sister, a lovely girl. Lovely girls have such delicate Seasons. One word, and it's all over by July."

She caught hold of his sleeve. What did she mean to do with it? He looked down at her hand on his cloth and took it off, one finger at a time, gently, until he got to the last.

"Such a distinctive little hand," he said, and let it go, and went back up past her into the light.

She stayed on the step. She was frightened, more frightened than she had been since the night the bailiff's letter came. She pressed her hand flat against the cold wall until it stopped shaking, and then she pressed it harder.

His Grace

Gervase Keverne, fourth Duke of Mardale, hated the Patrons' Gallery and the Magdalen hanging in it, and he had a tooth with a nail in it that somebody was driving home with great patience.

He had lent the picture because the bank had asked him to. His father had borrowed against the collection, and the bank's senior partner liked to see his security admired in public before he renewed a loan. He had offered, in writing, to lend the bank the picture outright, so its clerks could admire it all day over their desks, and the senior partner had written back at great length on the subject of interest.

His father used to sit in front of her for an hour after dinner. At ten, Gervase had been fetched down in his nightshirt to sit beside him and say what he saw. A lady crying, he had said, and been sent back to bed.

Today a woman in a wet dress had told him what the lady was feeling, and for half a minute he had seen it. Then she'd called it a copy. She had looked like somebody who'd been struck.

He ought to have told her who he was. But then she would have started calling him "Your Grace" and stopped saying things like she's tired of crying, and was it so very wicked to want one more minute of that? He wanted to stand in a crush beside her again. He wanted it more plainly than he had wanted anything since the funeral, and he could still feel the bent joint of her finger under his thumb. He had not meant to do that. He had meant it entirely. He went on wanting it while he watched her come back through the far door with a face like paper.

Marchbank had come through that door a minute before her, smoothing his waistcoat.

The Easel

In the corner by the window a young woman was working at a small easel. She was perhaps twenty-four, in a brown stuff dress with the sleeves pinned up, and she was copying the Magdalen's head on a canvas no bigger than a tea tray.

The young lady in the enormous bonnet had found her.

"Oh, look, she's doing Papa's Magdalen, isn't that killing? Is it the Venetian manner? Mr Fosse says the Venetian manner is all in the, the impasting, the warm grounds and the, he says everything is grounds with the Venetians, I've no idea what he means." She saw Lettice and seized her arm. "Mr Marchbank says you have the most extraordinary eye. A clergyman's daughter from Norfolk with an eye, he says, he's been telling everyone. I'm Honoria Keverne. You must tell us."

Lettice looked at Marchbank, and he bowed.

"The papers say half the old masters in England were painted last year in Soho," said one of the young gentlemen, a Mr Pargeter. "By starving girls exactly like this one. Is that true, miss? Did you paint Lady Honoria's papa's Titian?"

Everyone laughed. The young woman went on painting.

"Well, then, Miss Darrow," said Lady Honoria. "Is my brother's Titian a Titian? Or did she paint it?"

They were all looking at her. Marchbank was looking at her. And at the edge of the group, holding a dripping umbrella, the man with the toothache was looking at her too.

"Of course it's a Titian," Lettice said.

It wasn't enough. She could see that in Marchbank's pleasant face. So she stepped up beside the young woman's shoulder and said the rest.

"This is what a copy looks like. Look at it. She's counted every hair. She's so frightened of getting it wrong there isn't a breath of life on it anywhere. It's a sign for a coaching inn. It's a very careful sign for a coaching inn." The young gentlemen laughed, and that made it easier. "You can tell from the door, can't you? Anyone could. Poor thing, it died on the easel. I'm sorry, but it did."

The young woman put her brush down.

"It ain't finished," she said.

"Ain't it?" said Mr Pargeter, and the bonnets shook.

"You don't know nothing about it, miss."

She said it to Lettice, not loudly, and began to pack up. Her hands shook. She had trouble with the lid of her paint box, and when she lifted her jar of turpentine it slipped and went down her skirt in a dark stain.

"Mind the floor," said Mr Pargeter. "It's the society's floor."

Lettice didn't help her.

She stood with her hands folded, the left one under the right, while the young woman picked up her wet canvas and carried it out through the crowd, holding it away from her body so it wouldn't smear, and people stepped back from it as from something dirty.

The man with the umbrella looked at Lettice for a while, with no expression she could read. Then he turned and went into the crowd. She didn't even know his name.

In the carriage, with Sir Lucius snoring between them, Bel said, "Why did you say it was real?"

"Bel, please."

"It's the one with your hand on it. I was there. I held the candle."

"Never say that again. Not to anyone. Not to God."

"God knows already," Bel said. "It's your hand." She looked out at the rain. "I don't like it when you lie, Letty. You go on and on. And that girl's skirt is ruined, and she's only got the one, you could tell, it was the same as Mrs Plaskett's Sunday one and she's had that since before the war."

Lettice held her left hand in her right all the way home.

Thursday

On Thursday it rained harder. Sir Lucius read the newspaper aloud at breakfast while Bel ate toast.

Lettice had slept badly for two nights. Had anyone seen?

"Ha," said Sir Lucius. "Here's a bit of mischief. Mardale, that's the young one, the son, the father was the one with the pictures, I knew the father, I sat next to him at a dinner once in, it was either Bath or it was Brighton, and he talked the whole of the fish course about a nose. A nose in a painting. I thought it was his own nose for a good while. Where was I? Here. 'To the Editor. Sir, the picture lent this summer to the Patrons' Gallery by the Duke of Mardale, and hung there as a Titian, is no such thing.' Well! Who says so? It isn't signed. 'It is a copy. It was painted in a house in Norfolk, over one winter, mostly by candlelight.' Norfolk! Did you girls see it? Was it any good?"

Lettice couldn't speak.

"Is there any more of the orange kind?" Bel said. "Not the lemon. The lemon's got bits in it that look like fingernails."

Sir Lucius cleared his throat for the last line. "I know it is a copy," he read, "because I watched her paint it."
