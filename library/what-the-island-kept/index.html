The Penitence albatross breeds in one place on Earth.

Penitence Island lies in the South Atlantic, 2,400 kilometres from the nearest city. Sealers' ships brought house mice there two centuries ago. By this winter there were about a million, and a charity had paid for helicopters to poison every one of them in June.

First someone had to count them. The island's warden was to meet the scientists on the beach. He had stopped answering the radio three days earlier.

What the Island Kept

Chapter one. Baseline.

MV Polar Wren, off the east coast, 0740 hours

MV Polar Wren, radio log, extract

  • 11 May, 1800: called Penitence Station on the scheduled evening call, no response.
  • 12 May, 0800: called Penitence Station, no response. Tried the emergency channel, no response.
  • 13 May, 0800: no response. Station satellite beacon reporting normally (position, battery 81%). Master informed.
  • 14 May, 0700: no response. Landing to go ahead as planned. Station outbound freight to be collected from the beach 15 May, 0800. D Rakić, Master.

Piers Strick had printed the log and put it in a plastic sleeve, and he gave it to Vera Kastner at the rail while the crane took the weight of the boat. He was wearing the foundation's fleece over his life jacket.

"So, look, I don't want us reading too much into this," he said. "The beacon's green, so the power's fine, and Ansel's a known quantity. Get eyes on and we'll circle back at the evening call. We take his freight off at eight tomorrow, and then you've got the island to yourselves for thirty days, and on day thirty the helicopters come in and we start the drop. That date doesn't move. I can't stress that enough, from the foundation's side."

"Nothing comes off that beach on to this ship unless I open it first," said Tomasz Wilk, behind them. He was already in the boat, standing, with his hand on the crane hook. "Mice came to this island in a ship. That is how they go to the next island. In a ship, in a box somebody sealed."

"Totally. Absolutely. Biosecurity's front and centre for us," said Piers, and then the boat was going over the side, and Vera climbed down the ladder into it.

It hit the water in a trough so deep that for several seconds she could see only grey water above her on both sides. Then the swell lifted them and there was the island, much too close. A black wall of cliff rose with its top in cloud, and at its foot lay a beach of boulders the size of cars, furred green with weed. The noise from the beach was a long roar with a crack in it whenever a wave hit the rocks. The coxswain stood at the outboard with his eyes on the sea behind them, counting the waves under his breath in Croatian.

Tomasz had made himself the man at the front without anyone deciding it. He was 52, broad and heavy, and he had cleared rats and mice from more islands than anyone alive. He spoke to the four scientists in the boat as he would to a crew he was paying.

"Bags first, then people. The man with the box is last. When I say go, you go. You don't look at the water, you look at the rock."

"It's a sequencer," said Kofi Adjei, who had the case on his knees with both arms round it. "It's the new model, actually. My supervisor nearly didn't let me take it, he said what if it ends up in the sea, and I said, well, it's an island, the sea is sort of the main..."

"Go," said Tomasz. The bags went up first, and then Nayra Quispe followed them, light and certain, because she had made this landing every winter for most of her working life and knew which boulder to jump for. Signe Aalto, the vet, did exactly what she had been told and looked at the rock.

Vera looked at the water. She slipped and landed on her hip on the weed, and Tomasz hauled her up the boulder by the back of her jacket without looking at her. The boat dropped away on the backwash and came in again on the next wave, and Kofi stepped off it too early and went into the sea up to his waist. He held the case above his head. The wave went over his chest and into his collar, and when it drew back he was still standing there with his mouth open and his arms straight up.

"Your lips are blue," said Signe.

"It's nine degrees." Kofi was shaking so hard the words came out in pieces.

"Eight point six," said Signe. "I looked at the sea temperature on the ship."

While the others passed the cases up the boulders, Vera took a small black recorder out of her chest pocket and held it towards the surf. Tomasz shouted at her to carry something, and she kept the recorder out at arm's length while he shouted, until the coxswain had turned the boat and was running back through the swell towards the ship.

Penitence Station, 1120 hours

The path from the beach climbed through tussock grass higher than their heads. For twenty minutes they walked in a green trench with the wind going over the top and the ground so wet that every step sucked. The station stood on stilts at the edge of the coastal plain, a long hut clad in aluminium, with a wind turbine beside it and a satellite dish on the roof. A pair of grey socks had been pegged on the radio aerial to dry and had frozen there.

Inside it was colder than outside. It smelled of diesel and of something sweet and bad from the kitchen, where a pan of porridge had set solid on the cold stove with a spoon standing in it. The radio set was on its shelf. It had been unplugged at the wall, and the cable had been wound into a neat coil and laid on top. Vera stood and looked at the coil. Somebody careful had done that on purpose, and then walked away from it and left the porridge to set.

Along the wall by the door stood four plywood crates, banded with steel strap, each sealed with a numbered plastic tag and stencilled for the ship. Tomasz walked along them and put his hand flat on each lid.

They heard Moberg before they saw him, a voice out in the tussock talking in a friendly undertone that stopped and started. Then Ansel Moberg came up the steps with a white plastic bucket in one hand. He was tall and very thin. His beard had been cut with scissors and no mirror, and he wore a woollen hat over a second woollen hat. He looked at the five of them in his kitchen and nodded.

"Receiving you," he said. "Welcome to Penitence. You're early."

"We're on the day we said," said Vera. "Why haven't you answered the radio?"

"The anemometer's been reading low since about April, by two or three knots. I've logged it. You'll want to correct for it in anything you do with wind." He set the bucket down. "And the tide gauge needs a new battery, I've written that down as well."

Nayra crossed the kitchen and put her arms round him, and he let her, holding the bucket out at arm's length so that it would not touch her. In the bucket, in a few centimetres of brown water, drowned mice floated on their sides, swollen, with their tails trailing together in the middle like weed.

Tomasz picked one out by the tail and held it up to the window. Water ran down his wrist into his sleeve. "This is a rat."

"House mouse," said Moberg. "Weigh him if you want."

"I weigh all of them. What is in the boxes?"

"Samples for the university. My clothes. Rubbish, mostly. They're sealed, I sealed them on Friday, the numbers are on the manifest."

"Tonight I open them."

"Copy," said Moberg, quite kindly, and took the bucket outside and emptied it over the rail.

They set up the laboratory at the end of the hut. Nayra unpacked her bird kit next to Vera's and asked after Vera's mother, whom she had met once at a conference years ago, when she was still well enough to travel. "How is she?"

"The same."

"Is she still at home, or...?"

"Kofi, where's the spring balance? I want it on the bench before we go up." Vera put the trap case on the bench with her right hand and watched the hand while she did it. It was steady. She checked it most hours, and it was always steady, and when she looked up Nayra had turned away and was counting leg bands into a bag, and Moberg was at the door saying that if they wanted the plateau before dark they had to leave now.

The plateau, albatross study area 4, 1610 hours

The climb took two hours, up a gully of tree ferns and loose black scree and out on to the plateau, where the cloud had lifted and the light was yellow and thin. The ground was peat bog cut by streams, and all over it sat the albatross chicks on their nests. Each nest was a pedestal of mud and grass about knee-high, and on each one sat a single chick the size of a goose, in grey down so thick that the wind barely moved it.

Moberg walked between the nests fast, talking to them by number. "Four-oh-nine. Hello, four-ten." Nayra walked behind him with her tablet and kept calling the numbers before he did, which he did not seem to mind.

"We have an hour," Vera said. She was out of breath and trying not to show it. "They're nocturnal. They won't be on the surface till it's properly dark."

Nayra stopped walking. On the nest in front of her the chick was sitting upright with its head raised, and on its lower back, where the down met the tail, there was a mouse. It was not hiding. It crouched on the chick in full daylight with its face buried in the feathers, working. A second mouse came round the side of the nest, climbed the mud and went in beside the first. Where they were feeding, the down was matted dark and wet, and there was a hole in the bird about two centimetres across.

The chick made no attempt to move. It turned its head back towards the place and made a small clattering sound with its bill. Then it faced forward again into the wind, and sat.

"Four-one-twelve," said Nayra. "Vera, write it down, four-one-twelve, she hatched in March, she was a good weight, both parents are banded..."

"We don't interfere," said Moberg. "That's protocol. We record."

"In nineteen days I put poison on every square metre of this island," said Tomasz. "That one is dead already. I finish it."

He knocked both mice off the nest with the back of his glove and took the chick by the neck with both hands. He had killed thousands of animals and had never killed one of these. The neck was long and much thicker than it looked under the down, all muscle, and when he pulled, the chick only stretched. It screamed, a hoarse, honking scream that went on and on, and the half-grown wings came out on both sides, more than a metre from tip to tip, and beat at him. Its hooked bill came round and closed on his wrist above the glove.

He swore in Polish and pulled again, harder and at the wrong angle. Something in the neck gave, and still the chick did not die. It was making a different sound now, wet and breathy. It opened its bill and threw up over his arm a stream of yellow oil that stank of fish and of something worse than fish. He let go, and the chick fell off the nest into the bog with its head turned round on its neck and its wings still moving.

Nayra was shouting at him in Spanish and then in English. Vera stood with the tablet in both hands and did not move. She was frightened of the bird, and more frightened of Tomasz, and she could not make herself go forward.

Signe came over the edge of the plateau at a run with Kofi a long way behind. She knelt on the chick's wings with one hand on its head and one under its jaw, braced her knee and did something short and hard, and the legs pushed in the bog and stopped.

She sat back on her heels. "You pulled," she said to Tomasz. "With a bird this size you twist and pull at once, with a lot of force. Or you use something heavy on the back of the skull. You should have waited for me."

"I don't wait."

"I know. I saw."

He walked off a few metres and sat on a rock and wiped his sleeve on the tussock, over and over, though the oil was not coming off. His wrist was bleeding into the glove. Nayra crouched by the chick, crying, with her hand on its back, then took the hand away because the down was warm and sticky. Moberg stood apart with his hat pulled down, and his lips were moving while he looked along the line of nests.

The two mice had come back. They were on the dead chick in the bog, at the same hole, feeding, while the five of them stood round it. Signe knocked them off. They ran a few metres into the tussock and sat up to watch her.

"These are not hungry mice," Tomasz said from his rock. "Hungry mice run away. These mice are waiting for us to go."

"Set the station line when we get down," Vera said to Kofi. "All twenty traps. I want numbers tonight, before that boat comes in."

Penitence Station, laboratory, 2215 hours

Kofi and Vera had set 20 live traps round the station at 1800, small aluminium boxes baited with peanut butter and oats, and checked them at 2130. Fourteen of the 20 had caught a mouse. Two of the traps were full of blood where a mouse had chewed at its own back foot trying to get out.

They worked in head torches because the generator was struggling. Kofi tipped each mouse into a clear plastic bag and hung the bag on a spring balance. Vera took the mouse out by the loose skin behind its neck, turned it over on her palm and looked at the space between the tail and the genitals, which is longer in males, and called it. Kofi punched a two-millimetre clip from the edge of the ear and dropped it into a numbered tube of ethanol. The mice were very strong. One bit Vera through her glove, and she did not let go of it.

Penitence baseline survey, rodent data sheet, trap line A (station), night 1

  • A-01: 41.5 g, male, adult, ear clipped
  • A-02: 36.0 g, male, adult, old scar on tail
  • A-04: 44.0 g, male, adult, heavy wear on teeth
  • A-05: 33.5 g, male, subadult (query)
  • A-07: 39.0 g, male, adult, bit the handler

"My sister's getting married in August," said Kofi, somewhere around the sixth mouse. "In Kumasi. She's asked me to do a speech, and I said I'll be in the middle of the Atlantic, Efua, and she said they have phones on islands, and I said, not really, actually, not ones that..." He stopped and looked at the balance. "This one's 46 grams, Vera. A mouse in a kitchen is about 20."

"Male," said Vera.

"Are they all going to be male?"

"It's the first night. Males range further than females and they're bolder, so they find the traps first. You always get a male bias on night one."

"The survey three years ago was 51 per cent female, in the same month, with the same traps, it says in the methods. So if it's not the traps, then something's happening to the ratio itself, which is..." He let it go and wrote down the clip number.

"It's the traps."

She called the next one male, and the next. There were not many ways for a population of wild mice to stop making daughters. Most of them were starvation or disease, and these mice were neither starving nor sick. One of the others she had read about only in papers from laboratories whose animals were never allowed outside a locked room, and she did not want to think about that one in this hut at night. At the fourteenth her hands were cold and her right thumb jumped once on the mouse's back. Kofi looked up. She put the mouse in its bag and put the hand in her pocket and kept it there.

Tomasz came in without knocking, with his wrist bandaged in a tidy white cuff and his sleeve still stinking of oil, and read the sheet over Kofi's shoulder, all of it. "Fourteen males. No women. And four boxes at the door that go on a ship at eight o'clock."

"Open them," Vera said. "Tonight, all four. Anything alive in them stays on the island."

"It's mice, Vera," said Kofi. "There's mice in every port in the world already, one more box isn't going to..."

"Not these mice. Not till we know what's wrong with them."

Tomasz looked at her with more interest than he had shown all day, and went to fetch the bolt cutters. Outside the generator changed its note, and the bench light flickered and came back. Far out in the dark, past the beach, the lights of the Polar Wren rose and fell on the swell where she was waiting for the morning.

Moberg was in the doorway. He was in his socks with both hats on, holding a mug that had stopped steaming a while ago, and he was not looking at the crates, or at the bolt cutters in Tomasz's hand. He was looking past them at the sheet on the bench.

"There won't be many females," Moberg said.
