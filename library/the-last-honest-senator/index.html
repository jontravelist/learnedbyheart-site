My master could not tell one face from another, and on the morning Sulla's army woke up inside Rome I was the only person in the city who knew it.

He knew his wife by her voice and her step, and his father by his cough, and me because I was always at his left elbow. Everyone else was a stranger to him until I said their name into his ear. I was seventeen. I thought it was the most harmless trade in the world.

The Last Honest Senator

Chapter one. Who Is That?

Rome, 88 BC

The callers came late that morning, and few of them. On an ordinary day freedmen and small tradesmen filled the hall at first light, wanting to be seen greeting Aulus Sarrenus so that Aulus Sarrenus would remember them. That day they barely filled the bench by the door. They kept their cloaks on and talked about the fire. The houses on the Esquiline had burned the afternoon before, when people threw tiles down at Sulla's legion and his archers shot fire back up at the roofs; the smoke had come down our lane in the night and got into the water jar.

I stood where I always stood, and as each man came up I said his name without moving my lips much. My old teacher used to say the art was to let the master seem to remember on his own; a client who sees the slave whispering feels cheated, and he has a right to.

"Vettidius the baker," I said, "whose wife has just had the baby."

"Vettidius, of course, of course." My master took the man's hand in both of his. "And it's a boy, I hear, or I may be, no, I'm thinking of somebody else, I always do this, but the gods be thanked in any case, whichever it turned out to be."

"A girl," said the baker.

"A girl, good, that's very good, girls are, my own daughter is four, and she's, well."

He was thirty-two that year, a tall man with a long, anxious face. He had been quaestor, the censors had put him on the Senate's roll, and he sat at the back, where the men who are never asked their opinion sit. He had a way of starting a kindness and leaving it unfinished, and people took it for modesty.

The next man came up quickly, before I had his name ready, a broad man in a good cloak with his hand already out, and my master's face opened with relief.

"Gnaeus, my dear brother-in-law," he said. "You've come through it all right, then."

It was not Gnaeus. It was Marcus Opsius, who collected for the bankers my master's father owed money to. He had been told twice that month that the master was out. I saw him understand that he had been taken for someone dear, and I saw him decide to enjoy it.

"Opsius," I said, a little louder than I should have. "Master, it's Opsius, from the bankers. The light in here is so bad this morning with the smoke that I took him for your wife's brother myself." Opsius had to stop smiling to agree that the smoke was bad, and by then my master had let go of his hand.

That was when old Spurius came out. My master's father was about seventy, and for a year or more he had been going in and out of his own mind like a man trying several doors. He came across the hall in his sleeping tunic, which had egg down the front of it, holding a lamp that had gone out. He stopped in the middle of the floor and looked at the clients.

"Is it the Gracchus boy?" he said. "They're up on the Capitol again, are they, I heard them all night. Nasica will want men. Aulus, where's my, somebody fetch me a leg off a bench, they're the right weight. You can't do it with your fists. I said it to Nasica, I said a man has to have something in his hand."

"Father," my master said, "it's the morning greeting."

"I know what it is." He looked down. A dark patch was spreading over the front of his tunic, and then the smell reached us, and it was not only water. He stood in it on the floor his grandfather had laid and went on looking at the clients with great dignity, waiting for one of them to bring him his toga.

The baker studied the roof. My master stood with his hands half raised, still looking for the end of a sentence that would put this right. Then Atilia came out from the back with her sleeves pushed up and took the old man by the elbow. "Come on, Father, you're wet through. Dama, the floor."

So I got down on my knees with a cloth and a bucket and cleaned up after my master's father, and the clients stepped round me on their way out, holding their cloaks clear. Opsius was the last to go. He waited until I looked up, and then he said he would call again next week, and that he hoped the old gentleman would soon be himself.

I hated him, kneeling there. I hated all of them, and the old man most of all. And yet most nights that winter I had sat on the end of his bed saying the names of everyone in the house and the lane, quietly, over and over, until he slept. He thought it was a game we played. I never told anyone in the house I did it, because I did not want to be thanked for it.

The summons

The herald came before the floor was dry. The consuls summoned every senator to the Senate House at once, on business of the Republic. He moved on up the lane to say it at the next door, and my master stood in the doorway in his good toga and did not move.

"Don't go," Atilia said.

"I'm not saying I'll vote, I'm only saying that when a senator is summoned by the consul there's a, it isn't a question of choosing, Atilia, it's what the Senate is, and if nobody went..."

"If you go, and you vote the wrong way, and they take the house, we lose the house and still owe your father's debts. Forty-one thousand sesterces. Forty, I paid the tile man. And if you don't go, they'll have your name as a man who stayed away." She heard herself, and her voice went flat. "So go, I don't know, go and vote with whoever has the most soldiers."

"That isn't how it's meant to work."

"No," she said, and went back to the old man.

She was twenty-four, and she kept the accounts because he could not bear to. She was the only one in the house besides me who knew about his face. She had never mentioned it to me, nor I to her; between us we managed him.

The Sacred Way

There were soldiers at every corner of the Forum. I had never seen soldiers inside the city, and neither had anyone else alive. It was against every law Rome had, and here they were on the temple steps with their boots off. They had made a fire out of somebody's shutters at the bottom of the Sacred Way and were cooking chickpeas in a helmet. An old woman was sweeping broken roof tiles into a heap outside her door, and every time she finished the heap a file of soldiers came by and walked through it, and she started again. I was frightened, and I could see from the way my master kept touching the fold of his toga that he was frightened too.

A centurion stopped us by the soldiers' fire. He was short and bow-legged, with a burn scar across the back of one hand, and he spoke the Latin of the hill country, slow and flat.

"Name."

"I'm a member of the Senate," my master said, "summoned this morning by the consuls, both of them, or one of them, the herald wasn't, I think it was both..."

"Aulus Sarrenus, senator, summoned by the consul Sulla," I said.

The centurion looked at me with interest. "You do the names, do you." I said I did.

"Who's that, then?" He pointed with his chin at a man going past with a tray.

"Glycon, who sells perfume by the Temple of Castor and cheats on the measure."

The soldiers by the fire laughed. The centurion pointed at a woman, and I named her and her husband; he pointed at a man in a striped tunic, and I named him and the money he owed. I was good at it, and I was showing off. My master had drifted a few steps away to greet a senator and was saying "Who is that?" to me under his breath, and I told him over my shoulder without taking my eyes off the centurion's chin.

"And him?"

A young man in a good cloak was turning quickly into the alley beside the basilica, keeping his face down. I knew the back of his head.

"Lucius Arvenius," I said. "His father's an eques, and he walks with the tribune's young men." I added the last part because I knew it, and because I wanted the centurion to see that I knew it.

Three soldiers went after him. They brought him back by the arms. When he pulled one arm free, a soldier hit him in the face with the flat of a shield, and he went down on his knees in the gutter between the fullers' pots that stand on every corner for passers-by to piss in. One of the pots went over. He knelt there in it with his good cloak soaking and blood coming out of his nose, and he looked up past the centurion and found me.

"Dama, you know me," he said. "Tell him I was buying fish, tell him I don't, I haven't seen him for days, I don't know where he is. Dama, tell him."

I could not think of anything to say that would be true and would also help, and so I said nothing at all.

"Where's the tribune?" the centurion asked him.

"I don't know."

"We'll see," the centurion said, without any particular feeling, and they took him away towards the Capitol with his feet dragging. I told myself they would ask him a few questions and let him go home, because a man who knows nothing is no use to anybody. I believed it all the way to the Senate House.

The doors

The doors of the Senate House stand open when the Senate sits, so that the people can see nothing is done in secret. That day the people were soldiers. We slaves waited at the foot of the steps with lamps for the walk home, because a session may run until sunset but not one moment past it, and this one would run long. Through the doors I could see the backs of the benches and, in the far chair, a man with very fair hair and a face blotched red and white like scalded skin. That was the first time I saw Sulla. He did not look like a man who had just taken a city; he looked bored, and a little ill.

I looked for Kerdon among the slaves on the steps, the way I always did. Kerdon was nomenclator to the tribune Sulpicius. We had learned our trade from the same old man, who hit us across the knuckles with a stick whenever we got a name wrong. He was not there, and neither, of course, was his master.

I could not hear the speeches. I heard the consul's voice going on, and the senators answering when they were called, the most senior first. A murmur went round the steps when the name of Marius was read out with the others. Marius had saved Rome from the northern tribes when I was small; everyone said so. Now they were going to make him a man that anyone might kill.

Only one voice carried to us clearly. It was an old man's voice, high and thin. It said they could show him all the soldiers they had and threaten him with death as much as they liked. He was an old man with very little blood left in him, and he would not call Marius an enemy of Rome to save it. A slave next to me said it was Scaevola, the augur. The consul let him finish and called the next name.

Then came the noise I had been waiting for all afternoon. It was the sound of the vote: hundreds of men in leather shoes getting up off wooden benches and crossing a stone floor to stand beside the man they agreed with. It went on for a long time, and the sun was already on the roofs of the shops opposite when it stopped. The consul said something short, and the slaves around me started lighting their lamps.

My master came down the steps with the others. His face was grey, and he walked straight past me, and I had to go after him and touch his arm so that he would know who I was.

"Twelve of them," he said. "Marius, and his son, and Cethegus, and Sulpicius, the tribune, a sitting tribune, Dama, they can't, he's sacred, his person is, or it was, I don't know what it is now. Anybody may kill them and the killer is not to be tried. Everyone walked. Scaevola sat where he was and they let him, he's very old, I don't suppose they'll, he's an augur." He stopped on the bottom step. "I walked with the others, and I didn't think about it until I was standing there."

We went home through the dark with our lamp. At the corner of our lane he said, without looking at me, "That young man this morning, the one the soldiers took. Did you know him?"

"No, master."

I said it easily. He had no way of knowing otherwise, and he nodded, and we went in.

The Rock

They found Sulpicius some days later in the marshes near Laurentum, on the coast, hiding in a villa. His head came back to the city before the rest of him and was set up at the Rostra, the speakers' platform in the Forum where he had stood to propose his laws. It was smaller than I would have thought, and one of the eyes was not quite shut.

I did not know yet who had told them where to look. I found out in the Forum the same morning, because Kerdon came running across it to me wearing a freedman's felt cap.

It was too big for him and came down over his ears. He grabbed both my hands and laughed into my face, and he smelled of wine.

"Look at me, Dama, look, I'm free, the consul freed me himself, in front of everybody. I'm a citizen, I'm a Cornelius, I'll have a vote, I'll get a shop, I'll buy you off your master and free you, I mean it." He was crying. "He'd have sold me, Dama, Sulpicius would, the minute I was worth more sold, you know he would, I didn't owe him anything. I know where he goes. I've always known where he goes."

I said I was glad for him. I envied him so much that I could hardly look at the cap.

The lictors came for him while he was still holding my hands. They carried their bundles of rods on their shoulders. The one in front read out the consul's judgement in the voice they use for everything: the slave had earned his freedom by his information, and the freedman, for betraying his master, was to be thrown from the Rock. He smiled at the lictor and waited for the rest.

I followed them up the Capitol with the crowd; I could not have stayed away. At the top, where the cliff drops to the roofs below, he understood, and he fought them. He lost the cap. He fell down and they picked him up, and he wet himself, and some of the crowd laughed at that and a boy threw a cabbage stalk. He was screaming my name. He was screaming at me to tell them who he was, as though there had been some mistake about his name and I could put it right by saying it.

I stood with the others and kept my mouth shut, and they threw him off.

Afterwards I went down to the bottom to look. A slave from one of the houses was already there with a handcart, and the cap was lying in the street a little way off. I did not pick it up. The first thing I felt, before the sickness came, was gladness that it was him and not me, and I have never managed to make that feeling into anything better than it was.

The purse

That night I sat on the end of old Spurius's bed and said the names: the house and the lane, and then the baker and his wife and the new girl. He slept, eventually, with his mouth open, and I went on for a while after he was asleep, because I did not want to stop.

When I came out my master was standing in the hall with a lamp in one hand and a small leather purse in the other.

"A centurion brought this to the door," he said. "A short man with a burn on his hand, I think, or a scar, I didn't, you know I don't notice faces. He said it was for the boy with the good eye. He said the young man had been asked his questions and let go, and would be limping for a while, and that the consul's people might want to borrow the boy again." He weighed the purse in his palm and did not offer it to me. "I said there must be some mistake. I said my boy didn't know the young man."

The lamp shook a little. He was looking straight at me, trying to read my face, and I knew that all he could see was a smudge in the shape of one, the same as everybody's.

"Dama," he said. "Who was that young man?"
