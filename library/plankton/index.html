The boat is smaller than she remembers.

It is ten to five on a Monday morning in June, and Nessa Corrigan is standing at the top of the pier in Rossaveal with a rucksack and a tin of watercolours, looking down at the one boat in Ireland she promised herself she would never set foot on again. In the rucksack there is a contract for four months of work on it. The contract has his name on the second page.

Plankton

Part one, Bloom. Chapter one. Rossaveal.

They were twenty-four, and it was this boat. It was three in the morning in the wet lab and they were the two newest students on the survey, with a net just up and a white tray of seawater between them under the lamp. He held the light. She had the pencil, because she could draw and he couldn't, and he told her what he was looking at in a low voice so as not to wake the bunks. At some point he moved the lamp a few inches to the left without being asked. Her hand had been throwing a shadow across the page and she hadn't noticed it herself. She remembers thinking that she would need to be careful with this person, and then not being careful at all.

The pier

The pier at this hour is the grey of pencil lead, and wet, though it hasn't rained. There is a gull on every bollard. Halfway down, a man in a yellow jacket is hosing the deck of the island ferry and doesn't look up as she passes. The Muirín is tied at the far end, twenty-one metres of her, with a white hull that has gone the colour of old teeth and a single lit window in the wheelhouse.

Behind the glass Bríd Mulkerrin is reading the forecast with a mug in her other hand. She owns the boat and was skippering it long before Nessa first came aboard. She looks down and sees Nessa on the pier. She lifts the mug an inch. Nessa lifts her hand, and Bríd goes back to the forecast, and that is the whole of the welcome. Nessa is grateful for it. She spent the taxi ride preparing an answer to how have you been, and now she doesn't have to use it.

The job came in March, in an email from the contracts office of the institute in Galway. A summer plankton survey required an illustrator, to paint specimens on board over four trips between June and September. Your name has been put forward, the email said. It did not say by whom, and she did not write back to ask. It gave a day rate that was nearly twice what she gets for beetles. She had been painting beetles for a field guide since the spring before, in the back bedroom, from specimens that arrive in the post on pins. She said yes the same afternoon. At dinner she told Tom it was the money. Tom said it sounded great, and asked who would be running the thing, and she said some fella from the institute. Then he asked if she would need the good waterproofs, which are his, and she said she supposed she would.

She climbs down the iron ladder in the pier wall with the rucksack on both shoulders and the paint tin under her arm, and steps across onto the deck. It moves a little under her and her knees give with it. She goes below, since there is nowhere else to put a bag. The galley smells of diesel and toast, as it always did. Packie Conneely is at the stove with his back to the door, stirring a pot with a wooden spoon. He is the engineer and also the cook, and he spent forty years on trawlers before this. He doesn't turn round. Is that Nessa Corrigan? he says. It is, she says. You'll have porridge, he says. He tells her it has been going since Thursday and he has only been adding to it, so that at this stage it is a living thing and should be treated with respect. She says she'll have a small bit. He says there is no small bit, and puts a full bowl on the table.

She opens the press over the kettle to find a mug for tea, and hers is on the shelf. It is blue enamel with a chip out of the rim. She doesn't need to turn it over to know there is an N scratched into the base. She did it with a dissecting needle the summer she was twenty-six, when she believed she would be working on this boat for the rest of her life. She stands with the door of the press open. Somebody has kept the mug here for five years. Either it has been washed and put back after every survey or it has not been touched at all. She can't tell which from looking and can't decide which she would prefer. In those five years he has not rung her once. That is fair. She asked him not to, in the note, and he has always been a person who does what he is asked. She takes down a white mug with the name of a net supplier on it and closes the press.

She is eating the porridge when a young woman comes down the steps backwards with a holdall and a cloth bag with large print across it that Nessa takes care not to read. This is Orla Deasy, who is twenty-five and from Cork and is the survey's PhD student. She says all of that herself before she has put the bags down. She says she did her degree in physics and came over to the sea afterwards, and this is her first time out. She took two tablets for seasickness in the car and so far all they have done is dry her mouth. You're the artist, she says. I have your seaweed book. I had it on a field course and I got sand in it, I'm sorry. Nessa says that is what it's for. Orla sits down across from her. Dara says you used to be on the boat, she says. Years ago. He says you did your PhD on it.

Most of one, Nessa says. Orla waits for the rest of the sentence. When it doesn't come she nods, and Nessa watches her decide to leave it alone, which is more tact than she had been expecting. Orla tells her about the bunks instead. Nessa is in the lower one on the port side, and Orla is above her and has been told that she talks in her sleep. Overhead the engine starts, and the bowls shiver on the table. Nessa finishes the porridge. She washes her bowl and the white mug and dries them and puts them away, and then there is no more reason to stay below.

Dara Furey is on the aft deck with a clipboard, checking something on the winch, and his back is to her. He is thirty-five now. He is wearing a navy wool hat that is either the same one or its replacement, and there is a pencil behind his ear. He turns to call something up to Bríd in the wheelhouse and sees Nessa in the doorway. Nessa, he says. Dara, she says. You got down all right, he says. She says she did. He looks at the clipboard and then at her again. He says her bench is set up on the port side of the wet lab, where the light is better in the mornings. If anything on it is in the wrong place she is to move it. She says thanks. We'll be on station one at twenty past seven, he says. Wind's northwest, force three, and it should hold like that till Wednesday. His hand goes up to the pencil and finds it still there. Then Bríd calls down for the stern line and he goes to let it off.

Station one

They go out past the end of the pier into a morning that has no edges yet. The sun is up somewhere behind the land, but the light comes from the whole sky at once, a pale even grey with yellow under it. The sea takes the same colour and holds it flat. To the south-west the islands lie low along the horizon, the nearest one dark and the others fainter behind it. Nessa stands at the rail and looks at the water alongside. It is green and clouded, thick with whatever is growing in it, as it gets for a few weeks at this time of year. If she were painting it she would have to put the green down first and then dirty it. A fulmar keeps pace with the boat for a while on stiff wings, a hand's width above the surface, and then tilts away.

The wet lab is a low room at the stern with a steel sink and a door that hooks open onto the deck. Her bench runs under the port window. It has a raised lip around the edge to stop things sliding, with a microscope bolted down at one end and a lamp on a jointed arm. Somebody has cut a square of rubber matting for the place where her water jars will go. She unpacks the tin: the pans of colour, two sable brushes, pencils, a block of smooth paper taped at the corners. Her hands put each thing where it used to go. On the opposite bench there is a crate of empty sample jars with the labels already written, the station and the date, with a blank left for the depth. The handwriting is his. He still crosses his sevens. She reads the labels down one side of the crate and finds nothing in them but numbers.

At twenty past seven the engine drops to a mutter and Bríd holds the boat where it is. Nessa goes to the door of the lab. Dara is at the rail with the disk, which is white and the size of a dinner plate, weighted underneath, on a rope marked off in metres with coloured tape. He lowers it over the side and pays the rope out hand over hand. Orla stands beside him with the clipboard held against her chest. The disk goes down white, and then green. For a while it is a pale shape that might be the disk or might be the eye wanting it, and after that it is gone. He stops. He looks at the tape nearest his hand. Eleven metres, he says. He says it out loud to the water. It has been said out loud at this spot every month since 1988 by whoever was holding the rope, and Orla writes it down. For four years the person writing it down was Nessa. He doesn't look round at the door, and she finds that her own lips have moved.

The net goes down after it. It is a long cone of fine mesh on a steel ring, with a jar screwed to the narrow end. The winch lowers it most of the way to the seabed and brings it slowly up again. It comes over the rail streaming. Orla unscrews the jar with both hands and holds it up to the sky. The water in it is the colour of weak tea and it is moving, full of specks that flick and stop and flick. She carries it into the lab at arm's length and sets it down by the microscope. Nobody has told her what the counts are for, this year, and she doesn't ask. She draws up a little of the jar in a pipette and lets it out into a glass dish.

She has not looked down a microscope at anything alive in five years. The beetles are dead when they reach her, and they hold still. These don't. The dish is full of copepods, each one smaller than a grain of rice and clear as glass, crossing the light in short jerks with its two long antennae held out to the sides. Inside each there is a single drop of oil, amber, lit from below. She had forgotten the oil. She moves the lamp a few inches to the left, because her hand is throwing a shadow, and picks up the pencil. The first line she draws is wrong and the second is right. When Nessa looks up again there are three copepods on the page and twenty minutes have gone, and her tea is cold in the white mug.

Orla has started her count at the other bench, with a clicker in her left hand. She says that station one is the clearest of the eight, Dara told her, and the disk nearly always goes deepest here. Nessa says it does most years, although two winters ago they couldn't get out to it at all in February with the weather. Orla's clicker stops. How do you know that? she says. I must have read it somewhere, Nessa says, and bends to the eyepiece again.

She did read it. Every January the institute publishes the year's results from this line of stations, forty pages that are mostly tables. Every January she prints them at the library and reads all of it. Then she paints one copepod in the margin of the first page, small, from memory. There are five of these reports in the bottom drawer of her desk in Dublin, under a box of pen nibs. Tom has never seen them. He believes she left science because it bored her, and she has let him believe it, because he is kind and it is an easy thing to believe.

Night

The day goes by in stations. The second is at one o'clock and the third at seven in the evening, and between them she draws and Orla counts and Dara is mostly somewhere else. He comes into the lab twice. The first time he takes a jar from the crate and goes out again. The second time he stands at Orla's shoulder and reads down her count sheet and says, that's good. On his way to the door he passes Nessa's bench and sees the page, the three copepods and the four she has added since, and he stops. She keeps her eye to the lens. That one in the corner is a female, he says. I know she is, Nessa says. He nods, and then the doorway is empty. Orla breathes out. Is he always like that? she says. Nessa says she wouldn't know.

Dinner is a stew at half past six, and Dara takes his up to the wheelhouse so that Bríd can come down and eat hers at the table. Afterwards Nessa goes out to the stern with her phone and sits on an upturned fish box. It does not get properly dark out here in June. At eleven there is still a band of light along the north, greenish, and the sea under it is paler than the sky. The phone has one bar of signal and will have none by morning. There is a message from her mother in Ballina that says, Are you eating? There is one from Tom. How's the fella from the institute? Is he all right to work for? She types fine. She looks at it and deletes it. She types grand, which means the same and sounds like less, and sends it.

He writes back almost at once. Good. The waterproofs are in the side pocket of the rucksack if you haven't found them. Mind yourself. There are three words that this phone has been set never to show her, in any app that will allow it. She chose them five years ago, in the week she stopped sleeping, and she has not opened the list since. Orla's cloth bag is hanging on a hook inside the lab door with the print turned outwards. Nessa has walked past it all day with her eyes on the floor. She writes Yes to her mother. Then she puts the phone in her pocket and stays where she is until the cold comes through the fish box.

In the lower bunk she lies on her back with her eyes open. Above her Orla is talking in her sleep, though not in words. Five years ago, in April, in the kitchen of a flat they had over a bicycle shop in Galway, Dara asked her a question and she said yes. She meant it. Three weeks later she left a note on the same table and took the morning train to Dublin with one bag. The note said she had stopped loving him. It was the only thing she could think of that he would accept without an argument, and he accepted it. Her thesis went into a box. The box has moved with her through two flats and she has never cut the tape.

At midnight she gives up and climbs to the wheelhouse in her socks. Bríd is in the chair with one hand on the wheel and the radar turning green in the dark beside her. She is sixty-three and she doesn't look round. You're not asleep, Bríd says. No, Nessa says. You'll be called at one for the net anyway, Bríd says. Nessa says she knows. She stands by the chart table and looks ahead. After a while she says the boat is looking well. Bríd says it is looking its age. Nessa says she was surprised, back in March, that the institute had the money for an illustrator this year, when it never had before. She has made it sound like a remark.

Bríd doesn't take her eyes off the water. The institute hadn't, she says. It's out of his own grant. She says he filled in the charter sheet for the summer at her kitchen table in January, the same as every year. On the line for crew he wrote illustrator and then a name after it, in block capitals so the office couldn't get it wrong. She says she passed no comment then and she is passing none now. He asked for you by name, she says. That's all I know about it. Nessa looks at the small light over the compass, and at Bríd's hand on the wheel, and Bríd doesn't seem to need an answer from her.

Nessa has known that since March, and she has not once let herself know it.

End of chapter one
