The boat is smaller than she remembers.

It is ten to five on a Monday morning in June, and Nessa Corrigan is at the top of the pier in Rossaveal with a rucksack, a tin of paints and a bag of sandwiches she bought at the station in Dublin and didn't eat. She has been awake since half two. Down at the far end is the Muirín, and on the Muirín for the next four months, on and off, is a man she lived with for four years and left with a note on the kitchen table. She is hungry and she needs to pee.

Plankton

Part one, Bloom. Chapter one. Rossaveal.

They were twenty-four, and it was this boat, the wet lab, three in the morning. He had a cold. He sniffed every few seconds over the tray of seawater. In the end she told him to blow his nose, for God's sake, and he pulled the neck of his T-shirt up over his face and used that. She said it was disgusting. He said it was his T-shirt. Then he held the lamp while she drew, and when her hand threw a shadow on the page he moved the lamp a few inches to the left without being asked, and went on sniffing. She went back to her bunk at five and lay there wanting him, a man who had just wiped his nose on his own clothes.

The pier

The pier is wet though it hasn't rained, and it smells of diesel and the ice plant. Somebody has left a pair of yellow rubber gloves on a bollard, inside out, the fingers sticking up. Halfway down, a young lad in hi-vis is coiling a hose on the deck of the island ferry and eating a breakfast roll at the same time. The Muirín is tied up at the very end. Her white hull has gone the colour of old teeth. There is one light on, in the wheelhouse, where Bríd Mulkerrin is standing with a mug in one hand and her phone held out at arm's length in the other. Bríd looks down and lifts the mug about an inch. Nessa waves back with her whole arm, and she is still waving after Bríd has gone back to the phone.

The job came in March, in an email from the institute in Galway. A summer plankton survey needed an illustrator on board, four trips between June and September, to paint the specimens alive. Her name had been put forward. The day rate was nearly twice what she gets for beetles. She paints those for a field guide at a desk in the back bedroom, from dead ones that come in the post on pins. She said yes the same afternoon. At dinner she told Tom it was for the money, and when he asked who was running it she said some fella from the institute. He asked would she want the good waterproofs. They are his. She said she supposed she would, and he got up and went out to the car for them that minute, with his dinner going cold, and she sat at the table and finished hers.

She goes down the iron ladder in the pier wall with the rucksack dragging her backwards and steps across onto the deck. Below, the galley smells of toast and bleach. Packie Conneely is at the cooker with his phone propped against the salt, reading the death notices out loud to nobody. He finishes a woman from Lettermore, peacefully, in the loving care of the staff, and says, Is that Nessa Corrigan? It is, she says. You'll have porridge, he says. It has been on the go since Thursday and he has only been adding to it, so at this stage it's a living thing and you'd want to treat it with respect. She says just a small bit. He says there's no small bit, and puts down a bowl that would do two men, and goes back to a man in Spiddal who will be sadly missed.

She opens the press over the kettle for a mug and her mug is in it. Blue enamel, a chip out of the rim. She doesn't have to turn it over to know there's an N scratched into the bottom, because she did it with a dissecting needle the summer she was twenty-six. What she feels first is pleased. Somebody kept it. He hasn't rung her once in five years. She asked him not to, in the note. She takes down a white mug with the name of a net company on it and shuts the press harder than she means to. Mind the hinge, Packie says.

Orla Deasy comes down the steps backwards with a holdall and a cloth bag with big writing across it, which Nessa makes a point of not reading. Orla is twenty-five and from Cork and the survey's PhD student, and she says all that before the bags are down. She did physics first, she says, so she's a bit of a fraud, and this is her first time out on anything bigger than a ferry, and she took two seasick tablets in the car and all they've done is dry out her mouth, is that normal? Nessa says she doesn't know. You're the artist, Orla says. I have your seaweed book. I dropped it in a rock pool in Kerry, I'm sorry, it's gone kind of wavy. Nessa says that's what it's for. Dara says you used to be on the boat, Orla says. He says you did your PhD on it.

Most of one, Nessa says. Orla waits for the rest. When it doesn't come she starts on the bunks instead. Nessa is in the lower one and Orla is over her, and she should warn her that she talks in her sleep, her housemates recorded her once, it was mostly about a bus. Nessa eats the porridge and listens to her say Dara. Dara says the forecast is good till Wednesday. Dara says the bunks are damp but not that damp. By the fifth Dara she has decided they are sleeping together. She looks at Orla properly, at the gap in her front teeth and the good skin, and thinks, well, she's young, that's all she has going for her. Overhead the engine starts and the bowls shiver on the table.

On deck the hatch to the engine room is open and Dara Furey comes up out of it as she passes, head and shoulders first, with diesel on his hands and a pencil behind his ear. He is thirty-five now. His hair is cut very short and there is grey in it at the sides, and he has got heavier, and it suits him. Nessa, he says. Dara, she says. He wipes his hands on a rag and looks at her as if she were a delivery. You got the train, he says. She says she got the train and a taxi out from Galway. Put the taxi on a claim form, he says. Bríd has them. Your bench is port side. The light's better there before ten. I cut a mat for your jars. She says the mat is fine. He says station one at twenty past seven, northwest three, holding till Wednesday. His hand goes up to the pencil and finds it still there. Then he turns to shout up at Bríd about the stern line, and she looks at the back of his neck, already burnt in June, and would like to put her face into it.

Station one

Nessa stands at the rail on the way out. The sea is flat and thick with the bloom, a dirty green. If she had to paint it she would put the green down first and then muddy it with the grey she uses for beetle legs. At the harbour mouth a long swell comes in under the flat surface and her stomach rolls over once. She holds the rail and breathes through her nose and is not sick. In the wet lab her bench is under the port window, with the microscope bolted down at one end and the lamp on its jointed arm. The mat is a square cut out of an old deck mat with a knife, and none of the edges are straight. Everything in the room smells of formalin, and under the formalin, fish.

At twenty past seven the engine drops and Bríd holds them on the spot. Dara is at the rail with the Secchi disk, and Orla is beside him with the log sheet in a plastic sleeve. He lets the rope out hand over hand. The disk goes down white, then green, then it is a smudge, and then Nessa can't see it, and she says, Eleven. Out loud, from the lab door. Orla turns round and says, Sorry? Nothing, Nessa says. Dara is looking at the tape on the rope by his hand and he doesn't turn. Eleven metres, he says, to the water, and Orla writes it down. For four years the person writing it down was Nessa.

The bongo nets go down after it, two of them side by side on a frame, and come back up streaming. Dara asks for the flowmeter. Orla reads it off and he says it's low. There's weed in the impeller, he says. You check that before it goes in, not after. Every station. Orla says sorry, and then sorry again, and he has already gone to wash down the cod end. Nessa takes the jar from her and splits the sample and puts a tray of it under the lens. She hasn't looked at anything alive down a microscope in five years. The beetles come dead, and they hold still. These are copepods, hundreds of them, each one smaller than a grain of rice and clear as glass, crossing the light in jerks with their antennae out. In the middle of each one there is a drop of oil, amber, lit from underneath. She had forgotten the oil. She is happy. Her hand is throwing a shadow, so she moves the lamp to the left and starts. The first line is wrong. The second one is right.

Orla is counting at the other bench with a tally counter, and saying that Dara says the disk nearly always goes deepest at station one. Nessa says it does most years, except two winters ago when they couldn't get out to it at all in February. The counter stops. How do you know that? Orla says. I must have read it somewhere, Nessa says. She did read it. Every January the institute puts out the year's results for this line, forty pages of tables, and she prints it in the library in town and reads every table. Then she paints a copepod in the margin of the first page, small, from memory, and puts it in the bottom drawer of her desk. There are five of them in there. Tom has never seen them. He thinks she left science because she got bored of it, and she has let him think so.

The second station is the same as the first, and so is the third, and in between there is nothing to do but wait and rinse things. In the afternoon she and Orla are on the aft deck hosing the nets down, working the weed and the jelly out of the mesh with their fingers. Dara is up on the wheelhouse roof with Bríd's toolbox, doing something to the radio aerial. Orla turns the hose down so it isn't so loud. Can I ask you something, she says, and you can tell me to feck off. Were you two, like. Before. Nessa laughs. God, no, she says. Well. For a bit. It was nothing, we were kids, it was a student thing, everyone was with everyone on these surveys, it didn't mean anything.

Dara comes down the ladder from the roof with the toolbox. He puts it on the hatch cover and stands there wiping his hands. Don't mind her, Orla, he says. Nessa doesn't stay for things. Get the rest of that jelly out before it dries on, it's a bastard to shift. Then he goes forward with the toolbox. Orla turns the hose off. Oh my God, she says. I'm so sorry. Nessa says it's fine. Four years, in a flat over a bicycle shop in Galway. She takes the hose off Orla and goes on washing the net, and she is still washing it a long time after it is clean.

Night

At dinner Orla's phone buzzes against the salt. It's Aoife, she says, my girlfriend, she thinks I'm going to be lost at sea, and she turns the phone round to show them a photo of a fat brown dog in a raincoat. Packie asks what kind of dog. Orla says some kind of a terrier and some kind of a disaster, and Packie holds out his hand for the phone to have a proper look. Nessa eats her stew. She had built a whole thing out of how many times a girl said a man's name, and the girl has a girlfriend in Cork and a dog in a raincoat. Dara has taken his dinner up to the wheelhouse so that Bríd can come down, and Bríd eats hers reading the forecast and says the stew wants salt.

Afterwards Nessa sits on an upturned fish box at the stern with her phone. At eleven there is still light along the north and the sea is paler than the sky. There is a message from her mother in Ballina that says, Are you eating? There is one from Tom. How's the fella from the institute? Is he all right to work for? She types fine, and deletes it. She types grand and sends that. Then she sends another one. Bit of a dry shite to be honest. Tom writes back straight away. Ha. Waterproofs in the side pocket if you haven't found them. Mind yourself x. She writes Yes to her mother. The phone is set to hide three words from her, in every app that will let it, and she hasn't looked at the list in five years.

In the lower bunk she lies on her back. Above her Orla is talking in her sleep, not in words, just sounds. The bunk smells of somebody else's feet. Five years ago in April, in the kitchen of the flat in Galway, Dara asked her a question and she said yes, and meant it. Three weeks later she left a note on the same table and got the early train to Dublin with one bag. The note said she had stopped loving him. It was the only thing she could think of that he wouldn't argue with, and he didn't argue. Her thesis went into a box. She has moved the box twice and never cut the tape. She thinks about what he said on the deck. He is right, and she would like to hit him for it.

At midnight she gives up and climbs to the wheelhouse in her socks. Bríd is in the chair with one hand on the wheel and the radar sweeping round green beside her. A podcast about a murder in Kerry is going on her phone with the sound nearly off. You're not asleep, Bríd says. No, Nessa says. You'll be called at one for the net anyway, Bríd says. I know, Nessa says. After a while she says the boat is looking well. Bríd says the boat is looking old, because it is old, and it needs a new prop shaft she can't pay for. Nessa says she was surprised, back in March, that the institute had money for an illustrator this year. She has been practising it since the taxi.

The institute hasn't money for toilet roll, Bríd says. It's out of his own grant. He did the charter sheet at my kitchen table in January, same as every year, and on the line for crew he wrote illustrator and then your name, in big capitals, so the office couldn't get it wrong. I told him he was an eejit. He said he knew he was. Bríd keeps her eyes on the water. He asked for you by name, she says. Nessa can't think of one thing to say. The email in March said her name had been put forward and nothing about who by. She knew who by. She knew it the minute she read it, and she said yes the same afternoon, and she has been telling herself since then that it was for the money.

Bríd turns the podcast off. There's another thing, she says, and I'll tell you because it's money, and money is my business and nobody else's on this boat. He paid me for the four trips up front. The whole summer, in January. He never does that, I've to chase him every year. And he told me not to keep the boat for him after September. Not next summer, not the winter stations, nothing. Give the dates to the fisheries crowd, he said, they've been on at you for years. Nessa asks why. I asked him why, Bríd says, and he told me to mind my own business, and he has never once spoken to me like that. She looks at Nessa for the first time since she came up. So you can ask him yourself, she says. You've till September. The radar goes round. Down on the deck a door bangs, and Dara's voice comes up through the floor, saying something to Packie about the one o'clock net, and then he laughs. Bríd lifts the forecast off the chart table to read it under the lamp. There is a letter under it, folded in three and opened out again, on thick paper with a blue crest at the top. It is in a language Nessa can't read, except for his name, Dr Dara Furey, and one line near the bottom she can: 1. oktober. Bríd puts the forecast back down on top of it without looking.
