In the far north of Norway the sun had set for the winter, and on the second morning without it they found Isak Berg. He lay under the loading ramp of the fish plant with his coat zipped to the chin.

By the time the police car came down the hill, the town had its story. Isak drank. Isak took the harbour road home from the bar on Saturdays, past the plant. This Saturday he had gone down in the snow by the ramp and stayed down, and the cold had finished it. People told it to each other at the gate with their hands pushed into their armpits. They had buried men out of that story before.

The Cold Store

Chapter one. Minus Twenty-Eight.

06:05. Minus 16

Gunn Hjelle was awake when the phone rang, sitting on the edge of the bath in her coat with the light off. She was the police crime-scene technician for that whole stretch of coast, and she let it ring long enough to sound as if it had woken her.

"It's Hallvard. I'm down at the plant. By the ramp, where the lorries back in. There's been, well. Ilir rang me, he opens up there. It's Isak Berg." The chief of police breathed down the line. "Hege's on her way. Doctor Aune. I'd have said it was one for Hege, mostly, sign it and done. Only you like to be rung."

"Is he dead, Hallvard."

"That's what I'm telling you."

Her car stood behind the old bank, where she rented the flat upstairs, frozen to the ground on the sea side, and she kicked the door along its seal until it gave. Inside, it still smelled of enzyme cleaner from Saturday night, and under the cleaner, faint and sweet, of the linoleum in Hilde Nygård's kitchen. The old woman had lain on that floor from some time in October until a neighbour noticed the post piling up. Her family were in Bergen and had said on the phone that they could not face it. So on Saturday Gunn had parked streets away from the house, carried her own buckets in by the back door and done the floor on her knees with a paint scraper, the windows open to the frost. Her knees felt it now on the clutch.

In the boot were her scene kit, two aluminium cases and a holdall. Before anything else she opened the small case under the courtesy light and checked the brushes, each in its paper sleeve, each seal whole. She had bought them new in Tromsø in September. Each one went in the bin after a single scene, and the young constable, who had seen the invoices, thought she was mad.

The plant stood out on its concrete apron over the water with every floodlight burning. On the sound the coastal ferry slid north without stopping, lit from bow to stern, and the sea under it smoked. At midday there would be an hour of colour low in the south, the thin pink of a presumptive test that might be blood and might be rust, and then the dark again until the end of January.

06:40. Minus 17

The crowd at the plant gate was the night shift in white overalls and hairnets, women in parkas over their nightclothes, and a boy on a kick-sled who should have been in bed. Iselin Mo had run tape from the gatepost to a lamp standard and was walking up and down in front of it with her notebook open, taking names that nobody had offered.

"Inner cordon and outer cordon," she said when she saw Gunn. "I did two. You always do two. And I've got a log going, with times, everyone in and out."

"Who's been in."

"Ilir found him. He opens up at five. Then Hallvard. Then me, but only to the edge of the ramp. And Hege."

"Hege's in now."

"She was here before me. She lives just up there." Iselin pointed with her pen at a house on the hill.

Under the ramp, in the angle between the concrete and the drift, the district doctor was kneeling beside the body with her bag open on the snow and her bare fingers on the dead man's neck.

Gunn got into her overall at the back of the car, hood, mask, overshoes, two pairs of nitrile gloves, and walked out along the footprints that were already there, setting her own boots into them.

"Hege. Come out the way you went in."

Hege Aune looked up. She was a heavy woman of sixty with frost in her fringe, the only doctor on that stretch of coast. "He's dead, Gunn."

"Out."

The doctor got up off her bad knees, came back along the trodden line and pulled her glove on with her teeth. At the tape she said, "Hypothermia. Alcohol and cold. I'll sign by nine." She looked back at the ramp. "They go to sleep. It's a kind death, whatever people think."

"Don't sign anything."

Hallvard Grøtte had come up behind them, a big grey man in a police parka that would not close over his stomach. He had grown up along the road from the Bergs. "Gunn. The school bus. It comes along here at seven thirty, and there's kids on it who knew him, and his cousin's girl, so." He left it there for her to finish.

"So you want him in the van."

"I'd want that, yes."

"Then go up and stop the bus. I need a tent and lights and two hours."

He rubbed his face with his whole hand, from the forehead down, and kept the hand over his mouth while he talked. "He was back from the rigs in the spring. Money. You'll have seen him in the bar, maybe. And Saturday there was the business with Ilir, a stupid business, and half the town saw him go out at one with no legs under him. I'm only saying what's there."

Gunn was already lifting the tent bag out of the boot. After a while she heard his car door, and then its lights were climbing the hill towards the bus.

Ilir Krasniqi stood by himself at the corner of the plant on the water side, past the reach of the floodlights, in work overalls with a ring of keys on his belt. The crowd at the gate had left a space around him that it left around no one else. When Gunn looked at him he looked straight back and nodded once, a man who expected to be questioned and had decided to be ready.

07:10. Minus 17

The tent went up on the landward side, between the ramp and the gate. She photographed from the gate inwards in the order she had been taught in a classroom in Oslo, wide, then middle, then close, with a scale in every close frame: the trodden line, the dents of Hege's knees, the ramp, and the drift that Sunday's northerly had packed under it.

Isak Berg lay face down along the foot of the drift with his arms under him and his knees drawn up, a big man in a dark work jacket zipped to the top. Snow had crusted on his back and on the backs of his legs. His hair was white.

She knelt and brought the torch in close without touching. The white in his hair had not fallen there. It had grown, needles of frost standing straight out from every hair, from his eyebrows and from the stubble along his jaw, fine and stiff as the squirrel hair of her own brushes, the frost that builds in still air on the walls of a freezer room. A wind strips that off in minutes. All Sunday there had been a hard northerly.

Among the needles behind his left ear lay a few flat clear flakes the size of a fingernail. She lifted one with tweezers and turned it in the torchlight until the growth rings showed. A fish scale. She bagged it, sealed it and wrote the time across the seal.

She heard Hjørdis Berg before she saw her: felt slippers on the foreshore stones under the apron, on the sea side, where Iselin had run no tape. The mother came up the slipway in a pink fleece dressing gown over a nightdress, her grey hair flattened on one side from the pillow, and she was already pulling the dressing gown off her shoulders.

"He hasn't got his hat," she said. "He never takes his hat."

Gunn stood up into her path. Hjørdis tried to go round her, and Gunn stepped with her, and the dressing gown, half off one shoulder, dragged across the front of Gunn's overall and left it furred with pink.

"I'm putting this on him. He's cold. Get out of my way."

"You can't touch him."

"He's my son."

Gunn took the dressing gown out of her hands. It came easily, because Hjørdis had not imagined anyone would do that. Under it was a cotton nightdress with short sleeves. Her arms were thin and blotched with cold, and one slipper had come off on the slipway, so that she stood on the concrete in a grey sock with the snow soaking dark into it.

Gunn's first thought was for her overall. It had the woman's fibres all down the front now, and she would have to change it before she went near him again.

"If you put this on him," she said, "I can't tell what's yours from what's theirs. If somebody did this to him, you'll have cleaned up for them. Is that what you want?"

Hjørdis looked at her, and then past her at the jacket and the white hair. Her teeth had started to chatter, a hard fast rattle Gunn could hear over the generator for the lights.

Ilir had come along from the corner of the plant. He stopped well short of them and held out his own fleece, the company one.

"Not you," Hjørdis said. "Don't you come near me. Not you."

He stayed where he was with the fleece held out.

Gunn held the dressing gown at arm's length until Hjørdis took it back. She did not put it on. She found her slipper on the slipway by feel and went back along the stones the way she had come, in her nightdress, with the gown bundled against her chest, and Ilir walked a long way behind her with his fleece over his arm.

Gunn changed her overall in the tent. On the dead man's left sleeve, where Hjørdis had come closest, there was one pink fibre. She picked it off with the tweezers, bagged it, and wrote HB on the seal.

When Ilir came back he stopped outside the tent and spoke through the canvas. "I have walked behind her to her door. Her neighbour has taken her inside. I did not walk in front of her and I did not touch her, if you are asked."

07:55. Minus 17

The undertaker's van backed up to the tape. Up the hill the school bus sat where Hallvard had stopped it, engine running, its windows fogged blind.

She and Iselin and the undertaker's son laid a clean sheet on the snow beside him. "On three," Gunn said, and they took the shoulders and the hips and rolled him.

He came up off the snow in one piece, tipped on to his side and stayed there, rocking on the curve of his back until he settled. The knees stayed drawn up. The arms stayed raised and bent at the elbow in front of where his face would be, palms forward, fingers spread and a little hooked, held at the height a kneeling man holds his hands against a wall. The undertaker's son said "Jesus" and stepped off the sheet.

"Bag the hands," Iselin said. "Should we bag the hands? They always bag the hands first. And that's rigor, isn't it, they set like that in the cold."

Gunn set her thumb on his forearm through the sleeve and leaned. Nothing gave, at the elbow or anywhere. Under the cloth the arm had the density of the blocks of frozen saithe this plant sent south on pallets, cold through the coat and the muscle into the middle of him. The ones who died in the open froze at the hands and the face and stayed soft in the trunk for days.

The fingertips of both hands were raw. The pads had come away from every one of them, leaving the flesh underneath dark and dry with the ridges gone, and on the right hand the nails of the first two fingers were torn back to the quick.

"He'll have clawed at the ice," Hege said from the tape. "When he fell. They do."

Gunn looked at the place where he had lain. The snow there was new powder, dry enough to lift on a breath. There was no ice for anyone to claw.

Then she looked at the hollow he had left, and for the first time that morning she wanted, plainly and badly, to be wrong. A man who goes down warm gives his heat to the snow beneath him for an hour or two, and it melts and sets again into a glaze with his shape in it, hard enough to lift out whole. She had cast one once in Hedmark, in dental stone, for a court that wanted to hold it. Under Isak Berg there was nothing to cast. The powder beneath him was as loose as the powder around him. Whoever had laid him here had laid down something already as cold as the ground.

"Hallvard."

He had come back down the hill on foot. He walked the trodden line slowly and stood over the raised hands.

"He didn't die here," she said. "He froze somewhere else, kneeling, with his hands on something flat, and the skin stayed on it. Somebody brought him here afterwards. I'm stopping the certificate."

He looked at the hands for a long time. "You'll want to be sure, I'd think. Before. Before it goes round the town."

"Enough to stop Hege."

He turned and called across the apron, not loudly. "Ilir. A minute."

Ilir came to the tape and no further and stood with his hands at his sides.

"How cold do you keep the cold store," Gunn said.

"Minus twenty-eight." His Norwegian was careful and correct, every ending in place, with no dialect in it anywhere. "Minus twenty-eight degrees in the store. The blast freezer is colder, but a man cannot fit in the blast freezer. It is only trays."

He had not been asked that. Gunn saw Hallvard hear it.

"I want to see the store."

"It is not mine to open. It belongs to the company."

"I'll open it." A young woman was coming down the steps from the plant office in a company fleece, her hair scraped back, her eyes puffed. Inga Grøtte, the night manager. Hallvard's niece, whose father owned the plant and most of the boats that landed at it. "I wasn't on last night," she said. "Sundays are never me. I only came in because I heard. Uncle Hallvard, is it Isak?"

Hallvard nodded.

Inga put her hand flat over her mouth. The tips of her fingers were wrapped in blue plasters, the metal-detectable kind that food plants buy so that a lost one shows up on the line, grubby at the edges. She took the hand away. "Do what you need," she said to Gunn. "Ilir, give her the keys."

08:20. Minus 28

The cold store was at the back of the plant, past the filleting hall with its hoses coiled on the wet floor, behind an insulated door with a yellow stripe at waist height. Ilir found the key on his ring without looking. He put his shoulder to the door and it came off its seal with a long suck, and the cold rolled out white along the floor round their boots.

The air inside hurt. The hairs in Gunn's nose froze on the first breath and her eyes ran and the water thickened at the corners. Under caged bulbs stood steel racking twice a man's height, packed with waxed boxes stencilled for crab and for cod, and between the racks one aisle ran back into the dark. Everything wore the same white needles she had seen in his hair.

She wedged the door wide with a pallet and went in first, torch low. Hallvard came after her. Ilir stayed on the threshold where she had put him, and Inga stood behind him in the corridor holding her own elbows.

The floor was concrete under a skin of ice, scored where pallets had been dragged. Halfway along the aisle she found blood, round drops frozen black into the ice in a short line, then a broad smear on a rack upright at the height of a standing man's head, with a few short hairs set into it. She touched none of it. At the far end the racking stopped short of the wall and left a space the size of a small room, empty except for a crushed box and one work glove frozen palm up into a curl.

"Gunn," Hallvard said.

He was standing just inside the door, looking at the back of it.

The inside of the door was white-painted steel under frost. Where the release bar should have been, the bar that anyone shut inside a cold store pushes to get out, there was a square of bare metal with a bolt hole at each corner.

Below it, at the height of a kneeling man, the frost had been scraped away in long uneven streaks down to the paint, across a patch as wide as two spread hands. Stuck to the paint inside the patch were small grey curls and flaps that she knew before the torch reached them. They still had ridges on them. They were the pads of Isak Berg's fingers, and she could have rolled a print off any one of them.

Hallvard took his hat off and held it against his stomach in both hands.

"He was alive in here," he said. "He was in here alive. Trying the door."

"Yes."

"How long, Gunn."

"Long enough to use all ten."

Hallvard put his hat back on and turned round. Ilir stood in the doorway with the keys in his hand, holding his face still.

"Ilir," Hallvard said. "Who has a key to this door?"

"Me," Ilir said. "And..."

"Nobody," said Inga Grøtte from the corridor. "Only Ilir has the key to the cold store. Ilir's the only one."
