The Knife Table

Chapter one. The Knife Table.

His name was Aric Penn. He held a rope at the Marrow Ford ten years ago while the bridge burned under him, and the river took him when it fell. His widow walked two days through sleet to ask for the pension the Crown owes her, and a clerk with clean cuffs told her there was no record of her husband. Nobody kept lists at the Ford that winter.

So I keep them now, in the brown ink of that year and the slanting hand of the Ford's muster clerk, which I have studied until it lives in my wrist. Penn, Aric, ropeman, third company. I shake sand over the wet letters and blow, and for one breath a man who has been nothing for ten years exists again. If anyone at the pension office looks closely, it's a forgery, and the penalty for forging a Crown record is the hand that wrote it. I flex my fingers over the page and write the next name anyway.

Behind me, something taps the window. Three short, one long.

My pulse jumps against my collar. I lay the pen down slowly, as if the room were watching. It's the wind, nothing more. I know it isn't.

By the time I reach the glass there is only a shadow climbing away up the hill, and on the sill a letter folded small and sealed with plain grey wax, with no crest and no name on it. Inside, in a hand I would know in the dark, it says: Seren. Tell me what you're afraid of, and I'll tell you what I am.

I press the paper flat against my chest, where my heart is still going too fast. I have been answering that question for three years, to a man who lives somewhere in Hesk. Every word I have ever sent him is treason, and every word he sends back is the only thing in this cold place that feels like mine.

The Keeper

"You're still here."

The letter is in my sleeve before I turn. Ambrose Thale stands at the foot of the stairs with a lamp in his one hand, the empty sleeve of the other pinned across his chest, his shadow thrown huge up the vaulted shelves.

"The muster lists aren't finished," I tell him.

"The muster lists don't exist, Vey."

"That's why they aren't finished."

He comes down the last step and swings the lamp over my desk, over the brown ink and the widow's petition with its careful, unschooled capitals. Say it. Say I belong in a cell.

"That ink's too fresh," he says instead. "By midwinter the Ford clerk was thinning his with snow. Anyone who knows what they're looking at will see it."

"Nobody at the pension office knows what they're looking at."

"Somebody always does." He sets a fresh candle beside my elbow, hard enough to rattle the inkpot, and his voice drops. "Go to bed. I stood in that square once already for a Vey. I won't stand in it again to watch them take your hand."

He climbs the stairs and takes the light with him.

I'm the under-clerk of the Tally, twenty-four years old, with a memory that keeps every page I've read and a hand that can copy any writing I've seen twice. My leave is small, like most people's.

My father was Halden Vey, the envoy who made the Treaty of the Ford. The treaty failed and the Ford burned, and the court said he had hidden a trick in the drafting. They executed him in the spring, in the square under the palace windows, and I watched from the crowd because nobody thought to send me away. I was fourteen. I had made the fair copy of that treaty for him in my own hand. And you fell asleep before the last page. I have never said that part aloud to anyone.

Soup

The summons comes at dawn, from Lady Wick. Corisande Wick is the Chancellor of Callowmere, and after my father died she was the only person at court who still said my name as if it weren't a curse. She took me in. Whenever the weather turns, she sends soup down to the archive with a note in her looping hand: Eat something hot. Books won't keep you warm.

Her rooms smell of beeswax and orange peel. The fire is banked so high that the brass fender glows, and after the cold of the Tally the heat closes over my face like a hand. She rises in a rustle of plum silk, takes both my hands and turns them over, frowning at the ink.

"Seren. You've been up all night again."

"The lists, my lady."

"Your lists." She smiles at me with such fondness that my throat tightens. "Sit and eat. Then I'm going to ask you for something, and you're going to want to refuse."

The soup is leek and potato, thick with cream. I eat it because she watches until I do.

"The truce with Hesk ends at midwinter," she says, once the bowl is empty. "Twelve days. If there's no treaty by then, the armies at the Ford will stop watching each other across the water and cross it. Talks open tonight on Hollin Rock. Lord Fennick speaks for the Crown, and he needs a clerk to carry his papers. His own is in bed with a fever."

"And you thought of me."

"I thought of the only person in Callowmere who knows every treaty we've ever signed." She slides a folded paper across the table. "Your commission. Keep it on you. They're strict on the Rock about who may stand where."

I put it in my pocket without reading it. I read everything, and I should have read that. But she is watching me with her soft, worried look, and in this room I always feel warm and grateful and slightly too young for my own skin.

"My lady, the Hesk delegation will know my name. Every one of them lost someone at the Ford."

"Of course they'll know it." She tucks a loose strand of hair behind my ear, and her fingers are cool against my cheek. "That's why it must be you. Let them watch a Vey sit at that table and keep silent. It will do your father's memory more good than any speech."

"And if someone speaks to me?"

"You'll be behind a wall of boxes, and Aldous Fennick never lets anyone finish a sentence."

"But if they do, my lady."

"Then you'll pass Lord Fennick the right paper and let the men argue. Keep your eyes on your boxes and your hands in your lap, and in twelve days you'll come home to me." She touches my cheek. "Nobody is going to ask a clerk what she thinks."

No. Nobody ever has. I smile at her, and I hate how easily it comes.

The knife

When I get back to the Tally there's a box on my desk, long and narrow and lacquered black, with the Queen's seal on its clasp and no note. Thale is at the far end of the room, bent over a book he isn't reading.

Inside, on faded blue silk, lies my father's knife.

It's a plain table knife, the kind every envoy carries to Hollin Rock, with a handle of black horn worn pale where his thumb used to rest. The last time I saw it, it was in his belt on the morning they came for him. My knees give, and I sit down hard on the edge of the chair, and for a moment I can't get any air past the knot under my ribs.

I touch the handle. It's cold. Some foolish part of me expected it to be warm.

"Did the Queen send any word with it?" My voice comes out thin. "Anything at all?"

Thale turns a page. "The Queen doesn't explain herself to archivists."

"You saw who brought it."

"A page in royal livery. He set it down as if it might bite him, and he didn't stay to be thanked."

"Ten years, and she sends it today."

"Then perhaps today is the day it's for." He looks up at last, and whatever is in his face, he puts it away before I can name it. "Pack it with your papers. And keep it where you can reach it."

I wrap the knife in my scarf and lay it in my bag between the treaty copies. An apology, or a warning. My hand stays on the bundle long after it's packed, as though it might vanish if I let go.

The Rock

You reach Hollin Rock by rope. A flat-bottomed ferry hauls itself along a cable across the estuary, the tide dragging hard at its side and the fog closing in behind it until the shore is gone. Black water slides past a hand's breadth below the rail, so cold that it smokes. I keep my eyes on the cable and my bag clamped under my arm, the knife's weight against my ribs.

The ferry belongs to Ama Tullow, a broad woman in an oilskin coat who works the windlass alone. She hears our names and looks at me for a long moment.

"Vey," she says. "Halden Vey's daughter."

"Lord Fennick's clerk."

She leans into the windlass, and the ferry lurches against the current. "Hesk came across yesterday. Twelve of them, with their speaker, and a quartermaster who brought a crate of honey." She keeps her eyes on the fog. "Their speaker is Askell. They call him the Last Word. Once he says a thing at that table, he never takes it back."

"I know what they call him."

"Then stay behind your boxes, Vey. The last envoy who sat across from him went home with nothing, and was glad to get home at all."

Out in the fog a tide bell tolls once, low and slow, and the deck tilts under my boots. I'm not afraid of him. I'm not afraid of any of them. My fingers have gone white on the strap of the bag.

The island rises out of the grey, an old stone hall crouched on a black hump of rock, with the midwinter fair spread below it on the frozen strand, lantern light smeared gold across the ice and horses steaming in the cold. My brother Lark is somewhere in that crowd, and I don't look for him. If I see a face I love now, I'll get back on the ferry.

Leave

My father taught me the rules of leave at supper. Leave can only be given, never taken, and anything freely granted carries a small power the one who receives it can work, a lamp that needs no oil or a voice carried across a crowded hall.

Leave got by force or by a trick is hollow, and sooner or later it goes sour on whoever took it. It starts in the hands, which turn grey and dry as ash, and it can kill. He used to fold my small hands inside his when he told me that part, as though he could keep them clean.

The table

The hall on Hollin Rock is older than either kingdom. Its windows are tall and narrow and full of winter sea, and down its length runs one black table, so polished that every candle burns twice, once in the air and once in its surface. Callowmere sits on the south side and Hesk on the north. I sit at the very end of ours, behind a wall of document boxes, which is where clerks go.

Lord Fennick bends down to me before he rises. He's sixty-one, round and kind, with a voice made for reading out good news. "You know the custom, Miss Vey? Once my knife is down, blade towards me, nobody draws steel in this hall. If I pick it up again, Callowmere's part in the talks is over and we sail on the next tide."

"I know it, my lord." Walking away is built into the furniture. My father used to say that. Through the canvas of my bag, his knife presses against my hip.

"Nervous?"

"No, my lord." I can't feel my feet.

"Then you're braver than I am." He squeezes my shoulder. "Stay behind your boxes, and hand me the Salt Pans treaty if I start to look lost."

He lays down his knife, a good one with a silver handle. Across the table, the Hesk speaker rises to lay down his.

The hall goes quiet for Corvin Askell in a way it never went quiet for Fennick. He is younger than I expected, not yet thirty, tall and dark-haired, in plain black wool without a ring or a chain of office, as if he has never needed anything to prove what he is. He moves without hurry. Every face on our side follows him, wary and still, as if someone had drawn a blade and forgotten to sheathe it.

He sets his knife down with two fingers, the blade turned towards his own heart, and holds it there a moment longer than he needs to. Then he sits and turns his gaze on Fennick, and it is cold enough to lift the hair on my arms from the far end of the table.

He doesn't look at me once. I tell myself I'm glad.

I watch his hands instead, because hands are what I watch. His lie perfectly still on the black wood, long-fingered, with a thin white scar across one knuckle and a stain of ink along the side of the right forefinger, where a pen rests when a man writes a great deal and writes it fast. Something low in my stomach pulls tight, and I don't like it. Reports for his Regent. Cold little reports on how to break us.

Then Fennick begins, lifting a sheaf of letters, each one sealed with a river-lord's crest. "I speak today," he says, "with the leave of the river-lords of Callowmere, freely given by their own hands." He rests his palms on the letters, and I feel the air change, a pressure in my ears and a prickle at the back of my neck. His voice swells until it fills the hall without his raising it.

He gets as far as the second sentence. Then he stops, and looks down, and so does everyone else.

His hands are grey. It starts at the fingertips, where they touch the letters, and spreads while we watch, dry and pale as old ash, over the knuckles and up towards the wrists. Fennick tries to lift his hands from the paper, and they don't come. He finds me down the length of the table, and his eyes are wide and frightened as a boy's. "Miss Vey," he says, very quietly. "My hands." Then he folds forward onto the table, and the hall breaks apart.

Sour

Everyone shouts at once. Someone bellows for a physician, and somebody on our side says poison, and the word runs down our half of the table like fire along a fuse. Our steward is on his feet, his finger stabbing across the wood. "You brought a crate of honey across yesterday, a whole crate of it, and now this?"

"Honey doesn't do that to a man's hands," says Hesk's general, a broad woman in a soldier's coat with a voice like a boot on gravel. "Sit down."

"Then what does? Tell me, General, since you know so much about it!"

She doesn't answer him. She stares at Fennick's hands for a long moment with her jaw set hard, and then she sits back down and folds her arms.

I'm the only one on our side who doesn't move, because I'm watching the letters. They're the river-lords' own letters, under their own crests, and I watched the steward unwrap them this morning. Fennick's grey fingers still lie across them. So why can't I stop looking at them?

Across the table, Corvin Askell isn't moving either. Then he lifts his eyes, slowly, and looks straight down the length of the table, past the shouting and my wall of boxes.

At me.

It goes through me like the river, cold and then burning, all the way down to my boots. My heart slams once, hard, against my ribs. Don't look away. Don't you dare look away first. I don't. He does, though not before something moves at the corner of his mouth that on any other man I'd call surprise.

Then he stands and says a single word, not loudly. "Enough." The hall goes silent as if he'd worked leave to do it. He hasn't. People simply stop when he speaks.

"Lord Fennick needs a physician," he says, "not an accusation. If anyone in this hall believes Hesk did this, they may say so tomorrow, at this table, with their knife down. Tonight, Hesk will wait for Callowmere's next speaker." He picks nothing up, and his knife stays exactly where he laid it. "We are not going anywhere."

Our steward rounds on him. "And if Callowmere has no next speaker?"

"Then the truce ends at midwinter," says Corvin Askell, "and you can go to the Ford and tell them why." His gaze moves once more to the end of the table, to me, and stays there a heartbeat too long before he turns away.

Next speaker

They carry Fennick to our guesthouse on a door taken off its hinges. "He'll live, probably, if the sour stops spreading. I can't promise it will. I've seen this once before, and that man didn't live." The grey is already past Fennick's wrists and creeping towards his elbows, and his breath rasps in the quiet room.

"Will he wake tonight?" I ask.

"Not tonight, miss. Perhaps not this week. Leave that goes sour takes its time."

I learn what happens when a speaker falls in a cold corridor, from a steward who can't quite meet my eyes. "The Crown's leave to speak passes to the next member of the delegation who holds a written commission," he says, reciting it like a lesson. "That's the law of the Rock, Miss Vey. It has been for three hundred years."

"Then who holds one?"

He swallows and studies the flagstones. "His lordship's clerk had one. But his lordship's clerk is in bed in Callowmere with a fever."

"I'm his lordship's clerk now," I say.

"Yes, miss."

"And if I refuse it?"

"Then Callowmere has no voice on the Rock, and Hesk can tell the whole world why the talks failed. And the Ford will believe them."

And the whole world will remember whose daughter failed them. I take the paper out of my pocket, the one Lady Wick slid across the table with the soup, and unfold it under a lantern with hands that won't keep still. I read it for the first time.

Seren Vey, under-clerk of the Tally, is appointed deputy to Lord Aldous Fennick, with full leave to speak for the Crown at Hollin Rock in his absence.

It is my name, in the Chancellor's own looping hand, the same hand that writes Eat something hot on the notes that come down with my soup. Her seal is pressed deep into the wax. Something cold opens in my stomach and keeps opening. And then, at the very bottom, there is a date.

The commission in my hand has my name on it, and it is dated a week ago.
