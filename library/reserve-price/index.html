Reserve Price

Everyone has seen her emeralds. Nobody knows they’re glass.

The first thing I ever wrote about Odile Marchetti was a lie, and it was in a catalogue, which is the one place I'd always promised myself I'd never lie.

I've lied in plenty of other places. To my bank. To my mother, every Sunday, for three years. To myself, mostly at about two in the morning, when I'd add it all up in my head and get a different number every time.

But never in a catalogue. A catalogue is a promise. It says: this is what the thing is. This is where it came from. You can trust us.

Reserve Price

Chapter one. Lot One.

Lot 212. A brown glazed teapot, about 1950, with six matching cups.

Estimate: £10 to £15.

A heavy, plain, well-made pot. The glaze is worn pale on the right of the handle, where one hand held it for sixty years. There is a small chip on the left of the spout, which is the side you pour from if you are left-handed. Its owner was. She kept the cups on hooks rather than in a cupboard, so that they were always ready for somebody.

From the contents of a flat in London. Sold with a quantity of tea towels.

I wrote that entry on a Thursday night in February, alone in the basement at Fairlie's, long after everyone else had gone home. Nobody asked me to. Nobody paid me for the extra hours. The teapot sold for eleven pounds to a man who wanted the cups for his allotment shed, and I don't think he read a word of it.

I'm putting it first because I want you to like me before I tell you the rest.

Seven in the morning

The rest begins the following Monday, at seven in the morning, with my phone lighting up on the carpet beside my mattress.

You're close to your arranged overdraft limit. Log in to see your balance.

Friendly reminder! Your 3rd payment of 4 for "Wool coat, camel" is due tomorrow.

Missed call: Mum

I read the first line of each one. I didn't open any of them. I turned the phone face down, as if that would stop the numbers adding up, and lay looking at the ceiling of my box room. It was just wide enough for a single mattress and a clothes rail.

The coat from the second message was hanging on that rail. Camel wool, three-quarter length, horn buttons. I'd bought it in October because I had a viewing to run, and I wanted to look like the kind of woman who owned a coat like that. I still wanted to. I just hadn't finished paying for her.

The missed call was my mother. She rings every Sunday and asks if I'm all right for money, and I say, "We're fine, Mum. I'm saving loads." It's the family line. My dad first said it the year his framing shop closed, and none of us has stopped saying it since.

I didn't ring her back. I got up and went to work.

Fairlie's

Fairlie's is a narrow building in London that has been selling the furniture of dead people to living ones for a hundred years. It has a sale room with two dusty chandeliers, three floors of storerooms and a basement where the cataloguers work under strip lights. I had worked in that basement for four years.

Jude Fairlie had never once come down to it. That Monday he was waiting at the bottom of the stairs in a grey cashmere jumper, holding a cup of coffee somebody else had made him.

"Hattie," he said. "Good. Sit down."

There was one chair, and he was standing next to it, so I stayed where I was.

"You'll know we have the Marchetti sale," he said. "Everything she owns. Dresses, jewels, posters, furniture, scripts, three hundred lots, sold on her eighty-fifth birthday in April. It's the biggest sale this house has held in fifty years, Hattie. My grandfather would have given his right arm for it."

Everybody knew. It had been in the papers for a month. Odile Marchetti was the face on my grandmother's perfume bottle and the detective in the television series my mother watched every Sunday night in the eighties.

"Peter has resigned," said Jude. "Twenty-two years with us, and he's resigned. So has Fiona. I brought in a freelancer on Tuesday and he lasted until Thursday lunchtime."

"Is it the throwing?"

He looked at a spot just above my left shoulder. "She threw a shoe at Peter. Lot forty, a silver evening sandal from her second film. It caught him over one eyebrow." He drank some coffee. "Miss Marchetti wants a cataloguer who will write down exactly what she says about every lot, in her own words, and not argue. She means to tell the story of each piece herself."

"All three hundred?"

"Every fan, every glove, every hatpin." At last he looked at me. "Frankly, Hattie, you're the only person in this building who doesn't have opinions."

I took it as a compliment, because I couldn't afford to take it any other way.

"There's a bonus," he said, "if the catalogue goes to the printer by the end of March."

I said yes before he'd told me the figure. I was already working out which of the five places I owed money it would reach first.

The lift

Sami Haddad came with me. He was Fairlie's photographer and porter, quiet, with a camera round his neck and a bag of white cotton gloves. We had worked in the same building for a year and hardly spoken. Odile's mansion block had a lift with a brass gate that you closed by hand. Sami closed it, and we went up very slowly.

"Have you met her?" I asked.

"Once. On Tuesday. She asked if I was the freelancer's son."

"Are you?"

"No. I said yes, to see what would happen."

"What happened?"

"Nothing, at first. She watched me set up the lights. At the end she gave me forty pounds for a taxi I hadn't taken." He pulled the gate open. "I left it on the hall table on my way out. She was standing in the doorway when I turned round, and she looked at me as if I'd passed something."

Odile

The door of the flat stood open. Beyond it was a drawing room the size of a tennis court, crowded with clothes rails, packing crates and framed film posters leaning against the walls. It smelled of face powder and old silk. In the middle of it all, in a gilt chair that looked as if it had been stolen from a theatre box, sat Odile Marchetti.

She was smaller than her photographs. She wore a long silk dressing gown the dark green of a wine bottle, full make-up, diamond earrings and no shoes. Her hair was still dark, pinned up as if a photographer might arrive at any moment. It was eleven in the morning. Between two fingers she held a cigarette she hadn't lit.

"And you've brought the freelancer's son," she said, as Sami unfolded his tripod. "Good. Stand where the light is, young man, and don't touch anything with your bare hands. Every single thing in this room has been worn under hot lights, in front of people who paid to look at it, and the silk remembers every one of them."

"I've brought gloves," said Sami.

"Then you're already an improvement." She turned the cigarette towards me. "You're the new one."

"Hattie Brennan. From Fairlie's."

"I know where you're from, darling. I'm not senile, whatever my goddaughter tells the newspapers." She looked at me the way I look at a chest of drawers, from the feet up. Shoes, hem, coat, hands. Her eyes stopped on the coat. "Camel. Wool. Not paid for."

My face went hot. "I'm sorry?"

"The sleeves are too long for you, and you keep pulling them down over your hands. Nobody does that with a coat they own outright. You do it with a coat you're hiding in. I've had forty coats, darling, and I've hidden in most of them. Sit."

There was no chair. I sat on a packing crate.

"There are three rules," said Odile. "Your Mr Fairlie will have told you them badly, because he tells everything badly. One. I tell you the story of every lot, and you write it down exactly as I say it. Not nicely. Exactly. If I tell you a man was a pig, your catalogue will say he was a pig."

"Jude won't like that."

"Jude Fairlie has been frightened of me since he was nine years old. In nineteen sixty-three I was broke for the second time, and his father came to my flat in a van and sold every stick of my furniture by the end of the week. Jude carried the lamps. He watched me count the money on the bare floorboards, and he has never let me forget that he saw it. Two. You will not argue with me about what anything is worth. I know what everything in this room is worth, to the pound. I also know what each thing cost me, which is a different figure and nearly always a bigger one."

"And three?"

"Three. Not a penny of the money comes to me before the sale. No advance, no loan against the takings, nothing at all. The sale is on my birthday, and the money starts on my birthday. Write that down as well. People will ask you about it, and I want them to get the same answer every time."

I wrote it down. I didn't understand it. In four years I had never met a seller who didn't want an advance.

Odile pointed the cigarette at the nearest rail. "Lot one. Bring me the red shoes."

Lot one

They were in a box at the end of the rail, wrapped in tissue gone yellow with age. Red satin, size four, faded a little at the toes, with a heel as thin as a pencil. Sami put on his gloves and set them on a black cloth under his folding lamp. In that small bright circle, in the middle of the dusty room, they looked as if they'd been made that morning.

Odile looked at them for a long time before she spoke.

"Rome. Nineteen sixty-one. I was twenty-one years old and I had a part in a film, my first. I played a girl who sold flowers by a fountain. A prince kissed me in the second reel and I was never seen again. Eleven days' work. They paid me more than my father made in a year from his café in Glasgow, and he worked every day of that year except Christmas. I had never seen so much money in one place, darling. I held the cheque in both hands all the way home, in case the wind took it."

"What did you do with it?"

"What do you think I did with it? I was twenty-one. The cheque came on a Friday. On the Saturday morning I walked into the most expensive shoe shop in Rome. I'd been walking past that window for a year, slowing down every time, pretending to look at my own reflection. The assistant looked at my old shoes and tried not to laugh. I bought those. Then a handbag to match. Then a coat with velvet cuffs, two dresses, a pair of silk stockings and a little gold watch. I lost the watch in the sea at a party six weeks later, off a boat that belonged to a man who never paid for anything himself."

"You lost it in the sea?"

"I was showing someone the time. Gold sinks, darling. Put that in." She tapped the cigarette on the arm of the chair. "I took a flat with a balcony, because I had always wanted to stand on one. I gave a party for everybody I had ever met and several people I hadn't. By the end of the month I had a flat I couldn't pay for and a wardrobe I couldn't eat."

"And the shoes?"

"Ah. The shoes. On that Saturday evening I walked home in them, all the way across the city, over the cobbles, on that heel. I had spent my tram fare on the stockings, you see, and I would rather have died than go back into the shop and ask for it. So I walked, and I smiled at everybody I passed, so that they'd think I was walking for pleasure. When I got home my stockings were stuck to my heels with blood." She smiled then. It was the smile from the billboards, and it made the room feel smaller. "I was rich for about three weeks. The first time."

"The first time?"

"I have been rich seven times, darling. Write it down."

I wrote it down. Then I wrote the entry, and she made me read it aloud twice. She changed two words, crossed out a comma with my own pen and said it would do.

Lot 1. A pair of red satin evening shoes. Rome, 1961.

Estimate: £600 to £900.

Size four. Satin uppers, some fading to the toes. Heels sound. In the original tissue.

The seller's account: "Bought on a Saturday with the whole of my first film fee, then walked home in across Rome, because I had spent the tram fare on stockings. I was rich for three weeks. They were worth every blister." (O.M.)

Eleven lots

By two o'clock we had done eleven lots. Once, she called me Peter. She stopped, said sorry at once, quite quietly, and then went straight on as if the word had cost her something she didn't want to look at.

My hand ached. Sami had photographed a painted fan, a pair of opera glasses, a script with a director's coffee rings across the first page and a silver photograph frame with nothing in it. Odile wouldn't talk about the frame. "Empty frame," she said. "Silver. Write that." So I did.

Up close, from behind, the glamour came apart into work and bills. Inside every evening dress was a cotton label with a dressmaker's name and a date, and on several there was a second, smaller tag in pencil, with a price and the word owed. Three pairs of the famous shoes had been resoled. The long evening gloves from the perfume advertisements had been mended at the fingertips, twice, in slightly different thread.

"Somebody's darned these," I said.

"Win darned them. My dresser. She did the left one in the back of a car on the way to a premiere in Paris, with a needle she kept pinned inside her collar, and the right one in a lavatory at the Venice film festival while I was being sick with nerves. You can't see the stitches in any photograph, darling, I've checked. Put down that they were mended. Don't put down who by. Not yet."

"I'm bored," Odile announced. "You must want the emeralds. Everybody wants the emeralds. Take the emeralds and stop looking at me as if I were a shop window."

She pointed the cigarette at a flat leather case on the table by the window. Sami brought it over and opened it, and for a moment neither of us said anything at all.

I'd seen them in a hundred photographs. Fifteen square-cut green stones, each the size of a thumbnail, set in heavy yellow gold.

"You'll have read the stories," said Odile, while Sami found the clasp. "A prince gave them to me. A bank gave them to me. A man in Milan lost them to me at cards. I've heard all three told at my own dinner table, by people who had never met the prince, the bank or the man. I wore them to every premiere for forty years and I never once told anybody where they came from. People enjoyed guessing far more than they would have enjoyed the answer."

"Where did they come from?"

"Write the catalogue, darling. Don't write my biography."

I had written their entry the night before, at my kitchen table, from photographs. It was the first thing I ever wrote about her.

Lot 2. The Marchetti emeralds. A necklace of fifteen square-cut emeralds set in yellow gold, about 1965.

Estimate: £350,000 to £500,000.

Worn by the seller to every major premiere for four decades, and seen in more photographs than any other jewel of its time. The stones are of rich, even colour, with the natural inclusions typical of fine emeralds.

"Go on," said Odile. "Do whatever it is you people do."

The garden

I put on a pair of Sami's gloves. My hands were shaking a little, and I told myself it was the weight of the gold. I carried the necklace to the window, because you always look at stones in daylight, and I held the largest one up to the glass.

Here is something I know, and I'm sorry, but you need it for the next half minute to make sense. A real emerald nearly always has flaws inside it: tiny threads and cracks and specks, like moss in green water. The trade calls them inclusions. Jewellers call them the garden. Hold a real emerald up to the light and you can see the garden growing inside it.

Glass has no garden. Glass has bubbles, small, round and perfect, held in it like air caught in ice. And the edges of cut glass are softer than the edges of cut stone. Over the years they wear down, wherever they have rubbed against skin and silk.

I looked into the biggest stone for a long time. There was no garden. There were three small round bubbles, close together, and every edge was soft.

I looked at the next stone, and the next. Then I counted along the whole necklace with my thumb, all fifteen, and my mouth went completely dry.

Behind me the room had gone quiet. Sami had stopped moving his lamp. I turned round with the necklace lying across my gloved hands.

Odile was watching me from her gilt chair, the cigarette still unlit between her fingers. She didn't look surprised. She didn't look angry, or frightened, or caught. She looked like a woman who had been waiting a very long time to see who would notice first, and what they would do about it.

"Well, darling?" she said. "What are you going to write?"

I opened my mouth. Nothing came out.

"Lot two," said Odile pleasantly. "The Marchetti emeralds. Take as long as you need."

The Marchetti emeralds were glass, and she knew I knew.
