Every postie in the country will tell you the dogs know.

I mean your own dogs, the ones on your round. They know your van from the bottom of the hill and your boots from the gate, and by the time your hand reaches the letterbox they've been waiting for you all their lives.

I had thirty-eight of them in Scaurbeck that autumn, and I was frightened of every one.

Hackles

Chapter one. Two Lights in the Wall.

I know it was thirty-eight because each of them was marked on the back of my round card in red pen. (The round card is the laminated sheet that tells a postwoman which streets are hers and in what order.) A little D meant a dog behind the door. Two D's meant a dog loose in the yard, and a D with a ring round it meant don't, which meant that house's letters went into the door pocket of the van and stayed there. I'd been delivering the post in Scaurbeck for eleven years and I'd been frightened of dogs since I was nine, and in all that time not one person at the delivery office had put those two facts side by side. I worked hard to keep it that way. I'm not proud of how hard.

That was the autumn the sheep started dying up on Scaur Moss, and the town remembered the Lantern Dog.

I want to be careful how I tell this. I've had a few years to turn it over, and memory will swear blind to things it never saw, mine more than most (you'll see why). So I'll start with what I'm sure of. It was the second Tuesday in October and it was ten to six in the morning. On Tuesdays it was my turn to drive over the tops to the big sorting office in the next valley and bring back the town's mail, so I was coming home along the top road with four grey sacks behind me and the whole of Scaurbeck asleep somewhere below my left elbow. On my right was the moss. (A moss, round our way, is a moor.) The van's heater was broken (I'd reported it in June), so I drove with my coat zipped to the chin and a hot-water bottle on my knee, and the fog was down so thick that the headlights reached about a yard past the bonnet and stopped there.

I had the window open an inch to keep the windscreen clear, which is how I know how quiet it was. There's a house up there, the old quarry-master's place, where a man called Rory Stannard boarded and trained other people's dogs, and most mornings you could hear his kennels from a quarter of a mile off. That morning I went past his gate and heard only my own engine, and I was grateful for it.

Half a mile further on, the wall on the moor side has a gap where a gate used to hang. Everybody who drives that road knows it, because the gap is where the old packhorse road comes down off the moss and crosses the tarmac, and in fog it's the only landmark you get. I slowed for it out of habit.

There were two lights in the gap.

They were low down between the stone gateposts, side by side and perfectly steady. They were gold, each about the size of a coin, and as high off the ground as the middle bar of a farm gate.

Reflectors, I thought. Somebody had nailed reflectors to a post up here, and about time. Then the van rolled another yard, and the lights turned to follow it.

They didn't blink.

I've thought about that a great deal since. Neither did I.

I'd like to tell you I stopped. A braver woman would have stopped and had a proper look, and she might have saved a whole town a good deal of grief. What I did was put my foot down. I took that van into fog I couldn't see through at forty miles an hour, with my heart banging in my throat and my left forearm prickling under the sleeve from wrist to elbow.

The chain was the kind you'd use on a gate.

That's what came into my head, doing forty in the fog, and it had nothing to do with the lights. It belonged to a yard on Viaduct Row when I was nine years old, and a collie called Nell, and a length of chain lying in the dirt in the half circle she'd worn there herself. I'll come to Nell. I've spent most of my life not coming to her, so you'll have to give me a chapter or two.

The white van nearly ended this story before it began. It came down the moss track just past the cattle grid, out of the fog on my right, with no lights on at all. I saw a bumper and a wing mirror. I stood on the brake and the sacks slid forward into the back of my seat. The van swung onto the road in front of me without slowing and was gone downhill, and its tail lights never came on either.

"You bloody idiot!" I shouted, at a windscreen full of grey.

I sat with the engine running and my hands shaking on the wheel. A farmer, I decided. Some farmer who knew the track too well to bother with lights. I didn't look in the mirror to see what else might be on the road behind me. If I'd known then what I'd have given, three weeks later, to have read the number plate on that van, I'd have chased it all the way to the viaduct.

I got the van down into the yard behind the co-op with the sacks still on the floor. Stuart Fenton, who managed our delivery office and four others off one clipboard, was holding the back door open with his foot.

"You're eleven minutes late," he said.

"Fog, and some clown on the top road with no lights on."

"Are you all right? You look terrible."

"I'm fine. The heater's still broken, Stuart."

"It's on the list."

"It's been on the list since June."

"It's a long list." He took the first sack off me and stood there holding it. "Jess, have you got five minutes after your round? It's nothing to worry about."

"Course," I said. I carried the parcels in past him with my face arranged, and I spent the next hour at the sorting frame thinking about a single bed in a back bedroom, which you don't know about yet.

The round

Scaurbeck looks better in daylight. It's one long main street that climbs out of the valley bottom, so steep that the council grits it first every winter and still loses cars on it, with terraces of black stone stepping up either side. At the bottom the beck (our word for a stream) runs under the viaduct and past the mill, which shut when I was at school and has kept its chimney because nobody will pay to take it down. Halfway up you get the Methodist chapel and the chip shop, and the co-op with our delivery office round the back by the bins. At the top there's the Drover's Rest and the moor gate, and after that there's only the moss. I'd walked every yard of it six days a week since I was twenty-five. I could have told you whose curtains hadn't opened by nine, and whose post had changed that year from birthday cards to brown envelopes. A round is made of houses more than letters, and I loved mine, and I want that understood before I tell you what I was doing to it.

The dogs were the part I didn't love. The farm shop had a German shepherd the size of a sofa (two D's), who had never done a thing to me except stand up when I came through the gate. And at 14 Quarry Bank there was a terrier, about as big as a loaf, who heard my van at the bottom of the hill every morning and spent the next ten minutes working himself up to it. By the time I reached his gate he was throwing himself at the inside of the front door hard enough to rattle the knocker.

Number 14 had a ring round its D. I stood at that gate on the Tuesday with a gas bill and a postcard from Tenerife in my hand, listening to the knocker jump, and my legs would not take me up the path. My head knew the door was shut and that he weighed less than one of my boots. My legs were nine years old, and they weren't going. So the gas bill and the postcard went back in my bag, and at the end of the street they went into the door pocket of the van with the others.

Here's the part I've never enjoyed saying out loud. The door pocket got emptied every night into a carrier bag, and the carrier bag came home with me. In the back bedroom of my dad's old house there was a single bed nobody slept in, and on that bed, in bundles held with the office's own red rubber bands, was the post for nine houses. On my days off I took it round. I went in my own coat, in the middle of the day, and pushed a week's worth through each door. Mostly nobody noticed, because mostly it was bills. But a hospital letter is post too, and so is a postcard from Tenerife, and keeping people's mail from them is the single thing a postwoman can't do and go on being a postwoman. I knew that every time I shut the back-bedroom door, and I'd been shutting it for two years.

The Drover's Rest

I got to the top of the main street a bit after eleven. The fog had lifted off the town by then, but it was still lying on the moss in a flat white band above the last roofs. Lol Haigh was at the door of the Drover's Rest with his arms folded on top of his stomach, which was how he stood when he had news. His old spaniel, Pudding, was asleep across the step (one D, and that was mostly for the look of the thing). Sandy Rahal, who cleaned every window in Scaurbeck and heard most of what was said behind them, was up his ladder doing the pub's top floor.

"You'll have heard," Lol said.

"I've heard nowt, Lol. I've been at work since five."

"Annie Thwaite's lost three ewes."

"Lost them how?"

"Found them in the beck first thing, just below the moss wall. Three of them, Jess, all together."

"Four, I heard," Sandy said, from above.

"Three. I had it off the lad who drives the feed wagon, and he had it off Annie."

"They could have fallen," I said. "In that fog they could have walked straight off the bank."

"Sheep don't fall in threes." Lol shook his head. "Annie says it was no accident, and she's kept sheep on that common for forty years. She had the police up there before eight."

I held his post out to him over the dog. I always did it at full stretch, leaning in from the pavement, and Lol took it without looking, as he had for eleven years. Pudding opened one eye at my boots and shut it again.

"Dogs, then," I said. "Somebody's dog has got out."

"Whose? There's not a dog in this town could put three ewes in the beck." Lol looked up the street towards the moor gate and dropped his voice, though there was nobody nearer than the chip shop. "I'll tell you what my mother would have said. Ewes dead on the moss in October, and Lantern Night not three weeks off."

"Don't," Sandy said.

"I'm not saying it. I'm telling you what she'd have said."

"You're saying it, Lol."

"The Lantern Dog," Lol said. Once it was out he looked pleased with himself and slightly sick, both at the same time.

You'll want that explained, if you're not from here. Every child in Scaurbeck is told about the Lantern Dog before they can read. It's a black hound as big as a calf, with eyes that burn like two lamps, and it walks the old packhorse road over the moss at night. If you see it, somebody in the town will be dead inside the week. That's the whole of the story. It's why, on the last Saturday of October, the children carry lanterns cut from turnips up the main street to the moor gate, to keep the thing up there where it belongs. You grow out of it the same as you grow out of the tooth fairy, and I hadn't heard an adult say that name with a straight face in twenty years.

"It's a story for getting kids up a hill with a turnip," I said.

"I know what it is."

"Then don't go putting it on that page of yours."

"I'll put what I like on it. People have a right to be told."

"Told what, that your mother was superstitious?" Sandy said.

"That there's something up there." Lol nodded at the white band of fog. "Laugh if you want. Neither of you was on the top road at six o'clock this morning."

Tell them.

I had my mouth open to do it. Then I saw how it would go. It would be on Lol's page by noon and it would have grown teeth by teatime, and sooner or later somebody would ask me what I'd done when I saw it. So I told them I had a round to finish, and I went back down the hill and finished it.

The bus shelter

Stuart had gone off to one of his other offices by the time I got back. He'd left a note on my sorting frame: Tomorrow will do, Jess. I clocked off and walked home to the bottom of town, where my dad's end terrace looks out on the bus turning circle.

Tilly Bairstow was sitting on the wall beside the bus shelter with a drawing pad on her knees. Tilly was ten and lived in the flats at Mill Fold with her mum. She wore an anorak with room in it for a second child, and she drew everything she looked at. It was a quarter to three on a school day. I didn't ask. With Tilly you got further by not asking.

I should tell you something about her and me, since I've told you the worst already. In eleven years I had never put one thing through that door with Tilly's name on it. She got no birthday cards and no party invitations, and a postwoman notices that sort of gap. So every Monday night I drew a cat in a postman's hat on a plain card and posted it to her with a proper stamp. I never signed them. This week's cat was poking out of the top of her drawing pad, where she was using him as a bookmark.

"Hello, Tilly."

"Hello." She didn't look up. She was drawing the bus shelter, and she'd got the lean of it right.

"Are you all right there?"

"Yes." The pencil kept going. "Is it true the Lantern Dog eats children?"

"Who's told you that?"

"Mr Haigh was telling a lady outside the co-op. He said it's come back."

"It doesn't eat anybody. It's a story, Tilly, it's not real."

"But if it was."

"It isn't."

"But if it was." She did look up then. She looked at my whole face, a bit at a time, which was how she looked at anything she meant to draw. "What would it eat?"

"Turnips, I should think. There's never one left by the Sunday."

She gave that proper thought, and wrote something small in the corner of her page.

"Would they hurt it, if somebody found it?" she said.

It was an odd question to get from a frightened child, and it took me far too long to work out that she wasn't one.

"Nobody's going to find it, because there's nothing up there to find," I said. I heard my own voice saying it, bright and grown-up, and my arm prickled under the sleeve.

"Okay." She went back to the bus shelter. I had my key out when she spoke again. "I got a card today. It's a cat, and he's got a hat on like yours."

"Has he? Who's it from?"

"It doesn't say." She touched the corner of the card with one finger. "It never says."

I went in and shut the door, and I stood behind it for a while with my bag still on my shoulder.

That night

I had fish and chips on my knee in front of the television, and the front room smelled of vinegar until bedtime. Then I took the carrier bag upstairs. The gas bill for number 14 went on the back-bedroom bed with its bundle, and the postcard went on top of it, picture side up, because I'd already read the other side at the gate. Weather glorious. Hope Bobby is behaving himself. Bobby was the terrier. I stood there with a stranger's holiday in my hand, thinking about Stuart's five minutes, and then I shut the door on the lot of it.

About eleven, my arm started. There's a scar on my left forearm, a white crescent a bit longer than my thumb, and I've kept a sleeve over it since I was nine. It's twenty-seven years old and it doesn't hurt. That night it itched, deep down where you can't get at it, and I lay scratching it through my pyjama sleeve and seeing two gold lights turn to follow a van.

A sheep, I told myself. Any farmer will tell you a sheep's eyes shine in headlights, and the moss is covered in sheep. It was a ewe standing in a gateway, and I'd run from it at forty miles an hour.

I had very nearly got myself to believe that. The bedroom window was open an inch, as it always was. The town was quiet. I could hear the beck.

Up on the moss, something began to bay, and every dog in Scaurbeck answered it.
