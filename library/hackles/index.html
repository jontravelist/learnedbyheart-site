Every kid in Scaurbeck knows the rule, because an older kid tells it to them on the swings behind the chapel the year they turn seven.

There is a dog on the moss as big as a calf, with a lamp in each eye. If it crosses the road in front of you, somebody in the town is going to die. If it turns its head and follows you, the somebody is in your house.

I was thirty-six, I was the town's postwoman, and I lived on my own.

Hackles

Chapter one. Two Lights in the Wall.

Tuesdays I did the trunk run, over the tops in the dark to the sorting office in the next valley and back. You see who's up at that hour. There's a light in the back bedroom of the end house on Bank Top, where Kath Sugden sits up with her husband, who is dying. Kath took my dad his tea every night of his last month, and I loved her for it. Kath is also the woman who, the year I was nine and told the whole town Walter Brigg's dog had jumped his wall at me, stood on Walter's front step with a carrier bag of dog muck off the pavements and pushed it through his letterbox a handful at a time while two of her friends held the flap up. I was a little girl across the road in my school coat and I watched her do it. She laughed the whole time, and afterwards she wiped her fingers on the lining of her coat.

Past Bank Top the road climbs onto the top, and after that there is nothing on your right but a dry-stone wall with the moss behind it, black for miles. I drove with my coat zipped to the chin and the window down an inch, and the fog was so thick and yellow in the headlights that I could see the wall and a yard of road and nothing else at all.

I held my breath going past the kennels at the old quarry-master's house, where a man boards and trains other people's dogs, because most mornings the whole block goes up at the van. That morning there wasn't a sound out of the place, and I was grateful, and I want you to remember that.

Half a mile on, the wall has a gap where a gate used to hang, where the old packhorse road comes down off the moss and crosses the tarmac.

There were two lights in the gap.

They were low down and side by side, gold, each about the size of a ten-pence piece, and perfectly still.

Cat's-eyes, I thought. The council's put cat's-eyes on the gatepost. I knew while I was thinking it that the council puts nothing on that road but potholes.

I rolled forward, and the lights turned to keep me in them.

A reflector slides about when you move. These swung. They turned together, slowly, with weight behind them, and they kept the van exactly between them, and from how slowly they moved I knew roughly how big the head was.

Then they came up.

They came up off the ground with no hurry at all, past the gatepost, and stopped level with my window. Whatever it was had been lying in that gateway in the fog, waiting, and now it was standing, with its eyes level with mine a couple of yards away and nothing between us but an inch of open window.

Something clinked, metal on stone, small and bright.

The smell came in through the inch. Wet wool, and under the wool something thick and warm and old, the smell of a shed where an animal has been kept a long time and nobody has mucked it out.

Drive.

My hand was on the gearstick and it wouldn't do anything. My left arm had started, the prickle from wrist to elbow along the scar, and my legs had gone. I don't mean they were weak. I mean I couldn't find them.

Jess. Drive the sodding van.

I got it into second somehow and stamped, and the wall slid back into place on my right, and I had one whole breath of believing that was the end of it.

Clink.

It was on the far side of the wall, level with my door.

I put my foot down. The wall slid past a foot from my wing mirror, and the clink came with me, not behind me but with me, every yard or two, chink, chink, bright and patient. Under it now I could hear the rest: the long brush of something heavy along the far side of the stones, and a breath let out. It wasn't panting, which I could have stood. It was slower than mine, the breathing of something that didn't have to try.

I sped up, and it sped up. Then I had to brake for the bend above Scaur Gill bridge, and the clinking slowed down. It slowed down with me. A thing that's chasing you runs as hard as it can. This was keeping me company.

I was saying "fuck off, fuck off, fuck off" at the windscreen in a voice I'd never heard come out of me, and a stone came off the top of the wall behind me and cracked on the road, and the van hit the hump of the bridge at forty with a bang that came up through the seat, and the wall ended.

The clinking didn't come over the bridge.

And from nowhere: a chain, the kind you'd use on a gate, lying in the dirt of a yard on Viaduct Row in a half circle a collie had worn bare walking the end of it. Nell. The weight of her on my arm. Not the teeth, I never remember the teeth. The weight, how heavy a dog is when it's hanging off you, and my knees going down in the dirt, and me shouting for my mum, who couldn't hear. Then Walter's back door banged open, and he came across the yard at a run.

Just below the bridge a white van came out of the moss track with no lights on. I stood on the brake. It swung out in front of me and drove off down towards the town, and its tail lights never came on. Somebody else had been up on the moss in that dark, with whatever was behind the wall, and was coming down off it without lights so as not to be seen. I didn't think that at the time. All I thought was that I wasn't driving down any road that had come off the moss.

So when the van turned for Scaurbeck, I kept straight on over the top. I drove three miles the wrong way, towards the reservoir, before the sky greyed over the water. I turned in a farm gateway and came back the long way. I was the last van into the delivery office yard, and I sat in it with the engine off until my legs would hold me.

Quarry Bank

Stuart Fenton was holding the back door with his foot. Stuart wears his keys on a chain at his belt, and as I came across the yard they knocked against the door frame, and the sack dropped out of my hands onto the cobbles.

"Quarry Bank's rung," he said. "Number fourteen, tomorrow at nine."

That was all. He carried my sack in for me, and every time he moved at the sorting frame his keys chinked on their chain.

I locked myself in the staff toilet and pushed my left sleeve up. The scar is a pale bent seam the length of my thumb, puckered at both ends where the stitches pulled. It was pink. It was raised and hot to touch, and either side of it, in two short curved rows, were the little red dents where her teeth sank in, the way they were in the hospital when I was nine and the nurse made me look.

I was at fourteen Quarry Bank by nine. On the back of my round card that house had a red D with a ring round it, my mark for a house I couldn't make myself walk up to. There's a terrier at fourteen, and every other day of that year he had been at the inside of the front door before I reached the gate, throwing himself at it until the knocker lifted.

That morning the door was quiet. I could see him through the frosted glass, a little dark shape sitting bolt upright at the far end, with his back to me, facing the back of the house. He didn't turn round. He didn't so much as move his ears. I stood at the gate with a gas bill and a long envelope from the hospital, for the woman who had stopped me on the street the week before and asked me if anything had come.

He's not even looking at you. Go on.

My head knew that. My legs were nine years old, and they would not take me up the path.

At the van I folded the hospital letter in half, because the door pocket wouldn't take it flat any more, and pushed it in on top of the others.

Irene Booth was at the post office counter inside, buying a single first-class stamp. She turned and sniffed close to my coat.

"You've been on the top road," she said.

"I do the trunk run Tuesdays, Irene."

"Wet wool," she said. "You've got wet wool on you." She paid for her stamp in coppers, one at a time, and didn't look at me again.

The trailer

By eleven I was up at the Drover's Rest, under the moor gate. Lol Haigh was in his doorway with a tea towel on his shoulder, and his old spaniel asleep across the step. Sandy Rahal was there with his ladder, and Daz Midgley and two lads from the tyre place were stood in the road.

"Annie Thwaite," Lol said. "Three ewes, in the beck, under the moss wall. I've a brewery delivery due at twelve and they'll not get the lorry past if she brings it down the middle. Somebody wants to tell her."

"Three at once?" said Sandy. "Can sheep do that?"

Daz didn't answer him. Daz was watching the gate.

A quad bike came through it in low gear with Annie sat very straight on it in a wax jacket, towing a sheep trailer with a tarpaulin roped down over the top. You could see where the rope ran, and what shapes it lay over.

She brought it down the middle of the road at walking pace, and the town came out of the shops and stood along the kerb. When she came level with me she stopped the quad, with the engine running, and looked at me for a long time.

"You were on the top road this morning," she said. "I heard your van go over the bridge. Going like hell. And before that you slowed right down at the old gate. I heard you."

Everybody on the pavement looked at me.

"Did you see owt? On the road, or by the wall?"

"No," I said.

She put the quad in gear, and the trailer rolled by with its ropes creaking, and a smell came after it that I had already smelled once that day.

"And nobody start on about the Lantern Dog," Daz said. "It's a dog with an owner. And if it hasn't got an owner, I've a twelve-bore in the cupboard. Saturday night I'm going up, and Sandy's coming with me, aren't you, Sandy."

"Lantern Night three weeks Saturday," Lol said to me. "My mother'd have had the curtains shut by now. In broad daylight. She'd have had every curtain in the house shut."

I had the rule in my head the whole time: the lamps had turned, and kept turning, and I knew who lived in my house.

That was when the woman from six Moss View came up the hill with her dogs. Gemma Lowther, by her post: catalogues, a gym magazine, a card once a month from somewhere sunny. She had earbuds in and a coffee in a paper cup and two big grey dogs on short leads, the sleek kind with pale eyes, and she was halfway into the crowd before she noticed it was a crowd. Daz stepped out in front of her.

"Them yours? Where were they last night?"

She took an earbud out. "In the kitchen. They sleep in the kitchen. It's a utility room, really. They've never been up there, they don't even like the wind. Please, I've got to get to work."

"Where were they last night."

Somebody had their phone up. "Get back where you came from," somebody said behind me, and somebody laughed. She backed into the bus stop and the coffee slopped down the front of her coat, and the two dogs pressed in against her legs, and one of them turned its long grey head and looked straight at me.

My arm prickled, wrist to elbow.

"They're big enough," I said.

I said it because they were looking at her and not at me. It came out clear, in my delivering voice, the one people trust, and Daz nodded and said it again louder so the phone would get it. "Postie says they're big enough. You hear that? Even the postie says."

Gemma Lowther looked at me. I'd put her post through her door for two years. She stood with coffee dripping off her hem in front of half the town, and she said, "Why would you say that," not angry, only asking, and I didn't answer her, and she walked back down the hill with her dogs pressed against her, and the phone followed her all the way to the corner.

The bus shelter

Tilly Bairstow was sat on her own on the wall by the bus shelter at the bottom of town, with a drawing pad on her knees and a co-op carrier bag beside her. She was ten. She lived in the flats at Mill Fold with her mum and her mum's boyfriend, and nobody had come to any gate for her.

I had never once put a thing through the Mill Fold door with Tilly's name on it, so every Monday night I drew a cat in a postman's hat on a plain card and posted it to her, and never signed it. This week's was sticking out of her pad.

"I've got a key," she said, before I could ask. She was drawing the shelter. She didn't look up. "Did it walk next to you?"

"Did what?" I said. "What walked?"

"On the top road. It does that. It walks along by the wall with you to see you off." She rubbed something out. "Callum Midgley's dad says it eats children. It has the little ones first because they can't run."

"Who told you I was on the top road, Tilly? Did somebody tell you?"

"Would they hurt it? If they found it?"

"There's nothing up there to find," I said, to a child, with the smell of that gateway still on my coat.

"Okay," she said. "He isn't black, though. That's only in the story." And she bent over her pad again.

There were two packs of co-op sausages in the carrier bag, and I thought, her mum's not feeding her again, that's her tea, and I felt pleased with myself for noticing, and walked home. I was wrong about the sausages. I was at my own door before I thought of what she'd drawn in the corner of the page, under the shelter: two circles, side by side, traced round so many times that the pencil had worn through, and I stood scraping my key round the lock and missing it until next door came out to look.

That night

Lol's page had four hundred comments by nine o'clock. Somebody had put up the video from outside the Drover's, and under it the name of the street, and under that the house number. You could hear me in it, saying they were big enough,. At ten Daz wrote, Out with the lamp tonight. Owt moves up there it's getting it, and a lot of people I knew liked that.

I didn't sleep. The scar itched deep in, where you can't get at it, and at one I got up and didn't put the light on. From my landing window you can see over the roofs to the moss, and on the skyline Daz Midgley's lamp was swinging slowly back and forward over the heather.

Then I saw the other light.

It was much lower and much smaller, below the moor gate on the old packhorse road: a little yellow torch, bobbing, going up, at the height a torch is when it's held by somebody who doesn't come up to your shoulder.

I thought about a carrier bag with two packs of sausages in it.

On an ordinary night in Scaurbeck there's always a dog going somewhere. I stood with my forehead on the cold glass and I hadn't heard one since I got up, and I understood that they were all awake behind their doors, every one of them, listening.

I was frightened for her, more than I had been for myself on the top road, and I stood there in my nightie with my hand flat on the glass and I didn't put my boots on.

Up on the moss, something began to bay, and every dog in Scaurbeck answered it, and the little light went out.

I stood at that window until it was day, and I never saw it come back down.
