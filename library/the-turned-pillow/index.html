The Turned Pillow

Chapter one. No Sleep, No Fee.

The stick by the door

The night coach set Dorrie down in the square at one in the morning, threw her bundle after her and went on without waiting to be thanked. She stood in the frost with the deed buttoned inside her coat and looked at what she owned.

It was three storeys of grey moor-stone with an arch for coaches, and every window in it was lit. Across the square, outside a smaller inn, a lamp with a great curved mirror behind its flame stood on an iron post and poured white light over the cobbles and over her. When nobody answered her knock, she knocked again with her boot.

The door was opened by a boy of about fifteen holding a candle. He wore a coat made for somebody twice his width.

"The house is delighted to receive you," he said. "The house regrets that there is no supper. The house regrets that the cook has gone, and the maids. You're the new one, you've got the paper, I can see it in your coat. The house is very glad, mistress, and please to mind the stone."

Just inside the door sat a lump of grey granite as high as her knee, its top worn into a smooth hollow by hands. Beside it, on a nail, hung a hazel stick with a small knife tied to it by a thong. Notches ran all the way down one side of the stick, so close they nearly touched.

"What's that for, then, that stick?"

"That's mine," said the boy. He took it down and held it so she could see. "Every morning, anyone who didn't sleep puts their hand in the stone and says so, and they don't pay, and I cut a notch. That's the road's law, it's older than the road. That's a month of nights. And once they've sworn, the keeper can't take a coin off them, not one. That's the only thing the guild in Harl takes a house away for, Master Florian said, not rats, not bad beds, only that. A notch means sworn and not paid, and anybody can read it. Master Florian used to cut them, and then he gave me the knife and said, Pim, you're the house's tally-man now. I'm Pim. I came with the house, it says so in your paper."

Dorrie had read the paper until the folds went soft, and she didn't remember a Pim. She remembered a line saying that the Turned Pillow was the busiest house on the moor road. Had Florian Tapp written that with this stick hanging by his door?

"Nobody has paid in a whole month?"

"The ones who've heard about the stick don't come any more," said Pim. "Mostly now it's people who want a free bed. May I see the paper, mistress? Master Florian taught me my letters on the bills. I read very well, he says it's my best thing." He had it out of her hand before she could say no, and found the place with his finger. "Half the price now, the other half from the takings by midwinter, or the house and the first half go back to the seller. That's forty days." He looked up. "And it says, all staff to be kept on at the keeper's pleasure. That's me. I'm all staff."

"Give it here," said Dorrie, and he did, slowly, watching her face to see what her pleasure was going to be.

The guest

The common room was long and low and bitterly cold. Every lamp was lit, the fire was dead in the grate, and a kettle the size of a bucket stood on the counter stinking of old black-bean. One person sat in the whole room, in a chair pulled up to the cold hearth: a broad woman of about forty in a fur hat and a driving coat, with her boots on and a cup in her hands and her eyes wide open.

"That's Sal," Pim said. "She drives the mail and stops here every other night between the runs. She doesn't sleep, so she doesn't pay, but she's very regular, she's our most regular. I cut her notch every other morning."

"Good evening to you," said Dorrie. "I'm the keeper here now."

Sal looked at her for a while. "It's the morning, not the evening."

"Why are you sitting down here in the cold? There's eleven beds upstairs. Is something wrong with all of them?"

"No. The beds are good, Florian had new feathers put in them at harvest."

"Then why don't you go up and lie in one? I would, if I were you."

"I don't sleep here," said Sal. "Nobody sleeps here."

"So why stop here at all, if you can't sleep? There's another inn not fifty steps off."

Sal considered that and drank from her cup before she answered. "It's free, and it's on my road."

Dorrie felt her temper come up from her stomach. She had paid every coin she had for a clean house where she could lie down when it got dark and get up when it got light. The first person in it was this woman, drinking her black-bean and telling her it was free.

"How long have you been up, sitting there?"

"Since yesterday noon. The down mail goes at dawn." Sal looked into her cup. "It doesn't matter. The horses know the road, and so do I, every stone of it."

Dorrie went out of the room before she said something she couldn't take back to the only guest she had.

The kitchen

It was a good kitchen gone cold. She went round it the way she would go round a cart she was thinking of buying. The stove was big enough to roast a sheep and had a crack across its top plate. Two of the copper pans had holes in them. One flag of the floor rocked under her boot. The door at the far end, down to the cellar, had a latch hanging by a single nail, and somebody had tied it shut with a bit of string. In the larder she found a sack of oatmeal, a string of onions, salt in a crock and half a ham with the bone in.

She tipped her purse out on the table under the candle. Four coppers and a button.

"How much is the other half?" said Pim, behind her. "The midwinter half. You don't have to tell me. Is it a lot?"

"Six hundred silver."

Pim's mouth opened. "That's more money than there is," he said. "In the whole town, I mean, all of it put together. Is it? It might be."

Dorrie put her hands flat on the table to stop them shaking. Forty days, she thought, looking at the coppers and the button for a long time.

"I can't pay you, Pim," she said. "You may as well know now. I can't pay a pot-boy. Have you got people you can go to?"

"My mother, at Coldbeck, over the moor. She's got six without me." He said it very fast. "I don't want paying. I don't eat much. I'm awake all night, I can do the night door, I can do the lamps, I can read the bills. I can do the stick. Nobody else knows the stick. You'd have to learn it."

"It's a stick. You cut it with a knife."

"It's the law," said Pim, and held on to it with both hands.

She didn't have the heart to argue at two in the morning. She sent him for kindling and water instead, and got the stove lit, and fed it until the iron ticked and the cold went out of the corners. She shaved the fat off the ham into the big iron pan and sliced three onions into it. When they had gone soft and gold she worked in oatmeal and salt with her fingers until the whole mass held together, then pressed it flat into the pan with the back of a plate, and left it on the heat to crust. Pim came back with the buckets and stopped in the doorway with his nose up.

"That's a sod. That's a drover's sod! Nobody's made one of those here since Granny Lusk, and she put too much salt in. How do you know about sods? You're from the city."

"There's drovers in the city. They bring the cattle to market, and they eat."

The sod hissed and spat. When the edges came away from the pan and the smell of the bottom browning had filled the room, she slid a knife round it and turned it out whole onto a board, crust upward, as brown as a loaf. When Pim reached for it she smacked his hand.

"Bowls," she said. "On the shelf."

Under her feet the floor groaned, a long low sound that set the copper pans chiming on their hooks.

"That's the bear," said Pim. "He's meant to sleep till spring. It's in the deeds."

"The deeds said a lodger. I thought it meant a man."

"He is a lodger. He's lodged in the cellar since before the house had a roof on, Master Florian said. Moor-bears sleep the whole winter through, only he isn't sleeping. He's been up and walking about for weeks, same as everybody."

Pim was reaching up for the bowls when the string on the cellar door snapped. The door swung in, slowly, and something filled the doorway from side to side.

It was a grey striped thing the size of a pony, and it smelled of old hay. It stood blinking at the stove, and its nose worked at the onions.

Pim went up onto the table in one movement and stood on it with the stick held out in front of him. Dorrie stayed at the stove with the knife in her hand. Her heart was going so hard she could feel it in her teeth. She had met loose dogs in the dark in Harl, and once a bull, and she had never run from any of them.

"Go on back down," she said. "Go on with you. That's mine."

The bear came across the kitchen as if she wasn't there. It put its nose under the edge of the board and lifted. The board went over off the table, and the sod broke across the flags in a wide crumbling fan. The bear put its face to the floor and ate.

"That was my supper, you great thief," said Dorrie. "That was the only supper in the house."

It ate in rows. It started at the far edge of the spill and licked its way across in a straight line, then back along the next, like a man mowing a field, and it would not skip a row even when the best of the crust lay right under its nose. When the flags were clean it picked the empty pan off the stove by the handle in its teeth, very carefully, carried it to the hearth, and lay down along the warm stones with its chin resting inside the pan. It sighed into it, and the pan rang faintly. Once its eyes had closed, its great sides began after a while to rise and fall, and a little whistle came out of its nose with every breath.

Pim got down off the table. "He's asleep," he whispered. "Mistress, he's asleep, look at him. That's the first thing that's slept in this house in a month."

Dorrie laughed out loud for the first time since the coach left the city. A piece of the crust had skidded under the dresser, out of the bear's rows. She fished it out, broke it in two, and gave Pim the bigger half. They sat down on the warm flags with their backs against the bear's side, and ate. Under the coarse warm fur she could feel the bear's heart, much slower than her own.

"Why did they all go, the cook and the maids? Did he stop paying them?"

"He paid them in black-bean, mostly, at the end, and promises. The cook said he wasn't going to cook for people who sat up all night and paid nothing and then complained about the eggs. He said it to Master Florian's face in the yard. He said a lot of other things too, that the house wouldn't want me to repeat." Pim licked his fingers. "Master Fairlie from the Lark across the square came over twice with money for the house, but Master Florian said he'd sooner sell to a stranger than to the Lark. Then he sold up, to you. He went on the morning coach a week ago with his trunk, and he said, Pim, the new one will be very glad of you."

"Did he say why nobody sleeps? He must have had some notion."

"He said it was the moor. He said it was the bear, and the bad luck. He said the guests were all liars." Pim thought about it. "I don't think he knew. I sleep all right, only not at night. Is that wrong?"

"I don't know," said Dorrie. "I sleep all right in the day myself."

A cold breath came up the cellar steps, the sour dark smell of every deep place under every house. She got up and went down six steps without a candle, feeling each one with her boot, and stood at the bottom with her hand on the wall, breathing it in.

"How can you see anything down there, mistress? I can't even see you."

"I can't. You don't need to see to go down steps in the dark. You count them on the way down, and you count them back up." She came back up and wedged a chair under the broken latch, and sat on the chair, and stayed there with her eyes on the sleeping bear until Pim fell asleep against it too.

The chair

At four she went to the common room to tell Sal there was a warm kitchen. Sal was still in the chair by the dead fire. Her chin was on her chest and her mouth was open, and the cup was tipping slowly towards her knees. Dorrie took it out of her fingers before it spilled. Sal didn't move, but snored softly, with a catch at the back of the throat on every breath.

Was this what free looked like? When had Dorrie last slept like that herself, at night, with her mouth open? She put the cup on the mantel and left her there.

The stone

At first light the down mail came round under the arch with a fresh team steaming in the cold. Sal came to the door with her whip in her hand and her hat on, and put her other hand in the hollow of the stone.

"I didn't sleep here," she said.

Pim had the stick off its nail and the knife ready.

"Leave it," said Dorrie. "She slept, because I took the cup out of her hand at four o'clock and she never knew it, and she was snoring. You slept two hours in that chair, Sal Ambry. I stood there and watched you."

"I didn't sleep, I only shut my eyes for a bit."

"With your mouth open. You were snoring."

"I don't snore," said Sal, honestly puzzled. "I'd know if I'd been asleep, I always know. I've driven that road four years and I've never once been asleep on it."

"Well, you were asleep in my chair. Your cup was tipping into your lap, and I caught it. The room's two silver, and you can pay it."

"That's not the law," said Sal. "You know that isn't the law. And Master Fairlie asks me every morning what the Pillow took off me. He'll ask me today."

"Then tell him nothing."

"I can't tell him nothing. He'll ask."

Sal had a leather purse on her belt. Dorrie reached out and unhooked it, and Sal let her. She stood with her hand still in the stone while Dorrie opened the purse, took out two silver, hooked the purse back on her belt and stepped away. The coins were warm from Sal's body.

Behind her Dorrie heard the knife go into the stick. When she turned round, Pim had cut the notch. He was white to the lips and he didn't look at her.

"I told you to leave it."

"She said it on the stone, mistress. The stone takes the guest's word. It doesn't take the keeper's. That's the law, and the stick's mine, Master Florian gave it me. I won't cut it wrong. You can send me home to Coldbeck if you want."

"I didn't sleep," Sal said again, quietly. "I'd have known." She went out to her coach and climbed up onto the box.

Dorrie stood in the doorway with the money shut in her fist. It was the first money the house had taken in a month, and her eyes stung. In the kitchen behind her the bear was still asleep with its chin in her pan.

Pim held up the stick. The new notch was pale and fresh at the bottom of all the others. "Sworn and not paid, mistress," he said. "That's what it says now."

Dorrie opened her fist and looked at the two silver lying in it. Every coin she had ever saved had gone into this house. She had thrown it away in the doorway for the price of one bed.

Out under the arch the driver's whip cracked, and the mail began to roll.

Dorrie ran. She went out through the arch with the silver held up in her fist, slipping on the frozen cobbles, shouting Sal's name across the square. The mail didn't take the Harl road. It swung round the market cross, slowed, and turned in under the great white lamp of the Lark.

A tall man in shirtsleeves was already standing in the Lark's doorway with a mug in each hand. That would be Master Fairlie, who had come over twice with money for her house.

"Morning, Sal," he called up to the box. "What did the new keeper at the Pillow take off you?"
