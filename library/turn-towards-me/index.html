The first rule of live sound is that every microphone is on until you have personally checked that it's off. I say it to every trainee on their first day, and I've never once broken it.

The best man's microphone was on. I'd checked. That was my job, and at a quarter to six on the last Saturday in September, in a converted barn outside Bristol with fairy lights strung along the beams, I was doing my job with enormous professionalism, in black jeans, at a folding table behind the cheese.

What I hadn't checked was my own.

Turn Towards Me

Chapter one. Feedback.

The car park

I should go back four hours, to the car park, because it matters and I'd rather you heard it from me.

At two o'clock I was on my knees by the door of Hollins Barn, taping down a cable so that no great-aunt could trip on it, when I looked up at the seating plan.

I always read the seating plan. It tells you who's going to stand up and talk, and roughly how loud.

Top table. The groom's parents. The bride's parents. Best man: T. Mallory.

Then I went and stood in the car park, between a florist's van and a hedge, for thirty-five minutes.

Jo found me there, in a white dressing gown with half her hair curled and the other half in clips, carrying two glasses of fizz and a guilty expression.

"You saw the board," she said.

"I saw the board."

"Femi's brother broke his ankle on Thursday. It was all very last minute."

"So Femi asked Theo."

"So Femi asked Theo." She held out a glass. "Drink this."

"I'm working."

"You're standing in a hedge."

"I'm standing next to a hedge, which is very different, and I'm checking on this van for the florist. And you didn't tell me because?"

"Because you'd have sent somebody else from your studio, and I didn't want somebody else, I wanted you." She pushed the glass into my hand. "Also, I'm the bride. You're not allowed to be cross with the bride. It's in the contract."

"I wrote the contract."

"Then you know it's in there. And before you say anything, I've put him at the far end of the top table, as far from the cheese as it's physically possible to get. I thought about you, Marnie. I promise I did."

Jo has known all my faces since university, and she was looking at one of them now.

"It's fine," I said. "It was nine years ago. I barely remember what he looks like."

"He's got a beard now."

"Good for him. Beards are very brave."

"Marnie, you've been out here half an hour. The florist came looking for her van and went away again because she thought you were crying in it."

"I wasn't in it. I was near it. And I wasn't crying, I was thinking about levels."

"Levels."

"Microphone levels. He talks very quietly when he's nervous and then very loudly when he gets to the good bit. I'm planning ahead." I drank half the glass, which is not professional at two in the afternoon. "Honestly. I'll put a microphone in front of him, make him sound lovely, pack up and go home. Go and finish your hair."

"Are you coming back in?"

"In a minute. The van needs me."

She went. I stayed five more minutes, saying his name over in my head until it was just a word.

Point one

At four o'clock the band's singer came over to my table behind the cheese.

"Are you the sound lady? How does the ear thing work?"

"See this?" I tapped the little grey microphone on a bendy neck at the corner of my desk. "That's the talkback. When I press the button, my voice goes straight into your earpieces and nowhere else. When I don't press it, I'm talking to nobody. And this catch on the side locks it down, so I can talk to you while I've got both hands on the sliders."

"So if a voice in my ear tells me to stop, that's you?"

"That's me. Only ever me."

She went back to the stage. I locked the catch down.

"Keys," I said into the talkback, "you're a bit hot. Bring it down for me."

The keyboard player gave me a thumbs-up and turned it down, and I went back to my cheese, and I never unlocked the catch. I found that out later.

The father of the bride spoke first, too close to the microphone, so that every word popped.

"Back off a bit," I murmured into the talkback, out of habit, though he had no earpiece. "There you go. Lovely."

I talk every speaker through it like that. It keeps my hands steady, and nobody is supposed to hear.

Then the groom. Femi cried before he'd even started, and kept apologising for it.

"I'm sorry," he said, wiping his face with a napkin. "I'm so sorry, everyone. I just really, really like her. I've liked her since the first day, when she told me my shoes were wrong, and they were."

The whole room loved him, and I didn't say a word into anything.

Then the best man stood up.

He was tall, in a grey suit that looked borrowed, and he did have a beard now, a short, careful one, the beard of a man who had read an article about beards. He was holding index cards. Of course he was holding index cards.

I hadn't seen Theo Mallory for nine years, not once, not even across a street.

"Femi asked me to keep this short," he said, "so I've only got eleven points."

Everybody laughed. I didn't. I knew he wasn't joking.

"Point one," said Theo. "Love isn't luck. There's research."

And from somewhere near the cheese, at a volume that rattled the glasses on the top table, a voice I knew very well said, "Says the man who proposed with a bar chart."

It was mine.

A very good bar chart

A room going quiet makes a sound. I've recorded it. First the hum drops out from under everything, and then there's nothing at all, and in the nothing you can hear a chair leg scrape, and somebody's earring land on a plate.

A hundred and forty people turned round to look at the cheese.

I flicked the catch up, the little red light went out, and I thought, very calmly, that I would now have to move to another country.

Theo was looking straight down the barn at me. He used to look at me like that across rooms, as if there was nobody else in them, and I'd forgotten it. I hadn't. I'd tried to. He didn't look angry. He looked like a man checking a fact.

Then he leaned into the microphone, which I had set for him myself, at exactly the right height.

"It was a very good bar chart," he said.

The barn fell apart. Femi stood up and hugged him, and Jo laughed so hard her mother had to hold her veil.

"I'm going to skip to point eleven," said Theo, tapping his cards square, "since we've now established that I can't be trusted with charts."

A smaller laugh, and a kinder one.

"Point eleven. Femi, Jo. Turn up. That's all of it, really. Turn up on the good days and on the very boring ones, and keep turning up for each other, even on the days when you'd both rather be anywhere else." He lifted his glass. "To the bride and groom."

A hundred and forty people said it back. I pushed a slider so they'd sound warm, because that's my job, and my hand was shaking, and it sounded lovely.

Jo arrived at my table during the first dance, holding her skirt up with both fists.

"Marnie."

"Don't."

"You heckled the best man."

"I'm aware."

"At my wedding. Through the ceiling."

"I'm very aware of the ceiling, Jo. I'll be aware of the ceiling for the rest of my life. I'm going to refund you, and then I'm going to move to a small island where nobody has speakers."

"Refund me? Are you mad? It was the best bit of the whole day, and you know it. Everyone's asking who you are." She leaned over the cheese and lowered her voice. "Also, he hasn't stopped smiling since. I just thought you'd want to know that."

"I didn't want to know that."

"I know you didn't," said Jo. "He also asked me whether you were staying for the dancing. Theo. Who has never danced in his life, not once, not even at his own graduation."

"I'm staying for the dancing because I'm doing the sound for the dancing."

"That's what I told him," said Jo, and went back to her husband.

Nine years ago

October, and raining. I came home at seven in yesterday's clothes, and he was waiting on my doorstep under the porch light with a roll of paper on his knees.

"Is your head better?" he said.

"A bit."

"You look terrible."

"Thank you, Theo."

"Terrible in a nice way. Hold this end. No, the other end. I've done it on graph paper, so it's all to scale, and I've coloured it in and stayed inside the lines, which you know I find very hard when I'm excited."

There was a VISITOR sticker from a front desk on my jumper. I peeled it off and pocketed it while he unrolled the paper. He didn't notice.

The bars were in felt pen, and across the top, in his neat capitals, it said REASONS.

"Laughs at my frog facts," he read out. "That one's quite short, because you only laugh at about half of them. Parks better than me. That's medium. Makes every room sound better. Taller, obviously. And this one, I had to tape a second sheet on for this one, because it went off the top..."

"Theo."

I saw the tallest bar. I didn't let myself read it.

He knelt on the wet step and said my name the way he always said it, as if he'd only just learned it and liked it very much, and I said no.

Then I said something that made no sense to him at all. "It isn't about my dad."

He hadn't mentioned my dad.

Cake

At midnight, while the caterers stacked chairs, I was winding cables round my elbow.

"You left your catch down," said the band's singer, stopping by my table with her guitar case. "All night. And Rhodri sent us through the roof speakers before the toasts, ready for the dancing, so wherever we went, you went. I'm so sorry. I'd have said, only I thought you meant it."

"I didn't mean it," I said. "I mean, I meant it. I didn't mean to say it."

"Well," she said, "the best man didn't seem to mind." And she left me to my cables.

"Hello, Marnie."

"Theo."

His tie was in his pocket with the end hanging out, the way it always used to be.

"You've got a beard," I said, because somebody had to say something.

"I read an article."

"I knew it. I said that. I said to Jo, that's the beard of a man who's read an article."

"Jo told me you said beards were brave."

"Jo has a big mouth and it's her wedding, so she's allowed." He nodded at the desk.

"Is it off now?"

"It's off."

"Are you sure? Because I've got a few more points I could do, if you'd like to respond to them."

"I'm a professional, Theo. I checked it twice and then I unplugged it, and then I checked it again."

"I know," he said. "Everybody heard. Point two was about compromise, by the way. You'd have hated point two."

"I hate all eleven of them already, without hearing a single one."

I wanted to laugh, and I didn't want him to see me want to, so I bent down and unplugged something that was already unplugged.

"I'm sorry," I told the floor. "About the comment."

"Heckle."

"I was going to say comment."

"It was a heckle, and a good one. Your timing was excellent."

"My timing was a technical fault. There's a catch on the side of the button. I'll be writing to the manufacturers."

"Your timing has always been excellent," he said. "It's your volume I worry about."

He put something down beside my hand: a slice of wedding cake on a paper napkin, with half an icing flower still on top.

"You didn't get any. You never eat at these things. I asked for a corner, because the corners have the most icing, and the caterer said the sound lady never eats, and I said I know, that's why."

Nine years, and he still knew that.

"I'm not hungry."

"Okay."

That was it. Okay. The Theo I remembered would have argued. He'd have had a study about blood sugar.

"Do you still collect frog facts?" I said, before I could stop myself.

"I've got one. I've had it for about nine years, actually. Nobody else ever wanted it."

"Go on, then."

"There's a frog that freezes solid every winter. Its heart stops. Then the spring comes, and it thaws out, and its heart just starts again, as if it had only been waiting."

"That's not a funny one."

"No," said Theo. "I know it's not."

"It was good to hear your voice," he said. "Even at that volume."

He waited, just for a second, the way he used to wait on the doorstep for me to find my keys. I didn't say anything. I couldn't think of a single thing that was true and safe at the same time.

Then he went to find Femi, and I stood there holding a cable until the caterers wanted the table back.

I ate the cake in the van at one in the morning. Lemon. Then I copied the night's recording onto the studio's shared drive, because Priya makes us do it after every job. In case a couple ever sues us. I watched the blue bar fill up with the whole night inside it, and I didn't think about it once.

Sunday

On Sunday mornings I go to my dad's flat, the one with a balcony over a park. Nine years ago Dad had a stroke, and it took most of his words. He understands nearly everything, but the words get stuck on the way out, and he has to go and fetch them one at a time.

Every week I record him saying his practice words, and I build the best ones into a radio show. Nobody else knows it exists. I've never had a good reason not to tell them. I just haven't.

I played him last week's episode first, jingle and all.

"Good morning, Bristol, and welcome back to Sunday Best with Rob Cole, the only programme in the city that comes to you live from a balcony. This week Rob has been working very hard on the days of the week, and I'm told he's got a big one for us. Rob, over to you."

"Wednesday," said Dad's voice on the recording, clear as a bell, and a studio audience that was really a sound effect called CROWD APPLAUSE LARGE went wild.

Dad laughed until he had to wipe his eyes with the back of his good hand.

"Best," he said. It took him a moment. "Show."

"I know. You're a natural. They'll be giving you the breakfast slot."

He pointed at himself, then pulled a very serious face, like a man reading the news.

"Fine. You read the news, and I'll do the weather. It'll be raining. It's always raining. Shall we get on?"

This week's card said Kettle. Garden. Tuesday. Window. I clipped the microphone to his jumper and pressed record.

"This is Sunday Best, take one," I said, in the radio voice. "Our first word this morning is something every home in Bristol depends on. Rob, the floor is yours."

He looked up at the ceiling, which is where he goes to find them.

"K," he said. "K. Ket."

I waited. His therapist told me in the first month never to finish his words for him, and it took me a year to stop.

"Kettle," said Dad.

"Kettle! Ladies and gentlemen, the kettle! A word that has been giving Rob trouble since the spring, and he has just knocked it clean out of the park!" I did the applause with my mouth, badly, and he shook his head at me, delighted.

My phone buzzed. Then it rang, which on silent means the same person has called three times in a row. Only one person does that. I went out onto the balcony, above a park full of dogs.

"Two million, one hundred and six thousand," said Priya.

"Good morning to you too. Two million what?"

"Views. Since about eleven o'clock last night. Two million, one hundred and," a tap on a keyboard, "eleven thousand. Where are you? Why can I hear dogs?"

"I'm at the gym."

"You have never been to a gym in your life. Marnie. Have you seriously not seen it?"

"Seen what?"

"Oh, this is wonderful. I'm sending it to you now. Watch it while I'm still on the phone, I want to hear your face."

"You can't hear a face."

"I can hear yours."

"It's forty seconds. Somebody's put the sound over a photo of the barn, and across the bottom it says, 'Sound lady heckles best man. Wait for his reply.' Are you watching? You're not watching. I can tell."

I was watching. The sound was perfect. I remember thinking that, professionally, before the rest of me caught up.

"Now read the comments," said Priya. "No, I'll read them, you'll only skip the good ones. 'Wait, she's the one he proposed to?' 'He said it was a very good bar chart, I am on the floor.' 'Justice for bar chart man.' 'Somebody please find out what the tallest bar said.' And eleven thousand people saying he's adorable and you're a monster."

"Thank you, Priya. That's very helpful. Who filmed this?"

"Some guest. Everybody films everything at weddings. Does it matter? Look at the numbers, Marnie."

"I'm not looking at the numbers. I'm looking at my life."

"Your life has two million views, Marnie. Do you know what the studio could do with two million people who want to know what happens next? Come in tomorrow at nine. I've had an idea."

"I hate your ideas."

"You'll hate this one even more," she said, sounding thrilled, and rang off.

Through the glass, Dad was watching me with his head on one side. He can't ask much any more, so he looks instead. I went back in and straightened the microphone on his jumper.

"Where were we?" I said. "Garden."

By Sunday lunchtime, two million strangers had heard me turn Theo Mallory down. Every one of them thought it was the first time.
